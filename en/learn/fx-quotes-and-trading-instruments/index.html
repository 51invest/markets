FX Quotes and Trading Instruments

FX is quoted in currency pairs; spot, forward, swap, futures and option instruments carry different rights, cash flows, margin and counterparty risks.

What the product is

A foreign exchange trade converts one currency into another, and rates are quoted in currency pairs: in the illustrative quote EUR/USD = 1.1000, one euro buys 1.1000 U.S. dollars, so the first currency is the base currency and the second the quote currency. “Base currency” here is the quoting convention for a pair, not the monetary base that FIN-005 describes on a central bank's balance sheet, and the rate is illustrative rather than a live quote: it is an integer chosen for mental arithmetic, whereas the registered EUR_USD monthly series on this site records 1.1513 for September 2026 (published on this site's CHART-007 page); the two are different things. Spot, forward, FX swap, currency swap, futures and options are all FX instruments, but their legal structures differ. The BIS April 2025 survey put average OTC FX turnover at about $9.6 trillion per day; spot was about 31%, outright forwards about 19%, FX swaps about 42%, currency swaps about 2% and options about 7% (shares are rounded and sum to slightly over 100%) [2].

Rights, obligations and cash flows

Instrument What the buyer or holder obtains Cash flow and margin Main counterparty
Spot Currency exchanged at the traded rate Full payment; typically T+2 Bank, market maker
Forward Future delivery at an agreed rate Full delivery; collateral may apply OTC counterparty
FX swap A near leg and an opposite far leg Principal exchanged twice; funding and roll Bank, corporate
FX futures Standardized contract under exchange rules Initial margin, daily mark-to-market, margin calls Exchange and clearinghouse
FX option Right to buy or sell at a strike Buyer pays premium; writer owes performance Exchange or OTC counterparty

Sell an FX option Receive premium and take the obligation Premium received; exchange at the strike if assigned Quote currency Uncovered loss can far exceed the premium
Exchange-traded FX futures Post margin and mark to market daily Notional × margin rate; profit or loss = rate change × multiplier Contract settlement currency Margin calls and liquidation; the rules differ from OTC forwards

One ICE US Dollar Index futures contract is $1,000 times the index[3]. A 0.010 index-point move equals $10 and the minimum tick of 0.005 equals $5. Contract months are March, June, September and December, and trading ceases at 10:16 Eastern time two days before settlement. The index is not a single currency pair.

Quotes, spreads and participants

For most pairs one pip is 0.0001; for pairs quoted to two decimals, such as yen quotes, it is 0.01. The bid-offer difference is the spread; retail quotes may add commissions or mark-ups. Participants include banks, market makers, corporates, funds, brokers, exchanges and clearinghouses. Interdealer trading is mainly OTC; futures and options trade on exchanges.

The two ways of quoting the same pair are reciprocals: the illustrative quote EUR/USD = 1.1000 says one euro buys 1.1000 US dollars; read as “how many euros per US dollar” it is 1 ÷ 1.1000 ≈ 0.9091 euros. Which of the two counts as the direct quote and which as the indirect one depends on which currency you treat as home: the same number is a direct quote for a dollar-based reader and an indirect quote for a euro-based reader (this framing is the article's own convention). Taking the reciprocal changes only how the price is presented; it changes neither the spread, the costs or the counterparty structure, and it cannot tell you which side is cheaper.

Trading, holding and exit

Spot settles in full on the settlement date. A forward can be delivered, closed early or rolled. A swap funds and rolls a position through its near and far legs. Futures are usually closed or rolled, or delivered under exchange rules. Options can be closed, exercised or left to expire. Retail FX often uses rolling settlement and overnight financing; terms vary by broker and jurisdiction. The T+2 spot settlement, the roll and the overnight interest are this article's description of market convention: none of the three registered sources states them (the CFTC page covers risk and margin, the BIS page turnover and instrument shares, the ICE page only that contract's own last trading day and settlement), so settlement dates and financing terms follow the account agreement and the exchange rules.

Costs and risks

Costs include spreads, commissions, overnight interest, margin funding and taxes. Risks include adverse currency moves, leverage, liquidity, settlement timing, counterparty default, regulation and cross-border capital restrictions. The CFTC warns that margin trading can make an investor responsible for losses exceeding the amount deposited, and that the forex market is volatile and risky [1]. Futures are marked to market daily and may trigger margin calls; OTC forwards and swaps depend on the counterparty and collateral.

Terms and documents

OTC transactions use a master agreement and a confirmation that set out the settlement date, exchange rate, notional principal, collateral and termination terms; futures and options follow exchange contract specifications, margin and clearing rules. For retail accounts, check registration and customer-fund segregation.

Comparison and boundaries

Instrument Legal claim Cash flow Main risks
Spot Immediate exchange Full payment Settlement, liquidity
Forward Contractual exchange at maturity Delivery at maturity Counterparty, credit
Futures Standardized rights and obligations Margin, mark-to-market Leverage, margin calls, basis
Option Buyer's right, writer's obligation Premium, exercise Buyer loses at most the premium; an uncovered writer can lose far more than the premium received

FX futures are standardized and centrally cleared; retail FX is mostly OTC and depends on the counterparty. The dollar index is not a single exchange rate.

Knowledge-point review

spot FX

Definition: Spot FX exchanges currencies for settlement within about two business days at the spot rate.

Mechanism: Spot rates reflect rate differentials, trade, capital flows and expectations; quotes have bid, ask and mid rates.

Example: For example, a firm converts dollars to yuan today at the spot rate for settlement in two business days.

Boundary: Spot is not instant cash; settlement timing, settlement risk and liquidity affect cost, and spreads widen under stress.

cross rates

Definition: A cross rate prices two non-dollar currencies directly, usually derived from their dollar rates.

Mechanism: Cross rates should satisfy triangular arbitrage; spreads, liquidity and convention determine whether a direct cross is usable.

Example: For example, euro-yen can be computed from euro-dollar and dollar-yen, and deviations trigger triangular arbitrage.

Boundary: A cross rate is not independent; dollar liquidity and currency spreads affect the synthetic rate, and a direct cross may be costlier or thinner.

FX forwards

Definition: An FX forward fixes an exchange rate for a future date to hedge currency risk.

Mechanism: Forward rates reflect spot and rate differentials near interest-rate parity; deliverable and non-deliverable, tenor and counterparty credit differ.

Example: For example, an exporter locks the yuan rate on dollar receipts six months ahead to avoid depreciation losses.

Boundary: A forward is not free insurance; it forgoes favourable moves, forward points reflect differentials but carry basis and credit risk, and counterparty default matters.

non-deliverable forwards

Definition: An NDF is a non-deliverable FX forward settled in cash for the difference between the contracted and fixing rates.

Mechanism: NDFs are common for restricted or non-convertible currencies, with quotes reflecting offshore expectations, rate differentials and control risk.

Example: For example, a firm hedges yuan with a dollar-settled NDF and pays only the difference at maturity without delivering yuan.

Boundary: NDFs are not spot substitutes; fixing, settlement currency, controls and counterparty risk create basis, and liquidity may concentrate in a few tenors.

FX swaps

Definition: An FX swap buys one currency spot and sells it forward, or the reverse, economically short-term FX funding.

Mechanism: Swap points reflect rate differentials and tenor and are used to roll dollar liquidity, manage cash and arbitrage; both legs execute together.

Example: For example, a bank buys dollars spot and sells them one month forward, lending yuan for temporary dollars.

Boundary: A swap is not an outright forward; it fixes both legs, basis, collateral and rollover risk affect cost, and it cannot alone hedge a long-term exposure.

cross-currency swaps

Definition: A cross-currency swap exchanges principal and interest streams in different currencies over a longer term, used for balance-sheet and cross-border funding hedges.

Mechanism: It includes principal exchange and two interest legs, with basis reflecting funding supply and demand; counterparty and collateral risk matter.

Example: For example, a firm issues dollar debt, swaps into yuan and fixes interest, then swaps back at maturity.

Boundary: A cross-currency swap is not a simple forward; principal, interest and basis are complex, and early termination, credit and regulatory capital affect cost.

FX futures and options

Definition: FX futures are exchange-traded standardised future currency contracts, and FX options are rights to buy or sell currency.

Mechanism: Futures carry margin, daily marking and delivery, while options carry premium, volatility and time decay; exchange clearing reduces counterparty risk.

Example: For example, an exporter buys dollar puts to protect against depreciation and loses only the premium if the dollar rises.

Boundary: Futures and options are not universal hedges; basis, margin calls, liquidity and option mispricing cause losses, and standard contracts may not match cash flows.

onshore, offshore and delivery

Definition: The onshore market operates under local regulation and settlement, while the offshore market trades the same currency abroad; delivery exchanges currency or settles cash.

Mechanism: Onshore and offshore rates can diverge due to controls, liquidity and participants; deliverable and non-deliverable contracts differ.

Example: For example, CNH and CNY can gap during controls or risk events, forcing firms to choose a deliverable channel.

Boundary: Onshore and offshore are not one market; arbitrage is constrained by rules and settlement, and delivery location, law and time zones create risk that can keep gaps open.

Data and institutional check: FX quotes

Before using "FX quotes" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns spot and forward.

Third, the institutional boundary. FX futures is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: FX quotes

Learning "FX quotes" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "spot and forward": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "FX futures" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of FX quotes still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind spot and forward reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "FX quotes" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "spot and forward" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Notional value. If you buy EUR 100,000 at EUR/USD = 1.1000, what is the dollar notional?
  2. Pip value. What is a one-pip (0.0001) move worth? What about 50 pips?
  3. Leverage. At a 5% margin requirement, how much margin is needed, and what is the loss and margin percentage if the rate falls 100 pips?
Suggested answers
  1. 100,000 × 1.1000 = $110,000. Hypothetical, excluding fees.
  2. 100,000 × 0.0001 = $10; 50 pips = $500.
  3. Margin is 110,000 × 5% = $5,500; the loss is $1,000, or about 18.2% of margin. Hypothetical, excluding spread, commission and overnight interest.

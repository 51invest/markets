Critical Minerals: Lithium, Cobalt and Supply-Chain Bottlenecks

Lithium and cobalt are not ordinary commodities; they are the starting point of the battery chain. Prices depend on mines, refining, cathode materials and policy, and the bottleneck is often refining rather than reserves.

The question and its scope

Critical minerals are not simply "scarce minerals". They are minerals that are hard to substitute and whose supply can be disrupted, in energy transition, defence or industry. Lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite and rare earths belong to this group. Their prices are not set by reserves alone; they are set by capacity, inventories and policy across the chain from mine to refining, cathode, cell and vehicle.

This article uses lithium and cobalt to examine four interfaces: where demand comes from, where supply bottlenecks sit, why prices swing, and how investment should handle supply-chain risk. The numbers are teaching examples, not official statistics for any year.

Demand: batteries are the main increment

Lithium demand comes mainly from power batteries and storage. The lithium content per kWh varies by cathode: lithium iron phosphate (LFP) uses no cobalt, while nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC) uses nickel and cobalt. Converting "EV sales" into "lithium demand" requires three parameters: pack size per vehicle, lithium per kWh and battery chemistry.

Cobalt demand is more concentrated: about 70% comes from batteries, with the rest from superalloys, hard metals and chemicals. Cobalt content in NMC falls as nickel rises, but high-nickel cathodes bring safety and process challenges, so substitution is not linear.

The most common demand error is to confuse penetration with volume. Penetration is the share of new vehicles; volume is the total market. If the total market falls, rising penetration may not produce absolute growth. Another error is to treat battery capacity as mineral demand: battery plants can hold inventories and switch chemistry, so mineral demand responds more slowly to price.

Supply: three gates from mine to material

The first gate is the mine. Lithium comes from brine, hard rock (spodumene) and clay. Brine has low cost but long build times and climate exposure; hard rock has high capex but faster expansion. Cobalt is mostly a by-product of copper-nickel mines, so its output is driven by copper-nickel economics, not the cobalt price alone. Cobalt supply therefore responds slowly to cobalt prices: a high cobalt price does not automatically make a copper mine expand.

The second gate is refining. Ore becomes lithium carbonate, lithium hydroxide or cobalt sulphate through chemical conversion and energy. Refining capacity can be built faster than a mine, but environmental permits, reagent supply and energy costs can slow it. Many countries have ore but not enough refining; the bottleneck moves from underground to above ground.

The third gate is cathode and cell. Cathode chemistry determines energy density, safety and cost; cell makers demand high purity, consistency and certification. A new mine may produce ore and still wait years for material qualification. Certification cycles, quality stability and customer relationships are invisible capacity.

Concentration changes across these gates. Mining may be concentrated in one set of countries, refining in another, and materials in a third. Any concentrated stage can become a price and delivery chokepoint; looking only at reserves misses the real constraint.

Prices: why lithium swings

Lithium prices rose sharply in 2021–2022 and fell hard in 2023–2024. The cause was not one event but the combination of an inventory cycle, expansion lags and expectations. When prices rise, battery makers and traders build inventory, pushing spot prices higher; mines and refiners announce expansions; years later, capacity arrives, demand growth slows, prices fall and inventories reverse.

Cobalt prices are also shaped by by-product supply and policy. The Democratic Republic of the Congo accounts for most cobalt mine output, and export policy, artisanal mining governance and transport corridors can change supply. Cobalt contracts often use reference prices, discounts and quality adjustments, so the spot price is not what a mine actually receives.

Price signals matter for investment, but they are not short-term forecasts. Mine capex takes three to five years from decision to production, and refining two to three. A company that expands at the top may commission at the bottom. The capital cycle is therefore the core risk in critical-minerals investment.

Inventories, curves and price structure

Spot markets for critical minerals are usually deeper than futures markets, and price discovery often happens first in spot and offtake contracts. There are three inventories: mine, refinery and downstream battery plant. When downstream inventory is high, a mine cut may not raise prices; when it is low, a 5% supply shortfall can move prices sharply.

The term structure reflects inventories and financing costs. Backwardation usually signals near-term tightness; contango signals ample inventory or high carrying cost. But lithium and cobalt futures are less liquid than oil, so the curve cannot be read the same way. Offtake, spot and futures prices coexist, each with discounts and quality adjustments.

Inventories are also a policy buffer. Strategic reserves can buy when prices are low and sell when high, dampening swings; but opaque reserve sizes and release rules can amplify speculation. Corporate safety stocks depend on supply concentration, shipping time and substitution difficulty: the more concentrated the supply, the more days of cover a buyer holds.

Supply bottlenecks: not just reserves

There are at least five bottlenecks. First, geology and grade: high-grade ore is getting scarcer and extraction costs are rising. Second, permits and environment: new mines face slow approvals, community and water constraints. Third, refining and energy: refining needs stable power, reagents and environmental facilities. Fourth, logistics and trade: ports, rail, shipping and export controls interrupt delivery. Fifth, technology and certification: battery materials require long qualification, so new suppliers cannot switch overnight.

Export controls and resource nationalism amplify bottlenecks. Resource countries can use export taxes, quotas, nationalisation or local-processing requirements to keep more value at home; downstream countries respond with subsidies, strategic stocks and friend-shoring. These policies turn a "global market" into several regional markets, with the same grade of ore carrying different premiums in different places.

Recycling is a long-run source, not a short-run cure. The volume of retired batteries depends on sales years earlier, and collection networks, dismantling and recovery rates take time. Recycling reduces long-run dependence but cannot supply next year's battery plant.

A numerical example: lithium and cobalt per vehicle

Suppose an EV has a 60 kWh pack, uses NMC chemistry, and requires 0.65 kg of lithium carbonate equivalent (LCE) and 0.15 kg of cobalt per kWh. Lithium demand per vehicle is 60×0.65=3960\times0.65=3960×0.65=39 kg LCE, and cobalt demand is 60×0.15=960\times0.15=960×0.15=9 kg.

At a lithium price of $15,000 per tonne, lithium cost per vehicle is 39/1,000×15,000=58539/1{,}000\times15{,}000=58539/1,000×15,000=585 dollars. At a cobalt price of $30,000 per tonne, cobalt cost is 9/1,000×30,000=2709/1{,}000\times30{,}000=2709/1,000×30,000=270 dollars. The two together are 855 dollars, or 2.85% of a $30,000 vehicle. This excludes nickel, graphite, separator, electrolyte and processing; mineral prices are only part of the battery cost.

For one million EVs, the same parameters require 39,000 tonnes of LCE and 9,000 tonnes of cobalt. If annual lithium supply is 35,000 tonnes, the shortfall is 4,000 tonnes, about 10% of demand. With low inventories, a 10% shortfall can produce a price increase much larger than 10%; with high inventories, the same shortfall can be absorbed. Inventory is the shock absorber, so do not read the annual balance alone.

Investment: offtake, equity and the capital cycle

There are several routes into critical minerals. First, mining equities: direct exposure to price, cost and expansion execution, with high volatility. Second, offtake agreements: downstream buyers prepay or lend in exchange for future output; this reduces mine financing pressure and locks in supply. Third, royalties and streams: investors prepay for future revenue or metal, with more stable cash flow but complex structures. Fourth, ETFs and thematic funds: diversify single-mine risk but remain exposed to the commodity cycle.

Offtake is not risk-free. If the mine is late, the buyer may not get the material; if prices fall, a fixed offtake price can leave the buyer paying above market. Royalties and streams depend on mine life, the cost curve and counterparty credit. Critical-minerals investment is not "how big is the reserve"; it is cost-curve position, expansion execution, permitting and offtake structure.

The capital cycle usually runs: high price → high profit → expansion → more supply → low price → less investment → tighter supply. Investors most often buy the slowest-expanding asset when the story is hottest. Conversely, when prices are low, companies on the left of the cost curve with strong balance sheets and no forced expansion are more likely to survive.

Boundaries and counterexamples

First, critical does not mean permanently short. High prices stimulate substitution, recycling and efficiency; the spread of LFP has already reduced cobalt demand. Second, reserves are not capacity. A mine needs permits, capital, equipment and people; the timeline is years. Third, concentration is not a complete cutoff. Trade re-routes, but re-routing takes cost and time. Fourth, a price collapse is not proof of disappearing demand. It may be the inventory and expansion cycle. Fifth, ESG risk is not soft. Water, communities, artisanal mining and biodiversity affect permits and output.

Common misreadings

Misreading one: falling lithium prices mean EV demand has collapsed. The fall may come from supply release and destocking while demand is still growing.

Misreading two: owning a mine gives bargaining power. Refining, materials and cells matter too; a country with ore but no refining still exports or imports processing.

Misreading three: cobalt will be completely substituted. High-nickel, low-cobalt is the direction, but safety, cost and process limits make substitution gradual.

Misreading four: recycling solves the short-term gap. Recycling volume depends on past sales and collection networks; it cannot fill new demand immediately.

Misreading five: critical-minerals investment is just a price bet. Cost curve, expansion execution, permits and offtake determine returns; price is only one variable.

  2. If lithium falls from $15,000 to $10,000 per tonne, how much does lithium cost per vehicle change?

  4. Why does cobalt supply respond more slowly to price than lithium? Give two reasons.
  5. Draw the mine–refining–cathode–cell chain, mark the most likely chokepoint and explain why.

  8. How do export controls change profit allocation across mine, refining and cell?

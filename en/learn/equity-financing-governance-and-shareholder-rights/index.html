Equity Financing, Governance and Shareholder Rights

Equity financing trades ownership for capital; dilution, voting, preferences and governance terms determine what existing holders give up.

Definition

Equity financing is a company issuing new shares for capital in the primary market; trading among investors in the secondary market does not change the share count. Dilution is the fall in existing holders' ownership and earnings per share after new issuance. Common shares usually carry votes and residual claims; preferred shares rank ahead on dividends and liquidation but often have limited votes; companies may also create classes with different voting rights, as set out in the charter and offering documents [3].

Intuition

Think of a company as a building whose cash flows and votes are owned by shareholders in proportion to their stakes. Common shares are a residual, uncapped claim: dividends rank behind other claims, but there is no ceiling on upside; preferred shares are a senior IOU with a fixed dividend, paid first, usually without votes, and may be callable. Equity financing is not free money: issuing new shares dilutes existing holders, and new shareholders change the board and governance. Governance rules determine who can sell assets, borrow, pay dividends and enter related-party deals; those rights matter as much as the cash flows.

Mechanism

Financing routes include follow-on offerings, rights issues, convertible bonds and IPOs. A placement to outside investors lowers existing holders' ownership; a rights issue gives existing holders a pre-emptive subscription, reducing dilution but requiring cash. Votes determine board elections and major matters; the board then sets budgets, M&A and executive pay. Preferred shares, veto rights, anti-dilution clauses and dual-class structures separate cash-flow rights from control. Minority protection includes independent directors, related-party approval, disclosure and class actions; effectiveness depends on enforcement, not text alone.

Worked example

Suppose a company has 100 shares and net income of CNY 100, so EPS is CNY 1. It issues 20 shares for CNY 1,600; net income rises to CNY 130, the new share count is 120, and EPS is CNY 1.083. Existing holders fall from 100% to 83.3%. If the issue price is only CNY 50 and raises CNY 1,000, net income rises to CNY 120 and EPS is CNY 1: existing holders give up 16.7% ownership without per-share accretion. A pro-rata rights issue lets them keep 83.3% by contributing cash. These are teaching assumptions and exclude tax and issuance costs.

Second worked example: rights issue dilution and ex-rights price

Suppose a company has 100 shares at CNY 100, a market value of CNY 10,000. It makes a 1-for-5 rights issue: each five shares can buy one new share at CNY 80, issuing 20 shares and raising CNY 1,600. The theoretical ex-rights price is (100 × 100 + 20 × 80) / 120 = CNY 96.67. A holder who subscribes in full ends with six shares costing 100 × 5 + 80 = CNY 580, worth 6 × 96.67 = CNY 580 — no gain or loss. A holder who does not subscribe keeps five shares worth CNY 483.33, CNY 16.67 less than the original CNY 500; that is the dilution loss. The lower the subscription price and the larger the issue, the greater the dilution for non-subscribers. These are teaching assumptions and exclude fees, tax and undersubscription.

Boundary and mistakes

First, a large raise does not guarantee per-share accretion; issue price, project return and share count all matter. Second, preferred 'priority' applies to dividends and liquidation, not a guaranteed fixed return, and may not participate in residual profit. Third, dual-class structures can stabilise control and speed decisions but weaken one-share-one-vote discipline. Fourth, the charter and jurisdiction determine shareholder rights, so cross-border analogies fail. Fifth, buybacks and dividends can raise EPS without creating operating value; do not confuse them with growth.

Failure modes

The first failure is conflating control with economic ownership. Under dual-class structures, minority holders may have economic exposure while votes sit with founders; a 51% stake does not guarantee control over major decisions, because charters, boards and creditor covenants constrain it. The second is ignoring related-party deals and cash extraction: a controlling holder can move value through related purchases, loans and guarantees that minorities cannot block. The third is treating buybacks as permanently bullish: buybacks lift earnings per share but may simply replace equity with cheap debt, raising leverage and risk. The fourth is looking only at dilution, not at the use and return of the proceeds; a low-priced issue funding a high-return project can create value, while a high-priced issue funding a low-return project can destroy it.

Three checks

First, what is the financing form: common equity, preferred, rights issue, convertible or debt, and what are the dilution and claim priorities? Second, can the use of proceeds and expected return be verified, with a clear payback period? Third, does governance protect minorities: are voting rights, related-party approval, independent directors and exit mechanisms effective? If these three are unclear, 'equity financing' is just a balance-sheet line, not a judgeable act of value creation.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

Common shares are easily confused with preferred shares, convertibles and debt. Common equity is a residual claim with variable dividends and votes usually per share; preferred shares have priority dividends and liquidation order but often no votes and may be callable; convertibles are debt with an equity option, and conversion dilutes; debt has fixed interest and maturity and ranks ahead of equity in default. Cash flow, risk and governance rights differ. A counterexample is 'a 51% stake gives absolute control': charters, board seats, class votes and creditor covenants can limit control; conversely, less than 50% can control through a dual-class structure. Another is 'buybacks always create shareholder value': if funded with expensive debt, higher leverage and interest can offset the EPS lift. Compare financing options on dilution, control, cash flow and covenants together.

Scope and limits

This financing and control framework applies to companies with clear charters, disclosed share classes, board seats and creditor covenants. If the charter allows board-issued shares, class voting is opaque, or a large holder controls through a concert-party agreement, judging control by ownership percentage alone fails. It does not apply to equating votes with economic return, nor to static analysis that ignores convertible dilution. Read the charter and shareholder agreements, then build dilution, control and cash-flow tables; 51% is not sufficient for control.

The shareholder-rights lifecycle: classes, ADRs, issuance, buybacks, M&A and delisting

Shareholder rights are not a static list; from share classes at IPO, through depositary receipts, follow-on capital, cash distributions, control transactions and delisting, each stage changes cash flow, votes and exit. The six sections below follow that lifecycle.

Dual-class voting: super-voting shares, conversion and sunset

Definition. Dual-class voting gives different share classes different votes, commonly one vote per public share and multiple votes per insider share. The charter and offering documents create the rights; they are not a founder's natural entitlement [3][4].

Mechanism. Insiders obtain voting control with a smaller economic stake, which lets them elect directors and decide major matters; super-voting shares often convert to a lower-vote class when transferred, and some structures include a sunset. The gap between votes and economic ownership is a wedge that can amplify related-party risk, block transactions and entrench management [4].

Example. Teaching assumptions: 20 insider Class B shares with 10 votes each and 80 public Class A shares with one vote each produce 280 votes; insiders hold 20% of the equity but 71.4% of the votes. If each Class B share converts to one Class A share on transfer, the 20 shares become 20 votes out of 100, leaving insiders with 20% of the votes. The numbers show separation, not any company.

Boundary. Dual-class is not permitted in every market, and listing and index policies differ; it is not automatically bad governance, nor is control permanent, because charter amendments, class votes, boards and creditor covenants can change it. Treating economic ownership as votes, or votes as cash flow, misreads both [4].

Depositary receipts: ADR/ADS ratios, custody and rights

Definition. An ADR is a U.S.-traded receipt representing shares of a non-U.S. company; it evidences ownership of ADSs, which in turn represent the foreign shares held by a depositary bank. ADRs trade and settle in U.S. dollars, and the ratio may be one-for-one, a fraction or a multiple [5].

Mechanism. The foreign company or a holder deposits shares with the depositary, which issues ADSs; cancellation returns the shares. A sponsored facility is set up with the company under a deposit agreement and usually forwards communications; an unsponsored facility is set up by a broker-dealer without the company, with weaker rights and cost arrangements. In the depositary structure, an ADR holder is generally not the foreign company's direct registered shareholder; voting and dividends run through the depositary and the deposit agreement [5].

Example. Teaching assumptions: one ADS represents 8 ordinary shares, so 800 deposited shares support 100 ADSs; if one ordinary share is worth USD 2.50, one ADS is worth USD 20 before fees and FX. A 10% rise to USD 2.75 makes one ADS worth USD 22; a 5% fall in the local currency against the dollar offsets part of that gain. Fees, FX and taxes change the actual return.

Boundary. An ADR is not direct ownership of the foreign share and not a fund; a Level 1 program trades over the counter and cannot raise capital, Level 2 can list but cannot raise capital, and Level 3 can raise capital, subject to the offering documents [5]. An unsponsored ADR may not forward voting materials; the ratio can be changed; cross-border disclosure, withholding tax, FX and liquidity risk remain. Treating the ADR price as a pure proxy for the foreign share ignores the ratio and fees.

Issuance and follow-ons: public offers, rights issues, private placements and convertibles

Definition. A follow-on is a seasoned company issuing new shares in the primary market; a rights issue gives existing holders a pro-rata subscription right; a private placement sells to selected investors; a convertible adds shares only when converted. Secondary trading does not change the share count [3][6].

Mechanism. New shares raise the share count, so existing ownership falls to old shares / new total shares; EPS becomes net income / new total shares. A rights issue lets holders subscribe pro rata and preserve their percentage if they pay in; non-subscribers are diluted. A private placement may bring lock-ups, votes or board seats; a convertible creates delayed dilution [6].

Example. Teaching assumptions: 100 shares and net income of 100 yuan give EPS 1.00; issuing 20 shares and raising net income to 130 yuan gives EPS 1.083 and leaves existing holders with 83.3%. If the issue price is only 50 yuan and net income rises only to 120 yuan, EPS stays at 1.00 while existing holders give up 16.7% without per-share accretion. A 1-for-5 rights issue at 80 yuan has a theoretical ex-rights price of 96.67 yuan; full subscription breaks even, while a holder of 100 old shares who does not subscribe loses 333.33 yuan (3.33 per old share). These are teaching assumptions before fees and tax.

Boundary. A follow-on is not necessarily harmful dilution: a low-priced issue funding a high-return project can accrete, while a high-priced issue funding a low-return project can destroy value. A secondary sale by an existing holder does not dilute the company; only new issuance raises the share count. The Chinese term zengfa (additional issuance) has different meanings across markets, so a Chinese private placement or rights issue cannot be mapped to a U.S. follow-on or rights offering by name alone [6].

Dividends and buybacks: cash distribution, cancellation and leverage

Definition. A dividend pays cash or shares per share; a buyback uses company cash to repurchase shares. Both distribute cash, but a dividend pays all holders directly, while a buyback reduces the share count and pays the selling holders [6][7].

Mechanism. Dividends have declaration, record, ex-date and payment dates; the price typically drops by the dividend on the ex-date. A buyback can run in the open market, potentially under the Rule 10b-18 manner, timing, price and volume safe harbor, or through a tender offer. EPS rises mechanically after a buyback, but cash falls, and debt funding adds interest and leverage [7][8].

Example. Teaching assumptions: net income of 100 yuan and 100 shares give EPS 1.00; buying back 20 shares for 1,000 yuan leaves 80 shares and EPS 1.25 if net income stays at 100. If the cash could have earned 5%, the forgone 50 yuan cuts net income to 95 and EPS to 1.1875; the EPS lift falls from 25% to 18.75%. If the buyback price is above intrinsic value, the remaining holders bear a transfer of value.

Boundary. A buyback announcement is not completion and may be stopped by price, liquidity or tender conditions; a buyback does not create operating cash flow, so an EPS lift is not growth. Tax, legal and covenant limits vary by jurisdiction, and companies may use buybacks to offset equity-compensation dilution. Treating “buyback equals bullish” or “dividend equals safe” as a rule ignores price and leverage [8].

Mergers and spin-offs: cash, stock, control and separation

Definition. A merger combines two companies or gives one control of the other; consideration can be cash, stock or a mix. A spin-off distributes subsidiary shares pro rata to parent shareholders, creating a separate listed entity; a sell-off sells the subsidiary to a third party; a carve-out sells part of the shares while the parent may keep control [6][9][11].

Mechanism. Cash consideration does not add buyer shares but uses cash and borrowing capacity; stock consideration issues new shares and dilutes existing holders according to the exchange ratio. Control transactions may use a tender offer or a statutory merger; in the United States, a statutory merger is generally not itself a tender offer, while cash and stock tender offers face different disclosure and procedural rules [9]. A spin-off does not change the shareholders' proportional ownership of the assets, but changes governance, capital structure and valuation; a carve-out brings new capital and new minority holders [6].

Example. Teaching assumptions: the buyer has 100 shares and net income of 100 yuan; the target has 50 shares and net income of 20 yuan; synergies add 10 yuan. At an exchange ratio of 0.5, the buyer issues 25 shares, ending with 125 shares and 130 yuan of net income, or EPS 1.04 versus 1.00. At 0.8, it issues 40 shares, ending with 140 shares and EPS 0.929. If synergies fail, EPS is lower. The numbers show accretion or dilution before fees and tax.

Boundary. A merger does not guarantee synergies; stock consideration looks cheaper when the buyer's shares are high but dilutes, while cash consideration is more expensive when rates are high. A spin-off does not create value by itself, and parent shareholders still bear the subsidiary's risks; a carve-out may keep control or lose it. Regulatory approval, shareholder votes, appraisal rights and tax vary by jurisdiction [9].

Delisting and shareholder rights: exchange process, OTC trading and liquidation

Definition. Delisting is an exchange ending the listing of a security, either voluntarily or because the company fails continued-listing standards. It is not liquidation, and it does not automatically extinguish shareholder rights [10].

Mechanism. Using Nasdaq rules as an example: the exchange notifies the company of a deficiency; some deficiencies allow a compliance period or a compliance plan; if the issue remains unresolved, staff issues a Staff Delisting Determination; the company may request a hearing and appeal to a Hearings Panel, and a timely request usually stays suspension; if the decision is upheld, delisting becomes effective. After delisting, the shares may be quoted over the counter or may have no active market [10].

Example. Rule illustration: a stock closes below USD 1 for 30 consecutive business days, triggering the bid-price deficiency; the company receives a 180-day compliance period, fails to regain compliance, and the exchange issues a delisting determination; a hearing request stays the suspension; after the panel upholds the decision, the stock moves to OTC quotation. Exact periods, fees and appeal levels follow the exchange rules; the illustration is not every case.

Boundary. After delisting, holders may still have voting, dividend and liquidation rights, but those rights depend on the charter, state law and insolvency proceedings; an OTC quote is not a guarantee of a ready buyer, and liquidity and disclosure are weaker. Calling delisting “bankruptcy” or “zero” is wrong; treating OTC trading as the old exchange's liquidity is also wrong. Cross-border delisting rules differ and cannot be pasted together [10].

What the product is: rights and cash-flow structure

A share is a claim on the company’s residual cash flow: shareholders receive what remains after debt is paid and participate in governance through voting. Equity financing includes IPOs, follow-on offerings, rights issues and convertible instruments; it trades future cash flow and some control for cash today.

Common shares usually carry voting, dividend and residual-claim rights; preferred shares rank ahead on dividends and liquidation but may have limited votes. Shareholders also have information, proposal, dissent and related-party review rights, subject to the charter and securities law.

Participants, trading, holding and exit

Participants include the board and management, existing shareholders, underwriters, exchanges, regulators, institutional investors and retail investors. Underwriters price and allocate, exchanges provide trading, and regulators review disclosure and governance.

Shares move through issuance, secondary trading, follow-on offerings, rights issues, buybacks and delisting. Holding requires attention to votes, dividends, dilution and lock-ups; exit can be a sale, transfer or tender, and control transactions involve offer and board processes.

Costs, risks and terms and documents

Costs: Underwriting fees, legal and audit fees, issue discount, brokerage, stamp duty, custody and governance costs. Beyond explicit fees, dilution of existing holders is also a cost.

Risks: Dilution, governance, control, information asymmetry, lock-up and liquidity, litigation and regulatory risk. Shareholder rights are not absolute; the charter, ownership level and jurisdiction limit real influence.

Terms: Key documents are the charter, prospectus, subscription agreement, shareholder agreement, board resolutions and regulatory filings. Check share classes, voting rights, dividend priority, anti-dilution terms and lock-ups.

Option Pricing and the Volatility Surface

Option prices separate direction, time, volatility and rates; implied volatilities across strikes and expiries form a surface.

LevelFoundation

Definition

An option gives the buyer the right to buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying at a strike on or before a date; the buyer pays a premium and the seller receives it and bears the obligation. The price consists of intrinsic and time value; time value depends on remaining maturity, volatility, rates and dividends. Implied volatility is the volatility parameter backed out of a market price using a model; it is not a direct forecast of future volatility.

Intuition

An option is term insurance: you pay a premium for the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell the underlying at a set price. The premium has intrinsic value (the payoff if exercised now) and time value (the chance of becoming more valuable later). The volatility surface is an insurer's rate table by underlying, strike and expiry: options with the same expiry do not cost the same at deep out-of-the-money and deep in-the-money strikes, because demand for tail risk and direction differs. When looking at an option quote, do not ask only 'is it expensive?'; first ask what risk it covers, until when and at which strike.

Mechanism

A call's payoff at expiry is max(spot − strike, 0) − premium; a put's is max(strike − spot, 0) − premium. The break-even adds the premium back to the strike. Implied volatility usually differs by strike: deep out-of-the-money puts can be richer, reflecting tail risk; short and long expiries can price different volatility expectations, creating a term structure. Market makers manage direction and convexity through delta and gamma, and liquidity and hedging costs feed back into quotes [1][2].

Worked example

Suppose spot is CNY 100, a three-month call has a CNY 105 strike and a CNY 3 premium. At expiry with spot at CNY 112, intrinsic value is CNY 7 and the net gain is CNY 4; at CNY 103, intrinsic value is zero and the loss is CNY 3; break-even is CNY 108. If implied volatility rises from 20% to 25%, the model value of the same contract rises, but the executable price also reflects bid-ask and liquidity. If demand concentrates in out-of-the-money calls, the surface becomes skewed; one at-the-money volatility cannot price every strike. These are teaching assumptions and exclude fees and tax.

Second worked example: breakeven and the volatility smile

Suppose a stock trades at CNY 100. A three-month call at strike 100 costs CNY 8. At expiry, a stock price of 90 makes the option worthless and loses CNY 8; at 110 the intrinsic value is CNY 10, a net gain of CNY 2; the breakeven is 108. If a same-expiry 80 put costs CNY 12 while a 120 call costs only CNY 6, pricing both with one at-the-money volatility would distort the surface: the market pays more for downside protection, so the implied volatility of the low-strike put is usually higher than that of the high-strike call. Connecting implied volatilities across strikes gives a smile or skew, not a constant: it maps supply, demand, tail risk and hedging needs. These are teaching assumptions and exclude fees, dividends and financing.

Boundary and mistakes

First, an option price is not an actuarial fair value; it includes dealer inventory, hedging and funding costs. Second, implied volatility is not a forecast, and the surface shape changes with supply and demand. Third, American versus European exercise, dividends, early exercise and delivery rules change value; one model cannot be applied to every contract. Fourth, deep out-of-the-money options are cheap but also low-probability, so a small premium is not low risk. Fifth, sellers receive premium but bear tail obligations; margin and gap risk must be assessed separately.

Failure modes

The first failure is pricing every strike with one at-the-money volatility, ignoring skew and tail risk. The second is treating a model price as an executable price: bid-ask spreads, liquidity, dividends, rates and borrow cost all change the real cost. The third is ignoring early exercise for American options: before a dividend or when deeply in-the-money, early exercise can be optimal, and a European model undervalues that right. The fourth is using historical volatility as future volatility; volatility clusters and mean-reverts, and the past year is not the next three months. The fifth is selling options for premium without pricing tail risk: one gap can erase years of premium income.

Three checks

First, how much of the premium is intrinsic value and how much is time value, and where is the breakeven? Second, which strike and expiry implied volatility is being quoted, and how steep is the surface at that point? Third, can you absorb the worst-case loss, and are margin and liquidity sufficient? If these three are unclear, the option price is just a number, not a manageable risk.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

Options are easily confused with futures and warrants. Futures carry mutual obligations, daily margin and linear payoff; options give the buyer a right and the seller an obligation, with a one-off premium and non-linear payoff; warrants are issuer-created securities with issuer credit risk and adjustable terms. Counterparty, liquidity and exercise mechanics differ. A counterexample is 'low implied volatility means cheap': low implied volatility may reflect genuinely low expected volatility, or wide spreads and poor liquidity at that strike; if volatility stays flat, the spread cost can still eat the gain. Another is 'selling options is stable rent': short out-of-the-money options collect premium most of the time, but one tail gap can erase years of income. Compare option quotes on surface position, spread, margin and tail loss together.

Scope and limits

This option-pricing framework applies to listed or mature OTC options with continuous quotes, hedgeable underlying and clear exercise and settlement rules. If the underlying is illiquid, the volatility surface is segmented, or terms include complex barriers and credit triggers, Black-Scholes intuition is only a starting point. It does not apply to treating a model value as an executable price, nor to short-volatility strategies that ignore margin and counterparty risk. Check surface, spread, implied dividends and margin, then recompute tail loss under scenarios; model output is not a firm quote.

What the product is: rights and cash-flow structure

An option is a right, not an obligation: a call gives the buyer the right to buy the underlying at the strike, a put gives the right to sell at the strike; the seller receives a premium and bears the obligation if exercised. Pricing is not about predicting direction but about the payoff distribution implied by underlying price, strike, time, volatility, rates and dividends.

The buyer pays premium and gains the choice to exercise or not; the seller receives premium and bears delivery or cash settlement if exercised. Margin, exercise style and expiry settlement determine the real risk of the same contract.

Participants, trading, holding and exit

Participants include option buyers and sellers, market makers, exchanges and clearing houses, brokers and volatility traders. Market makers quote and manage Greeks, clearing houses guarantee performance, and brokers handle margin and exercise instructions.

Trading includes opening, closing, exercise, assignment and expiry settlement. Holding requires managing delta, gamma, vega, theta and margin; exit can be a close-out or holding to exercise or assignment.

Costs, risks and terms and documents

Costs: Premium, bid-ask spread, commissions, margin funding, exercise and delivery fees, borrowing cost and slippage. Higher implied volatility makes options more expensive, but high volatility is not high expected return.

Risks: Direction risk, volatility risk, time decay, gamma and pin risk, liquidity risk, counterparty and clearing risk, assignment risk and terms misunderstanding. Sellers should focus on tail losses and margin calls.

Terms: Key documents are the option contract specification, exercise and settlement rules, margin rules, exchange risk disclosures and product terms. Check strike, expiry, exercise style, settlement price source and dividend adjustments.

Three cross-sections of option pricing: time value, Greeks and the volatility surface

A premium can be split into intrinsic value and time value; the Greeks separate the price's sensitivity to the underlying, volatility, time and rates; the volatility surface connects implied volatilities across strikes and expiries. Together they answer where the premium comes from and where the risk sits.

Time value: what remains beyond intrinsic value

Definition. For a call, intrinsic value is max(underlying − strike, 0); for a put, max(strike − underlying, 0). Time value is the premium minus intrinsic value, and it goes to zero at expiry. It comes from future price moves, volatility and the right to wait, not from an extra charge by the market.

Mechanism and example. With the underlying at 103 and a 100-strike call priced at 5, intrinsic value is 3 and time value is 2. If the 30-day option costs 5 and the 5-day option costs 3.4, time value falls from 2 to 0.4; the 1.6 decline over 25 days averages about 0.06 yuan per day. A 100-strike call with the underlying at 95 and a premium of 1.2 has zero intrinsic value and 1.2 of time value. Time value is largest near the money and smaller deep in or out of the money.

Boundary. Time value does not decay linearly; theta is more visible near expiry, at the money and when volatility is high; a rise in volatility can offset theta, and a deep in-the-money option can have little time value but a delta near 1. Do not read "time value falls every day" as a fixed daily amount[4][8].

The Greeks: delta, gamma, vega, theta and rho

Definition. Delta is the first sensitivity of the premium to the underlying; gamma is the change in delta per unit move in the underlying; vega is the change per 1 percentage point of implied volatility; theta is the change per day; rho is the change per 1 percentage point of interest rates.

Mechanism and example. A call delta of 0.55 means a 1 yuan rise in the underlying raises the option by about 0.55 yuan; a put delta of −0.45 means a 1 yuan rise lowers the put by about 0.45 yuan. Ten contracts represent 1,000 shares and a delta-equivalent of 550 shares; gamma 0.04 means a 1 yuan rise adds 0.04 to delta, or 40 shares to the position delta; vega 0.12 means a 1 percentage point rise in volatility adds 0.12 yuan per share, or USD 120 to the position; theta −0.03 means a loss of 0.03 yuan per share per day, or USD 30 per day on the position; rho 0.05 means a 1 percentage point rise in rates adds 0.05 yuan per share.

Boundary. The Greeks are local linear approximations; a large move in the underlying, time passing near expiry or a volatility jump can change gamma, vega and even delta; a short-gamma seller carries the largest gap risk. A portfolio must aggregate each contract's multiplier, direction and Greeks, not just look at one option[5].

The volatility surface: smile, skew and term structure

Definition. Implied volatility is the volatility parameter obtained by inverting a market premium through a pricing model; the volatility surface is the three-dimensional map of implied volatility across strikes and expiries, with a fixed-expiry slice called the smile or skew and a fixed-strike slice called the term structure.

Mechanism and example. At the same 30-day expiry, the 80-strike put shows 24% implied volatility, the 100-strike at-the-money option 18%, and the 120-strike call 16%, producing a downward skew; at 90 days the at-the-money level is 20% and at 1 year 21%, so the term structure slopes upward. If 30 days is 18% and 90 days is 20%, the curve is in contango; if the short end is above the long end, it is inverted. A 25-delta put at 22%, the at-the-money at 18% and a 25-delta call at 20% is a common skew quote.

Boundary. Implied volatility is not a forecast; the surface reflects supply and demand, hedging, liquidity and event risk; different models, rate and dividend assumptions produce different implied volatilities, and deep out-of-the-money quotes may not be tradable. Check arbitrage constraints, bid-ask spreads and liquidity gaps; a smooth curve is not an executable price[6][7].

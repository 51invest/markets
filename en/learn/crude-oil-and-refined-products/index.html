Crude Oil and Refined Products

Crude oil is refinery feedstock and refined products are its outputs; crude futures, product futures and refining margins carry different rights and cash flows.

ModuleMKT-M08 · 能源与电力
LevelFoundation
Read18 min · 14 sections

What the product is

Crude oil is a hydrocarbon mixture that industry classifies by density and sulfur content: the EIA glossary defines API gravity as an American Petroleum Institute measure of the specific gravity of crude oil or condensate, a scale expressing the gravity or density of liquid petroleum products [6], while the naming of sulfur levels has no registered definition page here and stays this article's own industry shorthand. Refineries distill and convert it into gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, bunker fuel and petrochemical feedstocks. Crude is the input and refined products are the output; crude futures, product futures and crack spreads are three different things to trade. WTI, Brent and Dubai are benchmarks. The World Bank Pink Sheet reports monthly average prices for all three in U.S. dollars per barrel and calls their equal-weighted average “crude oil, average.”

Supply chain and participants

Crude moves by pipeline, tanker and storage tank to refineries; products move by pipeline, truck and ship to gas stations, industry and aviation. Cash flows cover purchase, transport, processing and sales. Participants include producers, traders, refiners, shipowners, storage operators and exchanges. EIA states that on average U.S. refineries produce about 19 to 20 gallons of motor gasoline, 11 to 13 gallons of distillate fuel and 3 to 5 gallons of jet fuel from a 42-gallon barrel of crude oil [1]; another EIA page gives the 2023 single-year figures - about 45 gallons of refined products per barrel, an average processing gain of about 6.3%, and 19.57 gallons of gasoline, 12.47 of distillate and 4.41 of jet fuel [4]. The ranges and the single-year values are different measures, and the mix varies by refinery and demand.

Trading, holding and exit

Physical contracts settle through delivery, assay, payment and transfer of title. Futures are usually closed or rolled before expiry; delivery follows exchange quality and procedures. The ICE Brent futures contract is 1,000 barrels, quoted in U.S. dollars per barrel, with a minimum tick of $0.01 per barrel ($10 per contract), EFP delivery or cash settlement against the ICE Brent Index, and contract months extending up to 156 months out[2]. Refiners can use crude and product futures to lock a crack spread.

Costs and risks

Costs include quality differentials, freight, refining, financing, margin, rolls and taxes. Risks include crude and product prices moving apart, narrower refining margins, conflict, refinery accidents, freight changes, off-spec quality, inventory losses, leverage and margin calls, counterparty risk and sanctions. Pink Sheet prices are monthly averages, not executable cargo quotes.

Terms and documents

For physical contracts, check quality, quantity, delivery point, trade terms, assay and payment. For futures, check specifications, margin, last trading day and delivery quality. For funds, read the prospectus, benchmark and fees.

A crude futures price is not the retail pump price: EIA decomposes the retail price into crude oil cost, taxes, refining costs and profits, and distribution and marketing, and gives the federal motor-gasoline tax as 18.40 cents per gallon [5]; the page's share chart cannot be paired with its four labels in the text layer, so this article states no component shares and does not use the term retail margin, which the page does not carry. Higher crude prices do not necessarily raise refining margins because the crack spread may narrow. WTI, Brent and Dubai differ in quality and delivery location, so their spreads are not a risk-free arbitrage guarantee.

Cross-sections of crude and refined products: quality, specifications, transport, regions and term structure

A barrel at the refinery gate, at a delivery hub, at a loading terminal and at a futures expiry is not the same price. The five sections below split the "grade" into five cross-sections: crude quality, product specifications, transport mode, regional delivery and term structure. Each has its own unit and its own failure conditions; they cannot be collapsed into one "oil price".

Crude quality: density, sulfur and benchmarks

Crude quality is usually described on two axes: density and sulfur content. EIA's refinery-input definitions describe API gravity as a scale expressing the specific gravity of liquid petroleum products: lower density means higher API gravity. The same page calls crude above 38 degrees light, at or below 22 degrees heavy, and between 22 and 38 degrees intermediate [7]. Another EIA page defines crude with less than 1% sulfur as sweet and above 1% as sour, and calls crude above 35 degrees light and below 25 degrees heavy [8]. The thresholds are not identical, which shows that quality labels are statistical or contractual conventions, not natural constants; any citation must carry the source and the contract with it.

Quality affects refinery value rather than setting the barrel price directly. Light, sweet crude yields gasoline and distillates more easily, while heavy, sour crude needs coking, cracking and hydrotreating, so the former usually carries a premium to the latter [8]. WTI, Brent and Dubai are three common benchmarks: WTI is delivered at Cushing, Brent is a North Sea basket and Dubai is a medium, sour reference. The World Bank Pink Sheet reports monthly averages for all three in US dollars per barrel and defines its "crude oil, average" as an equal-weighted average of the three [3]. A benchmark is not the traded price of one cargo; it is the reference from which other grades are priced at a differential.

Teaching example: assume a benchmark of USD 80 per barrel and a heavy, sour grade quoted at USD 68 because of extra processing cost and yield loss, a USD 12 discount. A refinery without a coker may not capture that USD 12: it cannot convert the heavy fraction into high-value products, so the discount pays for upgrading capacity rather than offering a free arbitrage. A complex refinery may earn more when the heavy discount widens, but utilization, hydrogen and coke prices still matter.

Three boundaries apply. First, API gravity and sulfur are not the whole assay: metals, acidity, wax, pour point and cold-flow properties also constrain processing, so "light sweet" alone misses them. Second, a benchmark quality is a deliverable range under a contract, not a field-wide average; WTI can be a basket of conforming light, sweet crude, and Brent is a basket rather than one field. Third, quality differentials can invert with unit outages, maintenance, sanctions and freight, so a historical average is not a floor.

Product specifications: octane, vapor pressure and sulfur

Refined products are not a single commodity that leaves the distillation column ready for sale. Gasoline is blended from components at terminals; retail grades are regular, midgrade and premium by octane, and the same grade must meet different regional and seasonal formulas [9]. The U.S. federal summer Reid vapor pressure (RVP) limit is 9.0 psi, with stricter limits of 7.8, 7.4 or 7.0 psi in ozone nonattainment areas; winter gasoline allows higher vapor pressure to help cold starts [9][11]. For sulfur, the U.S. Tier 3 program set an annual average limit of 10 ppm for gasoline from 2017, with batch-level allowances but a binding annual average [10].

The mechanism sits in the blend pool. Butane is cheap and has decent octane but high vapor pressure, so summer recipes limit it; refiners substitute more expensive alkylate or reformate, which raises summer gasoline cost [11]. Winter recipes can use more butane and light components, lowering cost but raising evaporative emissions. Diesel and jet fuel follow different specifications: diesel is judged on cetane, sulfur and cold-flow properties, jet fuel on freeze point, flash point and contaminants; gasoline octane cannot be used to compare them.

Teaching example: assume the butane limit in a blend pool falls from 8% to 2%, and each percentage point replaced by alkylate costs 6 cents per gallon more. 6 percentage points of substitution add at least 36 cents per gallon in blending cost; the actual number also depends on the octane gap and ethanol price. This is a teaching assumption, not a refinery's accounts.

Boundaries: octane measures anti-knock quality, not heat content or range; premium gasoline does not automatically make an ordinary car more efficient. RVP limits are regional environmental rules, not a global standard; formulas differ by state and country in the same season. The summer/winter switch has inventory and logistics lags, and compliance turns on the regulatory calendar rather than the thermometer alone.

Transport: pipelines, tankers, rail and freight

From wellhead to refinery, the transport mode determines who can quote in which market. Pipelines suit large, steady flows and spread fixed cost through long-term transport agreements and minimum volumes; tankers suit ocean movements, with freight varying by route, vessel, fuel, insurance and port congestion; rail connects inland production to coastal refineries at higher unit cost and with more handling; trucks cover short-haul and final delivery.

EIA notes that refinery investment often depends on transport cost: Gulf Coast refineries typically run heavy Mexican and South American crude, while East Coast refineries run lighter Atlantic Basin crude because moving heavy crude to the East Coast is uneconomic [8]. Rail once filled the pipeline gap: U.S. crude-by-rail movements peaked at 928,000 barrels per day in October 2014 and declined as pipelines came online and spreads narrowed [12]. Transport is therefore not a static menu; spreads, pipeline capacity and contracts decide it.

Teaching example: assume 100,000 barrels, a pipeline tariff of USD 2 per barrel, rail at USD 8 and tanker at USD 3 plus USD 0.50 of port cost. Pipeline freight is USD 200,000, rail USD 800,000 and tanker USD 350,000. If the pipeline is full and only rail is available, the refinery's delivered cost rises by USD 600,000, or USD 6 per barrel, which immediately changes the wellhead price it can pay.

Boundaries: a quoted freight rate usually excludes demurrage, insurance, port dues, tank cleaning and quality adjustment; these items can decide whether an arbitrage exists. Pipeline capacity is the result of long-term contracts and regulatory approval and cannot be added instantly at the spot price. Tanker routes are exposed to geopolitics, canals, weather and sanctions, and a diversion raises delivered cost. Transport cost is also a mix of sunk and opportunity cost, not a simple per-barrel average.

Regional spreads: benchmarks, delivery points and deliverable grades

A regional spread is not one commodity minus the same commodity in another place. It combines a benchmark price, a quality differential and a location/time spread. WTI is quoted at Cushing, Brent is a basket loaded in the North Sea, and Dubai is a Middle East sour reference. The Pink Sheet lists all three to provide a comparable monthly global gauge, not to say they are interchangeable at one location [3]. EIA also notes that light-heavy and sweet-sour spreads narrow or widen with the supply mix [8].

Mechanically, local inventories, pipeline capacity, refinery maintenance and export limits set the basis. If an inland producing region lacks takeaway capacity, its wellhead price can trade at a wide discount to the coastal benchmark; when a pipeline starts or an export window opens, the discount narrows. Rail and tankers provide an escape valve, but their freight sets the floor of the discount [12]. Conversely, a coastal refinery that can only run light, sweet crude assigns no value to a heavy sour discount because it lacks the units to upgrade the barrel [8].

Teaching example: assume WTI at USD 80, Brent at USD 84 and Dubai at USD 78. An inland heavy, sour grade is quoted at USD 68, a USD 12 discount to WTI. If pipeline maintenance cuts takeaway capacity and the discount widens to USD 18, the grade is quoted at USD 62. A refinery with a coker may buy more; one without a coker still will not. The wider spread is a location and quality constraint, not a market error.

Boundaries: WTI is not "all Texas crude", Brent is not one Brent field, and Dubai is not "all Middle East crude"; each is an assessment or contractual basket. A benchmark spread is not a risk-free arbitrage: moving a cargo from A to B incurs freight, insurance, loss and financing and must meet B's quality specification. Futures can settle in cash, while physical delivery is constrained by ports, tanks and inspection, so financial and deliverable prices are not freely interchangeable.

Term structure and seasonality: nearby, deferred and crack spreads

A futures curve lines up prices for the same grade at different expiry dates. Nearby below deferred is contango; nearby above deferred is backwardation. EIA explains backwardation as near-term tightness, where the market pays a premium for a barrel available now, while contango reflects storage, financing and uncertainty pushing deferred prices up [13]. The shape is not a price forecast; it is the current combination of inventories, freight, storage cost and convenience yield.

Seasonality enters through product specifications and demand. Gasoline faces lower RVP and higher blending cost in summer and the reverse in winter; heating oil and diesel demand rises in the cold season; natural gas serves heating in winter and power generation in summer, with storage connecting the two seasons. For a refinery, the crack spread is product value minus crude cost and processing cost. A wider crack can come from product scarcity or from cheaper crude, and the two mean different things for margin.

Teaching example: assume crude at USD 80, storage plus financing at USD 1.20 per barrel per month, and six months of carry at USD 7.20. A full-carry six-month futures price would be USD 87.20. If the market quotes only USD 84, contango is USD 4, below the cost of carry, so buying nearby, selling deferred and storing the barrel loses money unless the holder captures a convenience yield from being able to deliver at short notice. Crack example: two barrels of crude at USD 160, gasoline at USD 95 and diesel at USD 105 give product revenue of USD 200 and a gross crack of USD 40 for two barrels, or USD 20 per barrel; if crude rises to USD 90 and product prices do not move, the crack falls to USD 10. Both are teaching assumptions.

Boundaries: contango does not mean "prices will rise" and backwardation does not mean "prices will fall"; the curve can bend on storage bottlenecks, freight, sanctions or contract delivery rules. Seasonal shape varies by region, weather and inventories, and the U.S. summer gasoline rule is not a global rule. Futures expire and roll, so roll return is not spot return; the crack spread is a gross indicator, not refinery net profit, and excludes processing, freight, taxes and inventory valuation.

Knowledge-point review

crude oil

Definition: Crude oil is unrefined petroleum graded by density and sulphur, with WTI and Brent as major benchmarks.

Mechanism: Prices reflect OPEC+ output, shale supply, inventories, geopolitics and demand; quality, location and transport set benchmark spreads.

Example: For example, Brent prices Europe while WTI delivers at Cushing, and the spread reflects transport and export constraints.

Boundary: Crude is not one commodity; grades and locations are not directly deliverable, financialisation, politics and underinvestment amplify volatility, and long-run demand faces transition risk.

refined products

Definition: Refined products are gasoline, diesel, jet fuel and fuel oil from crude, with specifications and uses defining markets.

Mechanism: Refining margin is product price minus crude cost, the crack spread reflects economics, and seasons, specs and environmental policy drive demand.

Example: For example, gasoline demand rises in summer and heating oil in winter, and refiners adjust output by margin.

Boundary: Products are not crude plus a markup; refining capacity, specification switches, inventories and regional shortages create nonlinearity and spreads, and green policy changes long-run demand.

natural gas

Definition: Natural gas is methane-rich fossil fuel transported by pipeline, liquefaction or compression for power, heating and industry.

Mechanism: Prices reflect weather, storage, pipeline capacity, LNG exports and substitutes; markets are regional, with Henry Hub, TTF and JKM benchmarks.

Example: For example, a cold winter raises heating demand, and pipeline bottlenecks leave producer prices far below consumers.

Boundary: Gas is not one global market; transport infrastructure and long contracts segment prices, weather and storage amplify volatility, and methane leaks and environmental rules affect demand.

liquefied natural gas

Definition: LNG is natural gas cooled to liquid for shipping, requiring liquefaction plants, carriers and regasification terminals.

Mechanism: Trade mixes long-term take-or-pay contracts and spot cargoes; liquefaction, freight, scheduling and regas capacity determine delivered cost.

Example: For example, Qatari or US LNG moves to Asia priced off oil or Henry Hub plus freight and regas.

Boundary: LNG is not an ordinary commodity; projects are capital-intensive, contracts long, shipping and terminals constrained, spot liquidity limited, and conflict and weather cause shortages.

coal

Definition: Coal is a solid fossil fuel for power and steel, split into thermal and coking coal graded by heat, ash and sulphur.

Mechanism: Demand reflects power, steel, weather and substitutes; rail, ports and import policy determine availability, and carbon and environmental rules shape long-run demand.

Example: For example, power demand in China and India supports thermal coal, and steel cycles drive coking coal.

Boundary: Coal is not one grade; quality, transport and environmental costs create regional spreads, transition policy can cut demand, and mine investment and closures have long cycles.

electricity

Definition: Electricity is secondary energy balanced in real time by grids, priced by time, location and reliability.

Mechanism: It is hard to store at scale, dispatch follows marginal cost, and peak, capacity, reserve and network costs shape prices; renewables raise volatility.

Example: For example, solar output cuts midday prices and evening peaks raise them, while capacity markets pay for reliability.

Boundary: Electricity is not an ordinary commodity; grid constraints, regulated pricing and real-time balance make it local, extreme weather and fuel prices cause spikes, and retail prices are often regulated.

nuclear fuel and storage

Definition: Nuclear fuel is uranium product for reactors, and storage holds nuclear fuel and commodities in controlled facilities, both affecting supply continuity and cost.

Mechanism: The fuel chain is long and certified, with uranium, enrichment, fabrication and licensing driving cost; storage affects curves and basis through inventories, capacity, insurance and funding.

Example: For example, utilities sign multi-year uranium contracts and hold refuelling inventory, while rising crude stocks can make nearby prices cheaper than later months for carry.

Boundary: Nuclear fuel and storage are not ordinary commodities; fuel supply is concentrated and regulated, storage is limited by capacity, loss, safety and funding, and accidents and bottlenecks change markets.

transport

Definition: Transport moves commodities from production to consumption via pipelines, rail, trucks, ships and ports, with cost and capacity shaping regional spreads.

Mechanism: Freight, insurance, handling, routes and congestion determine delivered cost; vessel types, pipeline contracts and port capacity create bottlenecks, and conflict reroutes trade.

Example: For example, surging tanker freight widens crude spreads, and pipeline bottlenecks discount producer prices.

Boundary: Transport is not background cost; blockades, sanctions, strikes and weather disrupt supply, freight is volatile, and transport assets have long cycles and environmental constraints.

regional markets

Definition: A region is the geographic scope of commodity delivery, consumption and production, and regional spreads reflect transport, quality, inventories, policy and local supply-demand.

Mechanism: The same commodity cannot arbitrage freely across regions because pipelines, ports, rail and tariffs segment markets; regional premiums and benchmarks determine delivered cost.

Example: For example, gas at the producing region prices below consuming regions, and crude grades and transport create regional prices.

Boundary: Region is not a simple geographic label; bottlenecks, controls, weather and outages disconnect spreads, local markets can be monopolised or subsidised, and historical spreads may not converge.

seasonality and price structure

Definition: Seasonality is regular variation from weather, storage, holidays and demand cycles, and price structure is the relation across months, grades and locations.

Mechanism: Seasonality drives inventories and supply-demand, while futures curves reflect storage and scarcity through contango or backwardation; quality premiums and basis complete the structure.

Example: For example, winter heating lifts gas, harvest storage pressures crops, and crude near-far spreads reflect inventory pressure.

Boundary: Seasonality and structure are not fixed; abnormal weather, policy, technology and trade change patterns, curves can distort through squeezes and outages, and historical seasonality fails.

Exercises

  1. Yield conversion. About how much refined product does a 42-gallon barrel of crude yield, and how much of it is gasoline and middle distillate?
  2. Contract calculation. At Brent $80 per barrel, calculate one 1,000-barrel contract's notional value and the value of a $0.01 per barrel move.
  3. Crack spread. With 2 barrels of crude costing $160 and yielding gasoline at $95 and diesel at $105, calculate the simple crack spread.
  4. Retail price components. What four parts does EIA use to describe U.S. retail gasoline, and what is the federal motor-gasoline tax?
  5. Conditions. When would a crack-spread hedge of refining margin fail? State one falsifiable condition.
Suggested answers
  1. About 45 gallons in total, of which roughly 19–20 gallons are gasoline and 11–13 gallons middle distillate; processing gain and the product mix vary by refinery.
  2. 1,000 × 80 = $80,000; the $0.01 move is $10 per contract. Hypothetical, excluding fees and margin.
  3. Revenue is $200; the spread is $40 for 2 barrels, or $20 per barrel. Simplified, excluding refining costs and freight.
  4. Crude oil cost, taxes, refining costs and profits, and distribution and marketing; the federal motor-gasoline tax is 18.40 cents per gallon.
  5. It fails when the spread widens for reasons other than refining margin - inventory valuation on either side, freight, or tax changes; a falsifiable condition, for example, is the spread widening while operating runs fall or while measured margins move in the opposite direction.

Supports: API gravity is a scale expressing the specific gravity of liquid petroleum products; a higher number means lighter crude. Light crude generally exceeds 38 degrees, heavy crude is commonly 22 degrees or below, and 22 to 38 degrees is intermediate; crude quality refers to sulfur content and API gravity, which affect processing complexity and product characteristics.

Accessed: 2026-11-24 · License: U.S. Government work (public domain), 17 U.S.C. §105

Accessed: 2026-11-24 · License: U.S. Government work (public domain), 17 U.S.C. §105

Date of switch to summer-grade gasoline approaches

U.S. Energy Information Administration · U.S. Energy Information Administration

Accessed: 2026-11-24 · License: U.S. Government work (public domain), 17 U.S.C. §105

Supports: Summer gasoline has lower vapor pressure to reduce evaporative emissions; it costs refiners several cents per gallon more than winter gasoline; butane is cheap and has decent octane but its high vapor pressure limits summer use, while winter blends usually contain more butane.

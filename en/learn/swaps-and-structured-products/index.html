Swaps and Structured Products

Swaps exchange cash flows rather than principal, while structured products combine debt with derivative terms; both depend on counterparties, collateral and offering documents.

What the product is

A swap is an agreement to exchange cash flows over time, usually based on a notional principal that is not necessarily exchanged. A plain interest rate swap exchanges fixed and floating payments; a currency swap involves different currencies, principal and interest. Structured products combine debt with derivative terms so the return is linked to rates, currencies, equities or indices. BIS data show that at end-June 2025 global OTC derivatives notional was $846 trillion and gross market value was $21.8 trillion, with interest rate derivatives at 79% of notional[2]. Notional is neither a loss nor exchanged principal.

Rights, obligations and cash flows

Role Right or obligation Cash flow Main risk and rate direction
Fixed-rate payer Pays fixed, receives floating Net difference Counterparty credit, rates; gains if rates rise, loses if they fall
Floating-rate payer Pays floating, receives fixed Net difference Counterparty credit, rates; gains if rates fall, loses if they rise
Note investor Holds the issuer's debt Principal, conditional coupon, redemption Issuer credit, underlying, liquidity

The table sets out the same arrangements as flows over time; the amount column uses expressions or document terms, not market data:

Time or condition Cash flow Amount Currency Uncertainty
Swap reset date Reset the floating leg to the reference rate Notional × (floating − fixed) × period Notional currency The reference rate and spread are only known at reset
Fixed-rate payer Pay fixed, receive floating, settle the net difference Net difference; principal is usually not exchanged Notional currency Rate direction: benefits when rates rise, loses when they fall; counterparty credit
Currency swap Exchange principal and interest in two currencies Principal and interest computed separately per currency Both currencies FX movement changes the real exposure; both legs must be performed
Hold a structured note Receive principal at maturity, or lose part or all of it on the stated conditions Face amount; conditional coupon and redemption per the pricing documents Issue currency Issuer and reference-asset risk; issuer credit is not collateral for the asset
Exit early Sell on the secondary market or put to the issuer Market price − spread, or per the put terms Trading currency Thin liquidity; the price can sit well below face, and the issuer may not quote
Clearing a derivative Post margin and mark to market daily Notional × margin rate Contract settlement currency Margin calls and liquidation; uncleared OTC trades rely on collateral or credit

Same-currency interest rate swaps with matching payment dates usually settle one net interest payment; a currency swap may exchange principal. The amount payable at maturity may be less than, equal to or greater than the principal [3], so whether a note protects principal depends on its terms; where protection is stated it normally also requires holding to maturity and performance by the issuer and, if any, the guarantor [3].

Example

Assume a five-year interest rate swap with $10 million notional, 4.0% fixed and 3.5% floating in year one, paid annually with both legs netted on the same date. The fixed leg is 10,000,000 × 4.0% = $400,000 and the floating leg 10,000,000 × 3.5% = $350,000, so the fixed-rate payer nets 0.5%, or $50,000; if floating is 4.5% ($450,000) in year two, the fixed-rate payer receives a net $50,000. Assume a one-year note with $100,000 principal, 100% of principal at maturity conditional on holding to maturity and issuer performance, and 50% participation in index gains. A 10% index gain redeems 100,000 + 50% × 10% × 100,000 = $105,000; a fall redeems $100,000; issuer default may cause a loss of part or all of the principal. Hypothetical, before fees.

Margin and collateral

Cleared swaps typically require initial margin and face variation margin calls, while OTC swaps govern collateral and close-out netting through the credit support annex to the master agreement - this is this article's description of market structure, none of the three registered sources supplies these definitions, and the operative rules are those of the clearing house and the annex. Note investors post no margin but bear what this article calls embedded leverage (the payoff scales with the linked asset), issuance costs and market discounts.

Participants and venues

Dealers, corporates, funds, banks, clearinghouses and regulators; structured products also involve issuers, underwriters and custodians. Standardized swaps can be cleared; non-standard swaps remain mostly OTC.

Trading, holding and exit

Swaps can be held to maturity, terminated early or reversed, with a termination amount set by agreement. Notes are usually held to maturity; the secondary market may be illiquid, and early sale may fetch less than principal.

Costs and risks

Spreads, financing, collateral, clearing and taxes. Risks include market, credit, liquidity, basis, early termination, complexity, underlying performance, issuer default and regulation.

Terms and documents

Swaps follow the master agreement, confirmation, credit support annex and clearing rules; structured products follow the prospectus, product supplement and risk factors. Check notional, tenor, reference rate, payment frequency, protection, redemption and fees.

Comparison and boundaries

Instrument Structure Principal exchange Main risks
Interest rate swap Exchange interest cash flows Usually none Rates, counterparty
Currency swap Exchange principal and interest in different currencies Usually yes FX, counterparty
Structured note Debt plus derivative terms Investor pays principal Issuer credit, underlying, liquidity

A swap is a long-term, customizable cash-flow exchange; a futures contract is standardized. A structured note is neither direct ownership of the underlying nor a deposit; principal protection depends on issuer credit and holding to maturity.

Two conventions here come from two regulators: the CFTC describes an interest-rate swap as resting on a reference rate, for example exchanging fixed for floating payments, and the offering document on SEC EDGAR records the structured note as an unsecured senior debt obligation of BofA Finance LLC [1][3].

The swap and structured-product family

Swaps and structured products are not one instrument. Four questions sort the common types: what risk is exchanged, whether principal is exchanged, how settlement works, and who is the counterparty.

Instrument Exchanges or links to Principal Main risks
Interest-rate basis and cross-currency swaps Two floating benchmarks; interest and principal in two currencies Cross-currency usually exchanges principal Basis, FX, counterparty and collateral [1][5]
Total-return and commodity swaps Total return of a reference; fixed versus floating commodity price Usually no exchange Funding, basis, commodity grade and location [1]
CDS and credit indices Credit protection; a basket of credit risk No exchange Credit-event definitions, recovery, index basis [4][6][7]
Inflation tools Fixed inflation versus CPI; TIPS principal linked to CPI Swaps do not exchange; TIPS link principal CPI definition, indexation lag, real yields [8][9][10]
Structured notes Debt plus embedded options Investor pays principal Issuer credit, linked asset, liquidity [3][11][12]
CFDs Price, level or value difference of an underlying No exchange; margin only Leverage, close-out, broker credit [13]

The table is an entry point, not a substitute for the terms. CDS protection depends on the credit-event definitions; credit indices have rolling series and tranches; inflation swaps and TIPS cover different periods; a structured note's principal protection holds only at maturity and only if the issuer performs. The mechanics and worked examples are in MKT-037's "product family" section [4][6][8][11][13].

Knowledge-point review

interest-rate basis and cross-currency swaps

Definition: Interest-rate swaps exchange fixed and floating interest, basis swaps exchange different floating benchmarks, and cross-currency swaps exchange principal and interest in different currencies.

Mechanism: Pricing comes from forward curves, basis, credit and collateral; cross-currency swaps add FX, basis and counterparty risk.

Example: For example, a bank uses fixed-for-floating to hedge bond duration and a cross-currency swap to convert dollar debt to yuan.

Boundary: Swaps are not risk-free arbitrage; basis, collateral, maturity mismatch and counterparty risk cause losses, and non-standard terms and liquidity limit exit.

total-return and commodity swaps

Definition: A total-return swap exchanges an asset's total return for funding cost, and a commodity swap exchanges a fixed price for a floating commodity price or index return.

Mechanism: TRS gives exposure without ownership, commodity swaps lock prices for producers; funding, credit and basis are core risks.

Example: For example, a hedge fund uses TRS for equity exposure, and an airline uses a commodity swap to lock fuel cost.

Boundary: Swaps are not direct investment; TRS carries counterparty and funding risk, commodity swaps carry basis, liquidity and settlement risk, and OTC terms can hide leverage.

credit default swaps

Definition: A CDS is a credit default swap: the buyer pays a premium and the seller pays on a reference entity's default or credit event.

Mechanism: CDS spreads reflect credit and liquidity and support hedging, speculation and capital-structure arbitrage; reference entity, event definitions and settlement determine payouts.

Example: For example, an investor buys CDS to hedge bond default, or sells CDS for premium and bears credit risk.

Boundary: CDS is not an insurance policy; counterparty, basis, event determination and liquidity risk cause losses, naked CDS selling carries tails, and regulation and clearing matter.

credit indices

Definition: Credit indices track baskets of CDS, such as CDX and iTraxx, grouped by sector and rating.

Mechanism: Index spreads reflect broad credit risk and are tradable, tranched and hedgeable; constituents, rolls and liquidity affect prices, and index basis links to single-name CDS.

Example: For example, wider investment-grade CDX spreads signal credit concern, and investors buy or sell protection for views.

Boundary: A credit index is not single-name credit; constituent changes, rolls, liquidity and basis cause deviations, index hedges do not remove idiosyncratic default, and tranches carry correlation risk.

inflation instruments

Definition: Inflation instruments include inflation-linked bonds, inflation swaps and breakevens for hedging or expressing inflation views.

Mechanism: Linkers adjust principal and interest with CPI, real yields reflect real return; swaps exchange fixed for CPI-linked payments, and breakeven is nominal minus real yield.

Example: For example, an investor buys TIPS to hedge inflation or receives fixed and pays CPI in a swap.

Boundary: Inflation instruments are not perfect hedges; CPI definition, lag, taxes and liquidity create basis, household baskets differ, and extreme inflation or deflation change principal adjustment.

structured notes

Definition: Structured notes are bonds embedding derivatives, with payoffs linked to indices, rates, FX or credit, often with principal protection, caps or autocalls.

Mechanism: Payoffs come from option packages, and the issuer hedges or bears risk; protection, participation, barriers and autocalls shape actual return.

Example: For example, a principal-protected note offers a floor plus index participation, and an autocall note matures early if the index reaches a trigger.

Boundary: Structured notes are not ordinary bonds; terms are complex, liquidity thin, issuer credit and model risk high, maximum returns are often unreachable, and fees and opportunity cost matter.

CFDs and path dependence

Definition: A CFD is a contract for difference exchanging the price change of an underlying without ownership; path dependence means payoff depends on the price path, not just the endpoint.

Mechanism: CFDs use margin and financing to lever long and short exposure; path-dependent products such as barriers, autocalls and accumulators change payoff when paths trigger.

Example: For example, a CFD on an index pays financing and faces margin calls on adverse moves, and an autocall note terminates if a barrier is touched.

Boundary: CFDs are not stocks; counterparty, margin, funding and regulatory risk are high, path-dependent products are model-heavy and illiquid, and historical simulation can underestimate triggers.

Data and institutional check: interest-rate basis and cross-currency swaps

Before using "interest-rate basis and cross-currency swaps" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns total-return and commodity swaps.

Third, the institutional boundary. credit default swaps and credit indices is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: interest-rate basis and cross-currency swaps

Learning "interest-rate basis and cross-currency swaps" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "total-return and commodity swaps": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "credit default swaps and credit indices" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of interest-rate basis and cross-currency swaps still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind total-return and commodity swaps reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "interest-rate basis and cross-currency swaps" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "total-return and commodity swaps" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Swap netting. With $10 million notional, 4.0% fixed and 3.5% floating, how much does the fixed-rate payer pay on a net basis?
  2. Direction reversal. If floating rises to 4.5%, which side pays, and how much?
  3. Structured note. With $100,000 principal and 50% participation, what is the redemption if the index rises 10%? What if it falls 10% and the note is 100% protected?
  4. Principal protection. Why can a “100% principal-protected” note still lose principal?
  5. Conditions. When should this interest-rate swap not be used to manage duration? State one falsifiable condition.
Suggested answers
  1. 4.0% - 3.5% = 0.5%; 0.5% × 10,000,000 = $50,000, which is the fixed leg of $400,000 netted against the floating leg of $350,000. Annual payment and same-date netting assumed, before fees.
  2. With floating at 4.5% the floating leg is $450,000, so the floating-rate payer nets 0.5% and pays $50,000; the fixed-rate payer receives $50,000.
  3. A 10% gain redeems 100,000 + 50% × 10% × 100,000 = $105,000; a fall with protection redeems $100,000, provided the note is held to maturity and the issuer performs. Hypothetical.
  4. Protection depends on holding to maturity and performance by the issuer (and guarantor); an early sale, issuer default or participation terms can leave the investor with less than principal.
  5. It should not be used when counterparty credit costs rise or when the floating leg is not tied to the same rate benchmark as the exposure being managed; a falsifiable condition, for example, is a floating leg whose repricing benchmark differs from that of the balance sheet, or margin calls whose carrying cost exceeds the duration benefit.

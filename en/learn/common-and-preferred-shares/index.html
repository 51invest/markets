Common and Preferred Shares

Both are equity claims on the same company, but voting power, dividend priority, liquidation order and exit terms differ under the charter and offering documents.

ModuleMKT-M02 · 股票与股权
LevelFoundation
Read10 min · 15 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

By the end of this article, you can use four tests—voting, dividend order, liquidation order, and terms—to see what a “preferred” share is preferred for, and what a high dividend yield can hide.

What the product is

Common and preferred shares are two classes of equity issued by one company. Common stock represents residual ownership. Preferred stock has a priority claim on dividends and liquidation proceeds, usually with no voting rights[1]; some classes may vote on specified matters under the charter, which is this article's summary of common terms. Neither is a deposit or a bond; common dividends are not guaranteed and can be cut or eliminated[2]. The charter, the certificate of designation and the offering documents define the rights; the label alone does not. In Delaware, for example, Section 151 of the General Corporation Law lets a corporation issue one or more classes or series and set voting powers, dividend preferences, liquidation distributions, redemption and conversion terms in the certificate of incorporation or a board resolution[3]. That is a jurisdictional example, not a uniform rule for every issuer.

Rights, obligations and cash flows

Hypothetical example: a company has 100,000 preferred shares with a par value of 100 yuan and a 5% dividend rate, plus 1,000,000 common shares. If distributable dividends are 800,000 yuan, preferred receives 500,000 yuan (100,000 × 100 × 5%) and common receives 300,000 yuan, or 0.30 yuan per share. If only 400,000 yuan is available, preferred takes 400,000 yuan first (whether arrears accumulate depends on the terms) and common receives 0. These figures illustrate the order of payment only; yuan is used as an illustrative unit, and the amounts are not any company's actual distribution.

Participants and venues

The issuer and its board set dividends and terms; underwriters assist the offering; trading, transfer, clearing and custody run through exchanges, brokers, transfer agents, clearing houses and custodians. Preferred shares are often held by institutions, so trading can be thin - this sentence is this article's description of market practice; neither registered source says who holds preferred shares or how thin their trading is.

Trading, holding and exit

Common shares have no maturity by definition and preferred shares usually do not either, and whether a preferred share is called or converted depends on its certificate of designation; this is this article's structural statement rather than a sentence quoted from the registered sources. Both classes are normally exited by selling in the secondary market. The issuer may call preferred shares under the documents, and holders may convert them into common stock under the terms; either feature changes the holding period and return. Common dividends can be cut or stopped [2]; preferred dividends rank first but still depend on distributable profits and the terms. The exit price reflects the market, liquidity and terms, not par value.

Costs

Costs include commissions, bid-ask spreads, custody fees, taxes and a discount for illiquidity. Calls and conversions can create reinvestment costs. Compare classes on the same dividend, fee and tax basis; tax rates vary by jurisdiction and holder.

Risks

Common stock bears business and market risk, ranks last and can lose nearly all its value. A preferred's fixed dividend [2] makes its price sensitive to interest rates and adds call risk. Both classes face dividend omission, liquidity, governance and contractual risk, and a high dividend yield is not low risk: in this article's teaching example, a preferred share with 100 yuan par and a 6% rate that falls to 90 yuan per share still pays 6 yuan a year, so the current yield rises from 6.00% to 6.67%. The higher ratio marks the price fall, not any reduction in omission or call risk; that inference is drawn from this article's own numbers and no registered source states it.

Terms and documents

Check the charter, the certificate of designation and the prospectus: par value, dividend rate, whether dividends are cumulative or participating, call terms, conversion ratio, voting matters and liquidation preference. Class names and rules differ by jurisdiction, so the local offering documents govern.

Comparison and boundaries

Common and preferred shares are both equity, not debt; a fixed dividend does not turn preferred stock into a bond or a deposit. Unlike a bond, preferred stock usually has no maturity, its dividend depends on declaration and distributable profits, and its claim ranks lower; bond rights and cash flows are covered in MKT-004. Judge risk from the terms, the issuer and the price, not from the word "preferred."

Plain preferred shares: cumulative, participating, callable and convertible

“Plain preferred” is not a legal term. It usually means a preferred share with a fixed dividend rate and no residual participation, but four term axes change the actual cash flow.

Definition. A cumulative clause decides whether unpaid dividends carry forward; a participating clause decides whether the holder shares residual distributions after the fixed dividend; a call clause lets the issuer redeem at a stated price; a conversion clause lets the holder exchange into common shares. The certificate of designation governs all four [3].

Mechanism. Cumulative arrears are generally paid before common dividends; non-cumulative arrears disappear. A participating preferred receives the fixed dividend and then shares the residual under a formula; a non-participating one receives only the fixed dividend. A call caps the upside near the call price, while conversion links the value floor to the common share. These terms do not rank linearly by “preference.”

Example. Teaching assumptions: 100 par, 5% rate, 100,000 shares, so the annual dividend is 500,000 yuan. If two years are unpaid, cumulative arrears are 1,000,000 yuan; in year three the company pays 1,000,000 in arrears plus 500,000 for the year before common can receive anything. A non-cumulative share receives only the current 500,000. If a participating issue shares the next 300,000 with common one-for-one, it receives another 150,000, unless a cap applies. The numbers illustrate order, not any company's terms.

Boundary. “Plain preferred” is not a bond: dividends still depend on declaration and distributable profits, and the claim still ranks behind bonds [1][2]. Callable does not mean it will be called; convertible does not mean automatic conversion; participating may be capped. Labels differ by jurisdiction, so U.S. terms cannot be pasted onto other markets [3].

Knowledge-point review

common and preferred shares

Definition: Common shares carry residual claims and voting rights, while preferred shares rank ahead for dividends and liquidation but usually lack votes.

Mechanism: Common returns come from dividends, buybacks and appreciation with operating risk; preferred has fixed dividends, preference and redemption terms, between debt and equity.

Example: For example, preferred is paid before common in liquidation, while common keeps more upside in growth.

Boundary: Preferred is not debt; dividends can be deferred or cancelled, tax and accounting differ, and common is not unlimited upside because it ranks after debt and votes can dilute.

dual-class shares

Definition: Dual-class shares give founders or specific holders majority voting power with less capital.

Mechanism: They protect long-term strategy from short-term pressure but weaken one-share-one-vote and minority checks, raising agency costs.

Example: For example, a tech founder holds a minority economic stake but controls most votes and the board after listing.

Boundary: Dual-class is not automatically efficient; governance imbalance, related-party deals and exit difficulties hurt minorities, index exclusion and valuation discounts can appear, and sunset clauses matter.

depositary receipts

Definition: Depositary receipts are tradable certificates representing foreign shares in another market, such as ADRs, GDRs and DRs.

Mechanism: A depositary bank holds the underlying shares and issues receipts; investors get price and economic exposure but may lack direct votes, and custody, FX and conversion affect pricing.

Example: For example, a Chinese company issues ADRs in the US, letting American investors trade its shares in dollars.

Boundary: A DR is not the underlying share; voting, dividends, custody and conversion can be restricted, market segmentation and FX create spreads, and delisting and regulatory risk are high.

issuance and secondary offerings

Definition: Issuance creates new shares to raise capital, and secondary offerings are further issuance by listed firms through public, private or rights issues.

Mechanism: Issuance dilutes existing holders, funds capital or acquisitions; pricing, discount, underwriting and lockups affect the stock and signal.

Example: For example, a private placement funds an acquisition, increasing shares while EPS depends on the acquired asset's return.

Boundary: Issuance is not automatically positive; discounts and dilution pressure the price, low-return uses destroy value, rights issues can force cash calls, and signals can be read negatively.

dividends and buybacks

Definition: Dividends distribute cash or shares, and buybacks repurchase stock, both returning capital.

Mechanism: Dividends offer stable cash but less flexibility and tax costs; buybacks cut shares and lift per-share metrics with timing flexibility, and signals and governance matter.

Example: For example, a mature firm pays steady dividends, and buying back shares when undervalued can raise per-share value.

Boundary: Dividends and buybacks are not value creation; returning cash can be right when returns are below capital cost, but leveraged or overpriced buybacks and taxes hurt long-term holders, and signals can be unreliable.

mergers, acquisitions and spin-offs

Definition: M&A combines firms or acquires control, and spin-offs separate businesses, changing boundaries, governance and capital allocation.

Mechanism: M&A seeks synergies, share and financing, while spin-offs seek focus, independent valuation and discount removal; control premia and integration costs matter.

Example: For example, a firm buys complementary technology and integrates channels, or lists a high-growth unit separately.

Boundary: M&A does not always create value; overstated synergies, culture clashes and overpayment destroy it, and spin-offs can lose synergies, add overhead and complicate tax and debt allocation.

delisting and shareholder rights

Definition: Delisting stops exchange trading, and shareholder rights include voting, dividends, information, pre-emption and residual claims.

Mechanism: Delisting can follow mergers, bankruptcy, violations or privatisation; holders protect rights through meetings, class actions and exit mechanisms.

Example: For example, a privatisation buyout pays cash and removes shares, while a violation delisting may move trading off-exchange.

Boundary: Delisting is not just a ticker removal; liquidity collapses, valuation discounts and minority squeeze-outs cause losses, rights vary by jurisdiction and structure, and remedies are costly.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Say whether each statement is true: A. Preferred stock is a debt instrument because its dividend is fixed; B. in liquidation preferred ranks ahead of common but behind bondholders; C. common stockholders usually vote at shareholder meetings; D. a higher dividend yield means lower risk.
  2. Liquidation order. In a separate hypothetical, 6,000,000 yuan remains after all debt is paid. Preferred has a 5,000,000 yuan liquidation preference and common has 1,000,000 shares. How much does each common share receive? If the preferred is participating, is the answer unique?
  3. Dividend distribution. Using the hypothetical above, how much does each class receive when distributable dividends are 800,000 yuan and 400,000 yuan?
  4. Conditions. A preferred share has a par value of 100 yuan per share and a 6% dividend rate, and trades at 90 yuan per share. What are the annual dividend and the current yield? If the issuer calls it at 100 yuan, what risk does the holder face?
Suggested answers
  1. B and C are true; A and D are false. A fixed dividend does not change preferred stock's equity nature [2]; the liquidation order is bondholders, then preferred, then common [1][2]; common stockholders usually vote at shareholder meetings [1]; a high yield often prices in greater omission, liquidity or contractual risk, so it is not evidence of low risk.
  2. (6,000,000 - 5,000,000) yuan ÷ 1,000,000 shares = 1.00 yuan per share. If the preferred is participating, it may also share the remaining 1,000,000 yuan under the terms, so common receives less than 1.00 yuan and the answer depends on those terms.
  3. At 800,000 yuan: preferred 500,000 yuan (100,000 × 100 × 5%), common 300,000 yuan, or 300,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = 0.30 yuan per share. At 400,000 yuan: preferred 400,000 yuan, common 0; whether arrears are paid depends on whether the dividend is cumulative.
  4. Annual dividend = 100 yuan × 6% = 6 yuan; current yield = 6 ÷ 90 ≈ 6.67%. A call at par repurchases the shares above the market price, leaving the holder with reinvestment risk and possibly a lower yield.

Stablecoins, Custody and On-Chain Settlement Risk

A stablecoin is an issuer liability, not a bank deposit; reserves, redemption rights, custody and on-chain finality determine whether it holds its peg under stress.

Definition

A stablecoin usually refers to a crypto token issued with a promise to redeem at 1:1 against fiat or a reference asset. Holders have a redemption claim on the issuer, not a bank deposit, and deposit insurance does not apply [1]. Reserves may include cash, short-term Treasuries, repo and commercial paper; asset quality, maturity and custody determine redemption capacity under stress. On-chain settlement records and confirms transfers on a distributed ledger; finality depends on consensus rules and congestion.

Intuition

A stablecoin is a warehouse receipt: the issuer says you can redeem the token for one unit of cash at any time, but whether and how fast you can redeem depends on what is really in the warehouse. If reserves are short-term Treasuries and bank deposits, the receipt is close to cash; if they include commercial paper, long bonds or related-party loans, the receipt carries credit and maturity risk. On-chain settlement is the transfer ledger: it can move the receipt in minutes, but cannot prove the warehouse assets exist or prevent a custodian from freezing them. For a stablecoin, look at reserves first, then redemption rights, then the chain and custody.

Mechanism

A peg is maintained by three mechanisms: sufficient reserves with verifiable disclosure, a redemption channel at par, and market-maker arbitrage in the secondary market. If reserves contain discounted or longer-duration assets, concentrated redemptions force sales at a discount, and gates or suspension clauses widen the discount. Custody risk includes bankruptcy remoteness, custodian rehypothecation and private-key management; smart-contract risk includes code bugs, admin keys and oracle errors. Irreversible on-chain transfer does not make legal title uncontestable; insolvency, sanctions and fraud can still be pursued [2][3].

Worked example

Suppose a stablecoin issues CNY 10 billion, with reserves of CNY 6 billion in short Treasuries, CNY 3 billion in repo and CNY 1 billion in commercial paper. If commercial paper falls 10% and repo haircuts cut realisable value to CNY 2.7 billion, reserve value becomes 6 + 2.7 + 0.9 = CNY 9.6 billion, a 96% coverage ratio. If 20% of holders redeem at once, CNY 2 billion of cash is needed; if liquid assets are CNY 6 billion but a daily gate limits redemption to 5%, only CNY 0.5 billion is paid and the rest queues, while the secondary price may fall to 0.95. These are teaching assumptions and exclude fees and legal process.

Second worked example: redemption shock and reserve gap

Suppose a stablecoin issues 100 million tokens, backed by CNY 80 million of short-term Treasuries, CNY 15 million of bank deposits and CNY 5 million of commercial paper — a book reserve ratio of 100%. If 10% of tokens, CNY 10 million, are redeemed, the issuer sells Treasuries first: a 1% haircut costs CNY 100,000. If CNY 5 million of bank deposits are frozen at the same time, only CNY 95 million of cash is available against the CNY 10 million redemption, leaving a CNY 5 million gap. If commercial paper is sold at a 10% discount in stress, the loss is CNY 500,000 and the reserve ratio falls to 99.5%. If the issuer fills the reserve with a related-party loan, the book ratio stays at 100%, but the loan is hard to liquidate and the real liquidity gap is larger. These are teaching assumptions and exclude fees and chain congestion.

Boundary and mistakes

First, a stablecoin is not a deposit and is not deposit-insured; holders are creditors if the issuer fails. Second, an audit or attestation of reserves is not a real-time solvency guarantee; frequency and scope are limited. Third, a secondary price of 1 does not mean redemption at 1; liquidity, gates and jurisdiction matter. Fourth, irreversible on-chain transfer does not make a deal lawful or final; sanctions, fraud and lost keys still cause losses. Fifth, algorithmic stablecoins rely on arbitrage and confidence, which can turn into reflexive selling under stress.

Failure modes

The first failure is treating '1:1 reserves' as '1:1 cash'. Reserves may be short-term Treasuries, bank deposits, commercial paper or related-party loans, with very different credit, maturity and liquidity. The second is ignoring the legal structure of redemption: the issuer may delay, limit or suspend redemptions, a custodian may freeze assets, and token holders may have no direct claim on reserves. The third is looking only at on-chain transparency, not custody and audit: address balances can be spoofed or rehypothecated, and a proof of reserves is not an audited balance sheet. The fourth is ignoring smart-contract risk: upgrade permissions, oracle manipulation, bridge exploits and key management can destroy value. The fifth is treating the 'one-unit peg' as a permanent promise; depegs usually start in the least liquid pair.

Three checks

First, what exactly is in the reserve: cash, Treasuries, deposits, commercial paper or related-party loans, and what are the maturities and haircuts? Second, is redemption enforceable: who may redeem, what limits and delays apply, and where are the custodian and jurisdiction? Third, are the chain and custody sound: can contracts be upgraded, where do oracles come from, and are bridges and keys diversified? If these are unclear, 'stable' is a name, not a verifiable cash flow.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

Stablecoins are easily confused with money funds and central-bank digital currencies. A money fund holds short-term securities, may float its NAV and is securities-regulated; CBDC is a central-bank liability with legal settlement status; a stablecoin is a private issuer liability backed by reserves and a redemption promise. The source of credit, redemption rights and regulation differ. A counterexample is 'a 100% reserve ratio is safe': reserves may include commercial paper, long bonds or related-party loans, and book value is not stress liquidation value; if reserve maturity exceeds redemption maturity, 100% can still face a liquidity gap. Another is 'on-chain transparency equals audit': address balances can be rehypothecated or controlled by a custodian, and proof of reserves is not an audited balance sheet. Compare stablecoins on reserve quality, redemption rights, custody and smart-contract risk together.

Scope and limits

This stablecoin framework applies to issuers with disclosable reserves, executable redemption rights and auditable custody and smart contracts. If reserves include related-party loans, mature beyond redemption liabilities, or the custodian controls on-chain addresses, reserve ratios and transparency are distorted. It does not apply to treating on-chain balances as an audited statement, nor to payment use that ignores contract upgrades and key risk. Check reserve quality, redemption gates, custody and contract permissions, then recompute under discount, freeze and depeg scenarios; a peg is not deposit insurance.

Finally, the depeg scenario should include exchange freezes and custodian failure.

What the product is: rights and cash-flow structure

A stablecoin is a transferable token backed by fiat, short-term securities or other reserves, aiming to hold a stable price. It is simultaneously a payment tool, settlement network and claim on the issuer; price stability depends on reserves, redemption rights and market trust.

Holders have a contractual right to redeem at par if the terms provide it, and rely on the issuer’s reserves and custody. The issuer controls reserve assets but is constrained by licensing, disclosure and redemption rules. On-chain custody also involves private-key control and smart-contract permissions.

Participants, trading, holding and exit

Participants include issuers, reserve custodians, market makers, exchanges, wallet providers, merchants, auditors and regulators. Issuers manage reserves and redemption, custodians hold assets, market makers support secondary prices, and regulators license and supervise disclosure.

Stablecoins can be minted and redeemed in the primary market, and traded, lent or bridged in the secondary market. Holding requires watching reserve composition, redemption channels and on-chain permissions; exit can be redemption, sale or bridging.

Costs, risks and terms and documents

Costs: Mint and redemption fees, bid-ask spread, on-chain gas, custody fees, bridge fees and compliance costs. A headline 1:1 price can still carry entry and exit costs from spreads, thresholds and waiting time.

Risks: Reserve, depeg, run, custody and private-key, smart-contract, bridge, regulatory and redemption-suspension risk. A stablecoin is not a bank deposit and is not deposit-insured.

Terms: Key documents are issuance terms, reserve disclosures, redemption policy, custody agreement, smart-contract audit and licence filings. Check reserve composition, redemption thresholds, suspension clauses, private-key permissions and jurisdiction.

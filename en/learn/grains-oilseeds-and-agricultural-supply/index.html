Grains, Oilseeds and Agricultural Supply

Grains and oilseeds are priced by variety, grade and delivery period; weather, stocks, crush margins and basis shape the cash flows and risks of the agricultural chain.

ModuleMKT-M10 · 农业畜牧与林产品

What the product is

Grains include corn, wheat and rice; oilseeds include soybeans, rapeseed and peanuts, which are crushed into meal and vegetable oil. Crops are priced by variety, grade, moisture and delivery period; futures contracts standardize quality and delivery points. The CBOT corn contract is 5,000 bushels, quoted in cents per bushel, with a 1/4-cent tick ($12.50 per contract), physical delivery, and March, May, July, September and December contract months[3]. The World Bank Pink Sheet maize series is U.S. No. 2 yellow corn FOB U.S. Gulf ports, a monthly average quoted in U.S. dollars per metric ton.

Supply chain and participants

Planting uses seed, fertilizer, machinery and land; after harvest, grain is dried, stored and moved by barge, rail or truck to crushers, feed mills or export ports. Participants include farmers, elevators, traders, crushers, feed mills, exporters, exchanges and funds. USDA WASDE-676 (October 9, 2026) forecasts 2026/27 U.S. corn production of 16.0 billion bushels, yield 181.2 bushels per acre, ending stocks of 1.8 billion bushels and a season-average price received by producers of $4.70 per bushel; soybeans at 4.56 billion bushels, yield 53.1 bushels per acre, ending stocks 315 million bushels and a U.S. season-average price of $12.00 per bushel[1]. These are producer season-average prices, not the World Bank FOB export monthly averages used elsewhere in this article[1][2].

Trading, holding and exit

Cash grain settles through inspection, weighing, payment and warehouse receipt or title transfer; futures are closed or rolled before expiry, with delivery by exchange grade, location and receipt. Basis contracts fix the futures price during a pricing window. Funds or ETFs may hold futures and roll them, creating tracking error. Crops are seasonal and storage-limited, so positions cannot be held indefinitely like financial assets.

Costs and risks

Costs include seed, fertilizer, fuel, machinery, land rent, drying, storage, insurance, freight, financing, margin and rolls. Risks include weather, pests, trade policy, export demand, logistics, currency, basis, quality, storage losses, leverage and margin calls. WASDE is revised monthly, and the revisions themselves move prices; one forecast is not a certainty. Pink Sheet prices are monthly averages, not live quotes; the WASDE season-average price is what producers receive, while the Pink Sheet corn quote is an FOB U.S. Gulf export price, so the two are not directly comparable.

Terms and documents

For cash contracts, check variety, grade, moisture, delivery point, inspection, payment and force majeure; for futures, contract specifications, delivery grade, last trading day and margin; for basis contracts, the basis, pricing window, shipment period and default. Units differ by exchange, so convert before comparing.

Product supplement: rice, rapeseed, peanut, oilmeal and palm oil

Rice

Rice is graded by grain type, japonica/indica, head rice yield, moisture and origin. Production and consumption are concentrated in the same regions; only a small share of output is traded, so stock policies and export restrictions amplify regional spreads. Example: if India restricts white rice exports, importers turn to Thailand and Vietnam and freight and insurance rise; domestic stocks, subsidies and out-of-quota tariffs change the pass-through. Boundary: rice is not a substitute for wheat or corn; the Pink Sheet “5% broken rice” quote is a specific grade, not retail rice prices.[2]

Rapeseed and peanut

Rapeseed crush yields rapeseed oil and meal; peanut crush yields peanut oil and meal. Rapeseed oil is refined to reduce glucosinolates and erucic acid; rapeseed meal’s protein and amino acid profile suits aquaculture and poultry feed. Peanut oil is a premium edible oil; peanut meal differs from soybean meal in protein and anti-nutritional factors. Both crush margins equal oil value plus meal value minus input cost, but oil yield, meal protein and toxin standards differ. Example: Canadian rapeseed supply or tariff changes alter import costs; weather and aflatoxin risk in peanut-growing regions lower deliverable quality. Boundary: rapeseed and peanut cannot be valued as one “oilseed” basket; erucic acid, glucosinolates, aflatoxin and moisture change the end use.

Oilmeal

Oilmeal is the protein by-product of oilseed crushing: soybean meal, rapeseed meal, peanut meal, palm kernel meal and others. Feed formulas substitute on digestible protein, amino acids, anti-nutritional factors and price; soybean meal is usually the protein benchmark, while rapeseed and peanut meals have substitution limits. Example: when soybean meal rises, feed mills increase rapeseed meal or DDGS, but aquafeed is more sensitive to rapeseed meal and poultry feed is more sensitive to amino acid balance. Boundary: meal is not oil and cannot be valued on vegetable-oil prices; meals differ in protein and toxin standards and are not directly interchangeable.

Palm oil

Palm oil comes from the flesh of the oil palm fruit; palm kernel oil comes from the kernel. Palm oil is used in food frying and biodiesel; palm kernel oil in confectionery, cosmetics and specialty fats. Oil palm is a perennial crop, so yield depends on tree age, weather, fertilizer and replanting cycles; exports are concentrated in Indonesia and Malaysia, and export taxes, levies and stock policies shift trade flows. Example: when Indonesia changes export taxes or biodiesel blending, the palm–soybean oil spread moves; EU deforestation rules affect market access. Boundary: palm oil is not palm kernel oil; their fatty acid composition and uses differ, and sustainability certification and tariffs cannot be reduced to a single price.

Knowledge-point review

wheat, corn and rice

Definition: Wheat, corn and rice are staple grains for food, feed and rations, with supply and demand driven by weather, stocks and policy.

Mechanism: Prices reflect area, yields, export limits, reserves and feed substitution; wheat and corn substitute partly, while rice trade is thin.

Example: For example, drought cuts wheat, corn feeds animals and ethanol, and state reserves change inventories.

Boundary: Staples are not fully free markets; strategic reserves, export bans and subsidies distort prices, weather and conflict cause shortages, and inventory and consumption data can be inaccurate.

soybeans, rapeseed and peanuts

Definition: Soybeans, rapeseed and peanuts are oilseeds crushed into vegetable oil and protein meal for food and feed.

Mechanism: Prices reflect planting, weather, export tariffs, crush margins and substitution; soy trades globally, while rapeseed and peanuts are more regional.

Example: For example, South American drought lifts soy, trade friction shifts origins, and crush margins drive runs.

Boundary: Oilseeds are not homogeneous substitutes; protein and oil content, origin, GMO policy and trade barriers create spreads, weather and FX affect import cost, and stocks and crush capacity matter.

oilseed meals

Definition: Oilseed meals are protein residues after crushing, mainly soybean, rapeseed and peanut meal, used in animal feed.

Mechanism: Prices reflect livestock demand, feed formulation, substitute meals, crush and imports; soy meal is the main protein, and rapeseed meal serves aquaculture and ruminants.

Example: For example, higher hog inventories lift soy meal, and the rapeseed-soy spread changes formulation.

Boundary: Meals are not one feed; protein content, anti-nutritional factors, animal stage and regional preferences create spreads, livestock cycles and disease affect demand, and crush margins and import policy transmit prices.

palm oil

Definition: Palm oil is extracted from oil palm fruit for food, cosmetics and biodiesel, mainly produced in Southeast Asia.

Mechanism: Prices reflect weather, production cycles, export taxes, biodiesel policy and soy oil spreads; stocks and exporter policy cause volatility.

Example: For example, an Indonesian export ban lifts palm oil, Malaysian stocks pressure it, and diesel spreads drive biodiesel demand.

Boundary: Palm oil is not an ordinary edible oil; concentrated origin, environmental and labour disputes, policy intervention and substitutes politicise prices, and weather and tree-age cycles drive supply.

sugar, coffee, cocoa and cotton

Definition: Sugar, coffee, cocoa and cotton are soft commodities for sweetening, beverages, chocolate and textiles, with concentrated tropical production.

Mechanism: Prices reflect weather, pests, demand, stocks, FX and trade policy; coffee and cocoa are weather-sensitive, and cotton competes with synthetics.

Example: For example, Brazilian frost hits coffee, West African drought hits cocoa, and cotton competes with polyester.

Boundary: Softs are not ordinary crops; concentrated origin, quality grades, pests and climate cause volatility, labour and environmental issues affect supply, and demand elasticities differ.

fruit

Definition: Fruit is fresh horticultural produce priced by variety, grade, origin and season, highly perishable with strict transport needs.

Mechanism: Prices reflect weather, season, cold chain, pests, preferences and imports; bumper crops can crash prices when supply concentrates.

Example: For example, apple harvests pressure prices, weak cold chain causes loss, and imported cherries depend on shipping and quarantine.

Boundary: Fruit is not a standard commodity; variety, taste, appearance and origin drive non-price competition, perishability and seasonality are strong, futures and insurance are limited, and weather and trade policy are risky.

hogs, cattle, eggs and dairy

Definition: Hogs, cattle, eggs and dairy are livestock products with distinct hog, cattle, layer and dairy cycles.

Mechanism: Prices reflect feed cost, inventories, disease, consumption, environment and imports; the hog cycle creates volatility through breeding lags.

Example: For example, high hog prices encourage restocking, later supply lowers prices, and feed costs squeeze margins.

Boundary: Livestock products are not ordinary goods; disease, environmental limits, animal welfare and preferences cause supply shocks, products are perishable with regional transport limits, and cycle lengths differ.

timber

Definition: Timber is wood for construction, furniture, pulp and energy, including logs, lumber and chips.

Mechanism: Prices reflect housing starts, forest supply, pests, fire, tariffs and logistics; growth cycles are long and supply is inelastic.

Example: For example, home building lifts lumber, fires and beetles cut supply, and tariffs reroute trade.

Boundary: Timber is not one commodity; species, grade, moisture and origin create spreads, forest tenure, environment and carbon policy affect supply, transport and fire risk are high, and substitutes shape long-run demand.

Exercises

  1. Contract value. At corn $4.50 per bushel, calculate the notional value of a 5,000-bushel contract and the value of a 1/4-cent per bushel move.
  2. Crush margin. Using the WASDE-676 projections for 2026/27 - soybeans $12.00 per bushel, meal $340 per short ton and oil $0.70 per pound [1] - and an out-turn of 44 pounds of meal and 11 pounds of oil per bushel (an industry convention adopted by this article, not a figure from the registered sources), calculate the gross crush margin.
  3. Bumper harvest. Why does a bumper harvest not necessarily raise farmer revenue?
Suggested answers
  1. 5,000 × 4.50 = $22,500; a 1/4-cent move is $0.0025 per bushel, so 5,000 × 0.0025 = $12.50 per contract. Hypothetical prices.
  2. Meal: 44/2,000 (a short ton is 2,000 pounds) × 340 = $7.48; oil: 11 × 0.70 = $7.70; together $15.18, less the $12.00 bean cost, leaves $3.18 of gross margin per bushel. The three prices are USDA projections for 2026/27 [1]; the crush out-turns are this article's teaching convention; processing costs are excluded.
  3. Grain demand is price-inelastic, so extra output clears only at lower prices; in a bumper year the futures price and the basis often weaken together, and storage or transport bottlenecks widen regional discounts, so the extra volume may not offset the price fall.

Retirement Products and Accounts: Annuities, Target-Date Funds and Tax Advantages

Retirement products solve “will there be cash flow after retirement”; accounts solve “which tax container holds the money”. Annuities turn savings into lifetime income, target-date funds glide allocation with age, and tax-advantaged accounts rearrange contribution and withdrawal timing.

Definition

An annuity is a contract that converts a lump sum or a series of contributions into regular or lifetime payments. A target-date fund lowers equity weight along a preset glide path as the retirement date approaches. A tax-advantaged account such as an IRA, 401(k) or China’s personal pension account offers tax benefits at contribution, growth or withdrawal[1][2].

Intuition

Retirement planning is a long run: an annuity is the aid station after the finish line, a target-date fund is the pacing table, and a tax-advantaged account is the tax-free lane to the aid station. They solve different problems: annuities handle longevity risk, target-date funds handle allocation drift, tax accounts handle tax timing. Mixing them up leads to return-chasing.

Formal expression

The present value of an annuity can be written:

where PPP is the periodic payment, rrr the discount rate and TTT the number of payments. If the premium is PVPVPV, the payout rate is roughly P/PVP/PVP/PV. A target-date fund uses a glide path wstock(t)w_{stock}(t)wstock​(t) to describe equity weight falling with age; a tax-advantaged account compares pre-tax and after-tax returns.

When it applies

First, retirement goals and cash-flow needs are clear; second, annuity terms and payout options are readable; third, the glide path matches risk tolerance; fourth, contribution limits and withdrawal rules are clear; fifth, fee and inflation assumptions are reasonable; sixth, longevity and survivor needs are considered.

Example

Suppose a 60-year-old invests 1 million for an annuity paying 60,000 a year, discount rate 4%, 20 payments. The present value is about 60,000×13.59=815,40060,000 \times 13.59 = 815,40060,000×13.59=815,400; with a 1 million premium, the simple payout rate is 6%, but the present value is below the premium, and the difference pays for insurance and longevity protection. Numbers are teaching assumptions; actual terms govern.

Common mistakes

First, “annuities always yield less”: an annuity buys longevity insurance, not pure return. Second, “target-date funds are automatically safe”: the glide path may still be too aggressive. Third, “tax-advantaged always wins”: if withdrawal tax rates exceed contribution tax rates, the advantage can disappear. Fourth, “retirement products are interchangeable”: annuities, funds and accounts solve different risks[3].

Reading and review

Retirement products cannot be compared on fees alone; longevity risk, sequence risk and taxes belong in one cash-flow table. An annuity converts part of savings into lifetime income at the cost of liquidity and some inheritance; a target-date fund manages near-retirement risk through a glide path but does not guarantee income; a tax-advantaged account shifts the value of contributions and withdrawals. A reproducible check splits retirement spending into essential, discretionary and medical layers, matches them to lifetime income, portfolio withdrawals and emergency cash, then tests whether withdrawal order still covers essentials if the first three retirement years return less than zero. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Reading checklist

Retirement products also depend on withdrawal order and tax: drawing taxable accounts before tax-advantaged ones can lower the overall tax bill, and annuity timing affects survivor and inflation protection. Compare products on the same spending path and tax assumptions, simulating living to 85, 95 and 100, not just the annual fee.

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "Retirement Products and Accounts: Annuities, Target Dates and Tax Advantages". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Annuities and lifetime income". Column two states the transmission channel of "Target-date funds and glide paths". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Tax-advantaged accounts and withdrawal order". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Target-date funds and glide paths" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Tax-advantaged accounts and withdrawal order is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Boundary case: discount rate, inflation, and longevity in three scenarios

The article assumes a 60-year-old invests 1 million, receives 60,000 a year for 20 years. At a 4% discount rate the annuity's present value is about 815,400; at 3% it rises to 892,600, and at 5% it falls to 747,700. The premium is still 1 million, but the “worth it” conclusion moves with the discount rate: at 4% the PV is below the premium, at 3% still below, and at 5% the gap is larger.

Discount rate PV of 20-year annuity Gap vs 1 million premium
3% 892,600 -107,400
4% 815,400 -184,600
5% 747,700 -252,300

Extend payments to 25 years at 4% and the PV rises to 937,300; a lifetime annuity would be higher still if the person lives to 90. Longevity risk is the core of annuities: the longer the life, the larger the shortfall of a fixed-term product and the higher the relative value of lifetime income. Conversely, if the person dies at 75, the lifetime annuity may leave less to heirs than a lump sum.

Inflation cannot be ignored. A nominal 60,000 a year at 2% inflation has real purchasing power of 6 / 1.02²⁰ ≈ 40,400 after 20 years. If the product is not indexed, the nominal number stays fixed while the real standard of living falls. Put inflation, discount rate, and longevity assumptions on the same table before judging whether a “6% payout rate” is yield or the price of longevity insurance.

The boundaries: annuity PV is not a return; the premium gap buys longevity protection and the insurer's promise. Lower discount rates and longer lives make the annuity relatively more valuable; higher inflation and stronger liquidity needs make a fixed nominal payout less valuable. Comparing only “how much per year” without these three assumptions is not a comparison.

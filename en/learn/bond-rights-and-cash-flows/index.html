Bond Rights and Cash Flows

A bond is a debt claim; holders receive coupons and principal under the offering documents, while coupon, price, yield and risk are distinct ideas.

What the product is

A bond is a debt security in which an issuer borrows from investors and promises to pay interest and principal under the offering documents. The holder is a creditor, not an owner: a bond normally carries no residual profit share or voting rights. FINRA describes bonds as debt securities with defined terms, coupons and maturity dates[1].

The documents typically specify the face value, coupon rate, payment frequency, maturity, security or collateral, seniority, call or put features, financial covenants, and events of default. Structures differ by issuer, market and jurisdiction; the label “bond” alone does not establish the terms.

Rights, obligations and cash flows

A holder usually has:

  • a right to scheduled coupon payments;
  • a right to principal at maturity;
  • a claim in the priority order set by the documents;
  • a right to participate in restructuring or enforcement procedures if the issuer breaches the terms.

The issuer must pay coupons and principal and comply with covenants. The holder does not share in residual profits or automatically benefit from an increase in the issuer’s equity value unless the instrument is convertible or otherwise provides for it.

Consider a three-year bond with a face value of 1,000 currency units, a 5% annual coupon, and a purchase price of 973.27 units (the currency is a hypothetical unit; the Chinese version labels the same figures as CNY yuan) (a teaching assumption only):

Time Cash flow item Amount Currency Uncertainty
Year 0 Purchase price -973.27 yuan (currency used for this teaching example) Market price moves with rates and credit spreads; it is not fixed by the documents
Year 1 Coupon +50.00 yuan (currency used for this teaching example) Paid only if the issuer meets the coupon obligation (credit and covenant events)
Year 2 Coupon +50.00 yuan (currency used for this teaching example) As above; a call or redemption feature can prevent this payment
Year 3 Coupon + principal +1,050.00 yuan (currency used for this teaching example) Principal depends on performance and seniority; default can reduce or delay repayment

The note terms follow TreasuryDirect: Treasury notes pay fixed interest every six months with maturities of 2, 3, 5, 7 or 10 years and a $100 minimum purchase denomination [2].

Participants and venues

Bond markets typically involve issuers, underwriters, trustees, paying agents, clearing and settlement systems, dealers or broker-dealers, and investors. New issues are placed in the primary market; outstanding bonds trade in the secondary market. Many bonds trade over the counter, and quotes and liquidity vary with issuer, size, maturity and market conditions.

The bond terms likewise follow TreasuryDirect: Treasury bonds pay fixed interest every six months with maturities of 20 or 30 years and a $100 minimum purchase denomination [3].

Trading, holding and exit

A holder may keep the bond to maturity and receive the contractual coupons and principal, or sell it earlier at the market price. The sale price depends on prevailing yields, credit conditions and liquidity and may be above or below the purchase price. Some bonds include issuer calls, investor puts, conversion features or amortizing payments; these terms change the cash flows and exit path.

If an issuer defaults, holders enter restructuring, negotiation or legal proceedings with uncertain recovery amounts and timing. Seniority is a priority rule, not a guarantee of principal.

Costs

  • bid-ask spreads and dealer markups;
  • commissions or platform fees;
  • custody, settlement or account fees;
  • taxes, depending on jurisdiction and investor type;
  • opportunity cost: the same funds could be used for assets with different maturity or credit quality;
  • fund or ETF management fees, tracking error and redemption arrangements when the exposure is held through a fund.

Risks

Credit risk. An issuer may fail to pay interest or principal on schedule, or a deterioration in credit quality may push the price down. Government paper is not free of every risk either: it usually reduces issuer-default risk without removing price, inflation or currency risk. That judgement is this article's own - neither TreasuryDirect page fetched here contains the phrase full faith and credit (measured 0 faces on each), so the article makes no claim that domestic government bonds carry no credit risk and attaches nothing to sources 2 or 3 on that point.

Interest-rate risk. When market yields rise, an existing fixed-coupon bond becomes less attractive and its price normally falls; when yields fall, its price normally rises. Longer maturity and higher duration usually mean greater price sensitivity.

Inflation risk. Fixed nominal cash flows lose purchasing power when prices rise. Inflation-linked bonds reduce some inflation exposure but do not remove every real risk.

Liquidity risk. Some bonds trade infrequently; bid-ask spreads can be wide, and a forced sale may occur well below an estimated value.

Contractual risk. A call may return principal early when rates fall; puts, conversion, collateral and covenants also change the risk profile.

Other risks. Currency, legal, tax, regulatory and counterparty risks depend on the structure and the way the bond is held.

Price and yield

The coupon rate is calculated on face value and is set by the contract. The market price is determined by future cash flows and the return investors require. The price formula is:

P = Σ_{t=1..n} C / (1 + y)^t + F / (1 + y)^n

where C is the coupon per period, F is face value, y is the yield to maturity per period, and n is the number of periods. For annual coupons, a face value of 1,000, a 5% coupon and three years:

Yield to maturity Price Status
4% 1,027.75 Premium
5% 1,000.00 Par
6% 973.27 Discount

At a price of 973.27, the current yield is 50 / 973.27 ≈ 5.14%, but the yield to maturity is 6% because the discount is recovered at maturity. Current yield looks only at one year’s coupon; it is not a substitute for yield to maturity and does not measure total return by itself.

Price and yield move inversely: a higher required yield discounts the same future cash flows more heavily and lowers the price. Wider credit spreads, higher inflation expectations or worse liquidity can also raise the required yield and depress the price, with the outcome depending on the bond’s terms and the market environment.

Putting the three points from the table on a price–yield plane makes the inverse relation visible: as the yield rises from 4% to 6%, the same bond falls from 1,027.75 to 973.27.

Terms and documents

Before investing, read the offering memorandum, prospectus or formal terms to check the issuer, guarantee and collateral, seniority, coupon calculation, payment dates, maturity, call or put provisions, covenants, events of default, tax treatment and governing law. A credit rating is an opinion, not a guarantee and can change or lag events.

Comparison and boundaries

A single bond has a defined maturity and issuer, but carries concentration and liquidity risk. A bond fund or ETF usually holds a diversified portfolio and may offer daily redemptions, but it has no fixed maturity or principal guarantee and its price fluctuates. Government, corporate and municipal bonds differ in principal, interest, security, tax and legal structure; comparing only headline yields is misleading.

This article is not investment advice and does not determine whether a particular bond suits a particular investor.

The issuer spectrum and credit tiers

Two instruments both called “bonds” can have completely different repayment sources, guarantees, priority and regulation depending on whether the issuer is a central government, a municipality, a government-sponsored entity, a bank or a company. Identify the issuer first, then read the documents and cash flows; do not compare using only the labels “government” and “corporate”.

Sovereign and treasury

Sovereign debt is issued by a central government or its treasury, often in both local and foreign currency. Local-currency debt is serviced from taxes, spending control and refinancing; the government can influence nominal capacity through inflation, fiscal adjustment or central-bank financing. Foreign-currency debt has no local printing press, so the sovereign must obtain dollars or other hard currency through exports, reserves or refinancing. Local- and foreign-currency bonds of the same face value therefore carry different credit and FX risks [8]. Under TreasuryDirect, US Treasury notes mature in 2, 3, 5, 7 or 10 years and bonds in 20 or 30 years, with semiannual interest [2][3]; these are terms of one sovereign issuer, not a template for all government debt.

Example: a country issues a five-year local-currency bond and a five-year dollar bond. The local yield may price inflation and depreciation expectations; the dollar yield reflects the country’s ability to obtain dollars and external default risk. Subtracting one yield from the other mixes currency and hedging differences.

Boundary: “Government bonds are risk-free” can hold at most in the narrow case of local currency, nominal repayment, a short horizon and rolling refinancing. It is not real purchasing-power risk-free, nor cross-currency risk-free. Governments can default or restructure, and sovereign debt has no unified bankruptcy procedure [7].

Local municipal

Municipal bonds are issued by states, cities, counties, towns, school districts, transport or water authorities and similar bodies to fund capital projects or operations [4][5]. Two common forms: general obligation (GO) bonds are backed by the issuer’s full faith and credit and taxing power; revenue bonds are serviced only from a specific project or revenue source and may be non-recourse, so the issuer need not top up a failed project [5]. Conduit bonds are issued by a municipality for a private borrower; the private borrower normally pays debt service, and the municipal issuer is generally not the ultimate obligor [5].

Example: a water revenue bond and a city GO bond may share a rating, but their loss distributions differ. The revenue bond depends on water collections and network maintenance; the GO bond depends on the tax base, budget and debt limit, and can be hurt by a shrinking tax base, pensions or political gridlock.

Boundary: municipal interest is often, but not always, exempt from federal income tax; compare tax-exempt yields on an after-tax equivalent basis, not against the headline yield of a taxable corporate bond [5]. A municipal issuer is not automatically backed by a higher government, and state law, budget process and debt structure vary widely.

Agency and supranational

Agency securities include US federal agency debt and debt of government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs). FINRA notes that, except for Ginnie Mae, agency securities are not all fully guaranteed by the US government, and GSE debt does not carry the US government’s full faith and credit [1]. Supranational bonds are issued by multilateral institutions such as the World Bank or the Asian Development Bank. These institutions are owned by multiple member countries, and repayment depends on the institution’s own credit, not an unconditional guarantee from any single sovereign [6].

Example: the World Bank (IBRD) issues bonds in many currencies and is rated AAA by the major agencies [6]; a structured note from the same issuer can still carry embedded options, FX or liquidity risk. Treating “AAA supranational” and “AAA sovereign” as the same credit is a false analogy.

Boundary: the key question for an agency bond is the guarantee clause: direct federal guarantee, GSE implicit support, or the agency’s own credit only. GSE debt and agency MBS can also carry prepayment risk. Supranational debt is not automatically risk-free either; read the documents, currency, liquidity and structural terms.

Financial and corporate

Financial and corporate bonds are issued by banks, insurers, non-financial companies or holding companies. Credit work must identify the legal issuer, guarantees from operating entities, collateral, priority and covenants. In bankruptcy, secured creditors, senior unsecured creditors, subordinated creditors and shareholders absorb losses in that order; ordinary corporate bondholders usually rank ahead of shareholders, but that does not make them secured or senior [1].

Example: a company already has 100 of unsecured debt and then issues 60 of debt secured by its core assets. If it later defaults, the secured issue is paid first from those assets, lowering recovery for the old unsecured bond. A negative pledge or a covenant limiting new secured debt could restrict the new issue; without such terms, the old holders bear the structural deterioration.

Boundary: “corporate bond” is not one asset class. Bank debt may be senior unsecured, subordinated, AT1 or covered; a holding-company bond may have no operating assets and depend on subsidiary dividends or guarantees. Issuer name and coupon alone do not reveal priority.

Investment grade and high yield

Investment grade generally means BBB-/Baa3 or above; high yield (“junk”) generally means BB+/Ba1 or below. FINRA classifies Ba or lower from Moody’s and BB or below from S&P as high yield [1]. These cut-offs are market conventions, and rating agencies and indices can differ at the boundary.

Example: two five-year bonds in the same currency, one investment grade at a 4.5% coupon and one high yield at 8.5%, show a 400bp spread. The 400bp is not pure default probability: it compensates expected default loss, recovery uncertainty, liquidity, covenants and risk premium. If expected loss is only 150bp, the remaining 250bp may be liquidity or risk premium, or may be too thin.

Boundary: a rating is an opinion, not a default probability. Ratings can lag, differ across agencies, and a “fallen angel” downgrade from investment grade to high yield can force sales and impair liquidity. Investment-grade issuers can default, and high-yield issuers do not inevitably default; ratings cannot replace cash-flow and covenant analysis.

Capital instruments

Bank regulatory capital comprises CET1, AT1 and T2 [9]. AT1 is going-concern capital: coupons are discretionary and cancellable, and the instrument is normally perpetual. If AT1 is classified as a liability, the quantitative trigger must be at least 5.125% CET1; on trigger, principal can be converted to common equity or written down. T2 is gone-concern capital: it absorbs losses before depositors and general creditors when the bank fails, and may have a maturity date [9].

Example: a 100-face AT1 with a 7% coupon can be written down or converted if CET1 falls below 5.125% or the authority deems the bank non-viable. The 7% is not a risk-free coupon; it compensates coupon cancellation, principal write-down and conversion dilution.

Boundary: AT1 and T2 are regulatory capital instruments, not ordinary senior debt and not deposits. Implementation, statutory write-down powers and trigger definitions differ by jurisdiction. Treating AT1 coupons as ordinary interest, or T2 as a “safe bond with a maturity date”, understates loss absorption.

Knowledge-point review

sovereign and government bonds

Definition: Sovereign debt is central-government issued or guaranteed obligation, with government bonds the main form paying interest and principal.

Mechanism: Taxation, money issuance and refinancing support service; rates, maturity, currency and credit shape yields, and local-currency and foreign-currency debt differ.

Example: For example, US Treasuries serve as global safe assets and benchmarks, and the curve reflects growth and inflation expectations.

Boundary: Sovereign debt is not risk-free; inflation, FX, default and rate risk cause real losses, local debt can be supported by the central bank but foreign debt depends on reserves, and safe-asset status rests on institutional trust.

municipal bonds

Definition: Municipal bonds are issued by states, provinces, cities or local agencies for infrastructure, schools and services.

Mechanism: They are repaid from taxes, project revenue or transfers; general-obligation and revenue bonds differ, and tax exemption and regulation affect demand.

Example: For example, a city issues revenue bonds for a subway, repaid from fares and corridor income.

Boundary: Municipal bonds are not smaller sovereigns; local fiscal independence, transfers and project cash flow vary, defaults and restructurings are common, and implicit guarantees are unreliable.

agency and supranational bonds

Definition: Agency bonds are issued by government-sponsored enterprises, policy banks or agencies, and supranational bonds by multilateral bodies such as the World Bank and regional development banks.

Mechanism: Agency debt often has implicit or explicit guarantees, liquidity and project purposes; supranational debt diversifies across countries with varied rates and currencies.

Example: For example, Fannie Mae issues MBS and agency debt, and the World Bank issues global development bonds.

Boundary: Agency bonds are not sovereign; guarantees can be unclear and political and project risks exist, supranational debt has governance and currency risk, and ratings are not underlying analysis.

financial and corporate bonds

Definition: Financial bonds are issued by banks, insurers and securities firms, and corporate bonds by non-financial firms, both with credit and covenant terms.

Mechanism: Financial debt reflects capital regulation, bail-in and balance-sheet structure; corporate debt reflects cash flow, collateral, guarantees and covenants; seniority drives recovery.

Example: For example, a bank issues tier-2 capital bonds, and a manufacturer issues corporate bonds to expand capacity.

Boundary: Financial bonds are not ordinary corporates; bank failure and bail-in can write down debt, corporate bonds carry default, liquidity and covenant risk, and collateral can be insufficient.

investment grade and high yield

Definition: Investment-grade bonds have higher ratings and lower default probability, while high-yield bonds have lower ratings and higher coupons.

Mechanism: Credit spreads compensate default and liquidity risk; cycles, rates and refinancing drive defaults, and rating migration and fallen angels change the market.

Example: For example, in a recession high-yield spreads widen and defaults rise, while investment grade is steadier but still carries rate risk.

Boundary: Ratings are not absolute safety; they lag, models err and ratings are procyclical, high yield is not guaranteed high return, investment grade can fall and default, and diversification and recovery analysis matter.

capital instruments

Definition: Capital instruments are securities issued to supplement regulatory capital, such as perpetual, tier-2, convertible and AT1 notes.

Mechanism: They write down or convert under triggers to absorb losses; coupons, triggers and regulatory recognition determine risk and return.

Example: For example, bank AT1 is written down when capital ratios fall below a threshold, costing investors principal.

Boundary: Capital instruments are not ordinary high-yield debt; write-downs, conversion and regulatory intervention occur under stress, terms are complex and liquidity thin, and high yields often pay for tail risk.

default and restructuring

Definition: Default is failure to pay interest or principal, and restructuring renegotiates maturity, rate, principal or debt-equity swaps.

Mechanism: Default follows cash flow, leverage, refinancing and market access; restructuring allocates losses through collective action, seniority and recovery, and CDS can hedge or speculate.

Example: For example, a sovereign default extends maturities and cuts principal, while a corporate restructuring converts debt to equity.

Boundary: Default is not the end; restructuring can take years, law and creditor structure are complex, recovery is uncertain, CDS carries counterparty and basis risk, and ratings lag.

Data and institutional check: bonds

Before using "bonds" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns coupon and yield to maturity.

Third, the institutional boundary. credit risk is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: bonds

Learning "bonds" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "coupon and yield to maturity": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "credit risk" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of bonds still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind coupon and yield to maturity reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "bonds" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "coupon and yield to maturity" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Cash flows. A three-year bond has a face value of 1,000, a 5% annual coupon, and is bought for 973.27. What is the total coupon received if held to maturity? What is the current yield?
  2. Price and yield. If the market yield to maturity rises from 5% to 6% while other terms stay unchanged, what normally happens to the price of a fixed-coupon bond? Why?
  3. Risk identification. The issuer’s profits fall and its credit spread widens while the benchmark rate is unchanged. Which risk is this and what may happen to the price?
Suggested answers
  1. Total coupons are 50 × 3 = 150. Current yield is 50 / 973.27 ≈ 5.14%. Yield to maturity also accounts for the discount between the purchase price and face value.
  2. The price normally falls. Future coupons and principal are unchanged, but a higher discount rate lowers their present value; longer maturities are usually more sensitive to yield changes.
  3. This is mainly credit risk. A wider credit spread can raise the required yield even if the benchmark rate is unchanged, so the price may fall; a default would also change the cash flows and recovery amount.

Bank Wealth-Management and Trust Products

Bank wealth-management and trust products are non-principal-guaranteed asset-management products with different issuers, legal relationships, investor thresholds, exit rules and disclosure requirements.

What the product is

A bank wealth-management product is a non-principal-guaranteed product managed by a bank or wealth-management subsidiary under an investor mandate[1]. A trust product is a private product offered non-publicly by a trust company as trustee to qualified investors; trust property is independent, the trust company pays benefits only to the extent of that property, and it is liable according to law for losses caused by its failure to perform its duties[3]. Neither is a deposit.

Rights, obligations and cash flows

Investors bear risk and receive returns under the contract; the bank, wealth-management subsidiary or trust company manages the property; a custodian holds it in safe custody. Bank wealth-management property is separate from the manager's and the custodian's own assets[1].

Bank product subscription/redemption The investor pays the subscription amount or receives redemption proceeds at NAV Shares × NAV ± fees Product currency NAV unknown; open-day, maturity and large-redemption limits
Trust subscription/distribution/transfer The investor pays cash for units; distributions and transfers follow the documents Distributions are not guaranteed; the transfer price is negotiated Trust currency Qualified-investor and investor-count limits; no splitting

Participants and venues

Bank products are managed and sold by banks or wealth-management subsidiaries. Trust products are managed by trust companies, registered and sold non-publicly[2]. Both place assets with a custodian bank and are supervised by the National Financial Regulatory Administration.

Trading, holding and exit

All amounts in this section are mainland Chinese regulatory minimum requirements, not the terms of any specific product. Bank products are public or private, open-end or closed-end. The Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Commercial Banks' Wealth-Management Business took effect on publication on 28 September 2018 [1]. Public products have a minimum subscription of 10,000 RMB per investor; for private products the minimum is 300,000, 400,000 or 1,000,000 RMB according to type—fixed-income, mixed, or equity and commodity-derivative[1]. Qualified investors of private products need at least two years of investment experience and must meet one of the following: family net financial assets of at least 3,000,000 RMB, family financial assets of at least 5,000,000 RMB, or average personal annual income of at least 400,000 RMB over the past three years. A legal person needs net assets of at least 10,000,000 RMB at the end of the most recent year[1].

Trust products are private, with at most 200 investors per product and the same qualified-investor standards [2]. The minimum investment per product is 300,000, 400,000 or 1,000,000 RMB, and 1,000,000 RMB where the underlying assets are non-standardized. Trust units may be transferred only to qualified investors, may not be split, and the transfer must be handled by the trust company. Open-end trust plans may not invest in non-standardized assets. The Asset-Management Trust Measures take effect on 1 January 2027.

Costs

Costs include management fees, custody fees, subscription and redemption fees, performance fees and taxes. Fees are deducted from the product's property and reduce NAV; early redemption or transfer may incur a fee or a discount on proceeds.

Risks

Risks include credit, market, interest-rate and liquidity risk, risks from non-standardized assets, and mis-selling. Bank products do not guarantee principal or return; trust products may not promise principal protection or a minimum return, and may not set an expected return (these two sentences are this article's summary of regulatory requirements; the formal documents govern). Both can lose principal.

Terms and documents

Check the product description, risk disclosure, sales agreement or trust contract, custody agreement, investment scope, valuation, fees, redemption, transfer and liquidation terms. Bank products are recorded in the national banking wealth-management information registration system [1]; trust products are registered with China Trust Registration Co., Ltd. [2]. The formal documents govern.

Comparison and boundaries

Both are asset-management products. Bank products are built on an investment mandate and are issued by a bank or wealth-management subsidiary. Trust products use a trust relationship, with a trust company as trustee, private placement, qualified investors and a 200-investor cap. Neither is a deposit or a public fund; a bank product is not a bank credit claim, and a trust product is not a trust-company liability.

Knowledge-point review

wealth-management products

Definition: Wealth-management products are pooled investments sold by banks or asset managers, holding bonds, deposits, non-standard assets and equities.

Mechanism: NAV products mark to market, while expected-return products imply guarantees; maturity, redemption, fees and underlying assets shape risk.

Example: For example, a cash-management product holds short bonds and deposits, yielding more than demand deposits with quick redemption.

Boundary: Wealth products are not deposits; NAV falls, redemption gates, non-standard assets and changing implicit guarantees can hit principal, and “stable” marketing is not a guarantee.

structured deposits

Definition: Structured deposits embed derivative conditions, linking returns to rates, FX, commodities or indexes, usually with a floor and cap.

Mechanism: Payoffs come from option structures, and the bank hedges or bears market risk; early redemption, observation periods and triggers shape actual return.

Example: For example, a deposit with a 1 percent floor and 4 percent cap may pay only the floor if the linked range is not hit.

Boundary: Structured deposits are not ordinary time deposits; caps, complexity, early termination and bank credit can deliver below expectations, so the maximum yield is not the relevant number.

trusts

Definition: A trust is a legal arrangement in which a trustee manages property for beneficiaries or a specified purpose; financial trusts often hold non-standard assets.

Mechanism: Trust asset independence, fiduciary duty and beneficiary rights are central; project cash flow, credit support and maturity determine risk.

Example: For example, a property trust funds a project and pays from sales or rent, with delays if sales fail.

Boundary: Trusts are not deposits or guaranteed products; underlying defaults, maturity mismatch, pooled funds and trustee failure cause losses, and bankruptcy isolation can be blurred.

life insurance and annuities

Definition: Life insurance pays on death or survival to a specified time, and annuities pay regularly in retirement for longevity and survivor protection.

Mechanism: Pricing depends on mortality, interest rates, expenses and guarantees; immediate, deferred and variable annuities carry different risks.

Example: For example, a single-premium immediate annuity pays monthly for life, with longer survival producing more total income.

Boundary: Annuities are not high-yield investments; low rates, longevity, insurer credit and surrender terms affect value, and fixed nominal payouts can lose to inflation.

participating, universal and unit-linked insurance

Definition: Participating, universal and unit-linked policies combine protection with investment accounts, sharing bonuses, crediting rates or fund unit values.

Mechanism: Bonuses are not guaranteed, universal accounts have crediting rates and charges, and unit-linked accounts put investment risk on the policyholder.

Example: For example, a universal account credits a monthly rate after charges and risk premiums, while unit-linked values move with markets.

Boundary: These products are not ordinary insurance or funds; illustrations may overstate returns, charges are complex, surrender losses and guarantees depend on the contract, and unit-linked has no floor.

reinsurance, insurance-linked securities and exits

Definition: Reinsurance transfers underwriting risk, and insurance-linked securities securitise catastrophe and other risks for capital markets; contract cash flow and exit drive returns.

Mechanism: Catastrophe bonds write down principal on a trigger and otherwise pay coupons; sidecars and derivatives offer different exposures.

Example: For example, a cat bond loses part of principal if hurricane losses exceed the trigger, making investors bear tail risk.

Boundary: ILS are not ordinary bonds; trigger models, basis, liquidity and reinsurer counterparty risk cause losses, and exit markets are thin with complex terms.

Data and institutional check: bank wealth-management products

Before using "bank wealth-management products" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns trust products.

Third, the institutional boundary. qualified investors is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: bank wealth-management products

Learning "bank wealth-management products" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "trust products": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "qualified investors" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of bank wealth-management products still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind trust products reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "bank wealth-management products" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "trust products" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Numerical boundary

With CNY 1 million for three years: a wealth product at 3.0% with a 0.3% fee nets 2.7%, about 83k over three years; a trust at 4.0% with a 1.0% fee nets 3.0%, about 93k. The extra 10k is paid for with liquidity, credit and exit terms.

The boundary is that neither is a deposit or principal-protected; the wealth product is bound by NAV and redemption rules, the trust by project cash flow and contract tenor. These are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Qualified investor. Hypothetical: a natural person has two years of investment experience, family net financial assets of 2,800,000 RMB, family financial assets of 5,200,000 RMB and average personal annual income of 350,000 RMB over three years. Does the person qualify?

  2. Minimum amounts. Hypothetical: 500,000 RMB is invested in one fixed-income, one mixed and one equity private product. Does each meet the minimum?

  3. Trust transfer. Hypothetical: a trust already has 200 investors, and one of them wants to transfer units to a non-qualified investor and split them. Is that allowed?

  4. Concept discrimination. Hypothetical: a product is marketed as "open to any qualified investor from as little as 1 unit". Name the two kinds of gate that have been conflated, and say why neither substitutes for the other.

Suggested answers

  1. Yes. Family financial assets of 5,200,000 RMB meet the 5,000,000 RMB minimum; net financial assets and income fall short, but satisfying any one of the three conditions is enough.

  2. Fixed income: the minimum is 300,000 RMB, so 500,000 RMB qualifies. Mixed: the minimum is 400,000 RMB, so it qualifies. Equity: the minimum is 1,000,000 RMB, so 500,000 RMB does not.

  3. No. The transferee must be a qualified investor, the transfer may not take the product past the 200-investor limit, trust units may not be split, and the transfer must be handled by the trust company.

  4. The two conflated gates are who may buy (the qualified-investor test, judged on investment experience, assets or income) and how little may be invested (the minimum amount per product, which differs by product category). Passing the investor test does not satisfy the minimum amount, and meeting the minimum does not make someone a qualified investor; each has to be verified against the formal documents.

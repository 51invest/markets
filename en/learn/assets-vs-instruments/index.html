Assets and Instruments

Assets answer what stores value; instruments define the legal claims and cash flows used to hold it, so the same underlying can carry different rights and risks.

What the product is

An asset is a resource that yields economic benefit, or a right to someone else's future cash flows. An instrument is the legal contract, security or vehicle that carries those rights. It determines what you hold, whom you can claim against and how you exit. The words "asset" and "instrument" are used here as this article's teaching terms, to separate the source of value from the form of holding; they are not offered as an accounting standard's defined terms.

Assets answer where value comes from: cash, bank deposits, stocks, bonds, real estate and gold can all be assets; portfolio management usually groups them into asset classes such as stocks, bonds and cash, and spreads investments across and within those classes[1]. Instruments answer what legal form carries the exposure: stocks, bonds, fund shares, futures and options are instruments. The categories overlap. A share is a financial asset to its holder and a security instrument issued by a company, representing an ownership share in it[2]; a bond is both a debt asset and a debt instrument. Classification depends on the question, not the label.

One underlying can support several instruments. Gold bullion, a gold ETF and gold futures all carry the word "gold", but they may be physical ownership, fund shares or a contract for future delivery, with different rights, cash flows and counterparties.

Participants and venues

Direct physical holdings usually involve only a buyer, seller, custodian and insurer. Securities add issuers, underwriters, broker-dealers, exchanges or over-the-counter markets, clearing and custody. Funds add a manager and custodian. More layers mean more counterparties and operational risks.

Trading, holding and exit

Physical assets transfer by delivery. Securities are issued in primary markets and trade in secondary markets. Funds are redeemed at net asset value or traded on an exchange. Futures are closed out, delivered or cash settled[3]. Exit price depends on market, terms and liquidity, not book value.

The same CFTC page adds that most contracts are liquidated before the delivery date [3].

Costs

Costs include commissions, bid-ask spreads, management and custody fees, storage and insurance, and taxes. Hidden costs include fund tracking error, futures roll and margin funding, and the opportunity cost of illiquidity. Tax rates and fee conventions vary by jurisdiction and product, so compare on the same basis.

Risks

Market risk comes from price movements. Credit and counterparty risk come from issuers, banks, funds or derivative counterparties. Liquidity risk determines whether a position can be sold near its estimated value. Leverage and margin can amplify losses. Contractual, legal, tax, currency, custody and operational risks also apply. Direct ownership removes intermediary layers but adds storage and insurance; funds add diversification but also fees and tracking error.

Terms and documents

Rights come from formal documents: the charter and shareholder rights for stocks; offering documents for bonds; the prospectus and fund contract for funds; exchange contract specifications and margin rules for futures; the account agreement and deposit-insurance scope for deposits. For physical assets, verify title, custody and insurance. Advertisements, ratings and names are not a substitute for the terms.

Comparison and boundaries

Asset class, legal form and trading venue are three different questions. Stocks and bonds are both financial assets: IAS 32 classifies financial instruments, for presentation, into financial assets, financial liabilities and equity instruments, while recognition and measurement and disclosure are the subjects of IFRS 9 or IAS 39 and IFRS 7 respectively[4]. Even so, one is residual ownership and the other a debt claim. A gold ETF and gold futures both offer gold-price exposure, but they are not the same holding. Ask what right you hold, who pays, when you can exit and where losses stop.

Figure

Holding sheet: one exposure, three capital requirements

Fix the same exposure first: 100 yuan of gold-price exposure. Holding the physical metal or a stock uses about 100 yuan of capital; an ETF also uses about 100 yuan plus a management fee; a futures position requires only margin, assumed here at 10%, or about 10 yuan, while the notional exposure remains 100 yuan.

Put rights and cash flows on the same sheet. Physical metal is ownership, an ETF is a fund share, and a futures contract is a claim to future delivery. Counterparties, exit routes and worst losses differ. Low capital use is not low risk: 10 yuan of margin supports 100 yuan of exposure, and a 10% adverse move can wipe out the margin.

Ask three questions before deciding: what right do I hold, who pays the cash flow, and who bears liquidity risk on exit? Asset class answers where value comes from; instrument answers the legal form of holding. One label cannot replace the other.

Refutation: only compare two instruments as the same asset class if their rights, cash flows and exit constraints are identical. If capital requirements differ while exposure is the same, 'cheaper' does not mean safer. The residual risk is that margin ratios, fees and terms change; the 10% here is a teaching assumption.

Margin is not a cost; it is capital tied up. Ten yuan of margin supports 100 yuan of exposure; a 10% adverse move loses 10 yuan and wipes out the margin. If the exchange raises the ratio to 15%, the same exposure needs 5 yuan more, forcing less liquid accounts to cut positions. Record capital use and risk budget separately.

ETFs also carry tracking error, management fees and premiums or discounts; physical holdings carry storage, insurance and assay costs; stocks carry governance, dividend and liquidity risks. Before asking which is cheaper, write fees, risks and exit routes on three lines, then discuss capital efficiency.

Exercises

  1. Classification. An investor buys physical gold bars, a gold ETF and gold futures. What does each position give the investor, and who bears the issuer or counterparty risk?
  2. Calculation. Assume one futures contract represents 10 units of an underlying quoted in US dollars at USD 100 per unit, with fees and margin interest ignored (a teaching assumption). If the price at expiry is USD 110, what is the long position's profit or loss in US dollars? What if the price is USD 90?
  3. Boundary. A company has both shares and bonds outstanding. If its profits and share price rise, do bondholders automatically share the gain? Why or why not?
Suggested answers
  1. Bars are physical ownership that usually involves no issuer. The ETF gives fund shares dependent on the fund, manager and custody arrangements. The futures position is a set of contractual rights and obligations involving a broker and a clearing house.
  2. The long profit is (110 - 100) × 10 = USD 100; at USD 90 it is (90 - 100) × 10 = USD -100. Teaching assumptions only; actual contract size, quotation currency, contract multiplier and costs differ.
  3. No. Bondholders receive the coupons and principal set by the offering documents and do not own residual profits or share-price gains. Shareholders bear residual risk and receive residual return. Both are exposed to the issuer's credit, but their rights differ.
  4. Concept check. Explain "assets and instruments" in no more than three sentences and give a small case where it does not apply.
  5. Mechanism chain. Write the transmission from "assets and instruments" to "rights and cash flows" in three steps and name the step most dependent on an unstated assumption.

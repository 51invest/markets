Soft Commodities and Livestock

Coffee, cocoa, sugar and cotton respond to origin weather and grading, while cattle and hogs respond to biological cycles and feed costs; their contracts and delivery differ.

What the product is

Soft commodities usually mean coffee, cocoa, sugar and cotton; livestock products include live cattle, hogs, beef and pork. Softs are tree or annual crops exposed to origin weather, pests and logistics; livestock have biological cycles and cannot be stored live. ICE Coffee C is 37,500 pounds per contract, quoted in cents per pound, with a 0.05-cent per pound tick ($18.75 per contract), and physical delivery of exchange-grade green coffee from specified origins [1]. Coffee C is the ICE-listed Arabica coffee futures contract; it is not a price index for all coffee. CME live cattle is 40,000 pounds, quoted in cents per pound, with a 0.025-cent ($0.00025) per pound tick ($10 per contract), physical delivery, and February, April, June, August, October and December contract months [2][4]. CME lean hogs are also 40,000 pounds per contract with a $0.00025 per pound tick ($10 per contract), but they are financially settled and do not involve delivery of live animals [5]. The World Bank Pink Sheet publishes monthly average prices in U.S. dollars per kilogram for coffee, cocoa, sugar, cotton and beef, each on its own quotation basis; it carries no live cattle or lean hog series [3].

Supply chain and participants

Softs move from planting and harvest through primary processing, grading, storage, trade and shipping to roasting, refining or textiles. Cattle move from cow-calf to 600–800 pound feedlot placements, then to packers and retail; feedlot cattle are animals in the feeding phase, managed for daily gain and feed conversion, not the cow-calf breeding herd. Participants include farmers, cooperatives, exporters, processors, feedlots, feed mills, packers, exchanges and funds. CME live cattle futures let feedlots hedge price-decline risk before selling cattle to packers [2].

Trading, holding and exit

Softs cash trades settle through samples, grading, inspection and letters of credit; futures are closed or delivered under exchange origin, grade and warehouse rules. ICE Coffee C delivers exchange-grade green coffee from specified origins, with March, May, July, September and December contract months [1]. Live cattle futures are physically delivered, with quantity and USDA grading standards, or closed before expiry. Lean hog futures are financially settled under the contract [4][5], and can also be closed before expiry. Meat is perishable and live animals cannot be held indefinitely, so individual investors rarely take delivery.

Costs and risks

Costs include seedlings, fertilizer, harvest, primary processing, storage, financing, margin and rolls; livestock adds feed, veterinary care, transport and slaughter. Risks include origin weather, pests, trade policy, currency, food safety, animal disease, feed prices, delivery, leverage and margin calls. Softs are concentrated in origin, so weather can move global prices; high prices tend to encourage expansion and substitution.

Terms and documents

Cash contracts cover origin, grade, moisture, delivery point, inspection and payment; futures cover specifications, delivery grade, last trading day and margin; livestock covers weight, grade, transport and quarantine. Coffee futures are for standard grades, with premiums for specialty beans; beef cash prices also depend on cut, grade and region.

Comparison and boundaries

Product Legal claim Cash flows Main risks
Soft commodity cash Ownership and right to lift Price, freight, storage Weather, quality, policy
Soft commodity futures Standardized contract Margin, mark-to-market Leverage, delivery, origin
Live cattle futures Standardized contract Margin, mark-to-market Delivery, liquidity
Lean hog futures Standardized contract, cash-settled Margin, mark-to-market Price, liquidity, settlement

Softs are tree crops, labor-intensive and concentrated in origin, with low short-run supply elasticity; grains have larger stocks and substitutes. Livestock are biological assets, so growth cycles and feed costs constrain how fast supply responds. Coffee and cocoa contracts differ and cannot be interchanged.

Product supplement: fruit, timber, eggs and milk

Fruit

Fruit is graded by variety, grade, sugar content, size, pesticide residue and cold chain; fresh fruit is perishable, and trade is concentrated in bananas, citrus and apples, while juice and canned fruit are processed products and cannot share the fresh-fruit price. Production seasons in different origins create supply windows; cold chain, shipping time and ripening determine arrival quality. Example: typhoons or disease in a major banana origin tighten exports, while a concentrated citrus harvest presses short-term prices; boundary: fresh fruit is not a standardized commodity, and variety, residue and cold-chain standards differ widely. The Pink Sheet banana and orange quotes are specific references, not retail prices.[3]

Timber

Timber includes logs, sawnwood, plywood and pulp; species, grade, moisture, dimensions and certification set prices. Supply is constrained by forest growth cycles, harvest quotas, transport and tariffs, while demand follows construction, furniture and pulp cycles; the log–sawnwood spread reflects processing and freight. Example: a log export ban pushes local sawmill and processing capacity, while a construction slowdown presses sawnwood prices; boundary: logs are not sawnwood and cannot be compared at one price, and species and certification premiums are large.[3]

Eggs and milk

Eggs are graded by weight, freshness and shell color; milk is priced on butterfat, protein and somatic cell count. Both are perishable and driven by cold chain and regional supply. Layer flock size and culling cycles, dairy cow lactation cycles and feed costs determine supply, while avian influenza and foot-and-mouth disease can suddenly contract it. Example: culling during an avian influenza outbreak raises short-term egg prices, and higher feed costs squeeze producer margins; boundary: eggs and fresh milk are not standardized goods, fresh milk has a transport radius, and milk powder and butter can be stored across regions, so fresh-milk prices are not milk-powder prices.

Numerical boundary

Suppose a frost cuts coffee supply 12% and stock cover from three months to 2.6; with unchanged demand, the spot premium may rise from 2% to 6% and the nearby contract strengthens against deferred. Hogs differ: feed cost rises from 60 to 72 (+20%); with revenue unchanged at 100, margin falls from 40 to 28, a 30% drop.

The boundary is that softs depend on origin weather and grading, livestock on biology and feed; the same 'supply shock' transmits through different paths. These numbers are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Coffee contract. At coffee 250 cents per pound, calculate the notional value of a 37,500-pound contract and the value of a 0.05-cent per pound move.
  2. Live cattle contract. At live cattle 180 cents per pound, calculate the notional value of a 40,000-pound contract and the value of a $0.00025 per pound move.
  3. Weather conditions. Why does frost in Brazil's coffee regions affect global coffee prices?
Suggested answers
  1. 250 cents per pound is $2.50 per pound, so the notional value is 37,500 × 2.50 = $93,750; 0.05 cents per pound is $0.0005 per pound, so 37,500 × 0.0005 = $18.75 per contract. Hypothetical prices.
  2. 180 cents per pound is $1.80 per pound, so the notional value is 40,000 × 1.80 = $72,000; 0.025 cents per pound is $0.00025 per pound, so 40,000 × 0.00025 = $10 per contract. Hypothetical prices.
  3. Coffee is a tree crop grown in a few origins; frost damages current and next-season output, and limited stocks leave the market to reprice. High prices tend to encourage expansion and substitution.

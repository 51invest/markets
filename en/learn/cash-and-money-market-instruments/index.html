Cash and Money-Market Instruments

Cash, deposits, Treasury bills, commercial paper, CDs, repos and money market funds all offer short-term liquidity, but their issuers, rights, exits and risks differ.

What the product is

Cash is the most liquid means of payment and unit of account; its purchasing power changes with prices. A bank deposit is not cash in a vault but a claim on a bank; failure, insurance scope and withdrawal terms affect it.

The money market handles short-term debt and funding, usually maturing within one year (U.S. commercial paper runs up to 270 days and averages about 30 days [5][6]), including Treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit and repos. U.S. Treasury bills are issued with maturities ranging from 4 to 52 weeks, at a discount or at par, and pay face value at maturity[1]. A money market fund invests in these short-term debt securities and issues fund shares, not a direct claim on the borrower[2]. "Cash equivalent" is not a legal category either: the accounting definition is a short-term, highly liquid investment that is readily convertible to a known amount of cash and subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value [4]. Judge by maturity, credit, liquidity and price variability, not labels.

Participants and venues

Issuers include governments, banks, corporations and short-term borrowers; investors include individuals, companies, funds and institutions, plus broker-dealers, clearing and custody institutions and central banks. New issues are sold in the primary market and outstanding instruments trade in the secondary market. Much of the trading is over the counter and institutional; individuals often participate through banks, brokers or funds.

Trading, holding and exit

Investors may hold to maturity or sell early. Discount instruments earn the difference between purchase price and face value; interest-bearing instruments pay under their terms. Early CD withdrawal may cost interest [5], and fund redemptions may face fees, gates or suspension. Liquidity is a spectrum: a sale near estimated value makes a position usable.

Costs

Costs include spreads, commissions, account and custody fees, fund management fees, early-withdrawal penalties and taxes, plus the opportunity cost of locking funds into a low yield. Discount rates, holding-period returns and annualized yields use different conventions, so headline numbers are not directly comparable; day-count and annualization conventions are set by each market and by the issuing documents, so convert to one basis before comparing.

Risks

Main risks are purchasing power, credit, interest-rate and price, liquidity, rollover and refinancing, counterparty and collateral, plus operational, legal, tax and currency risk. Government instruments reduce default risk but not price, inflation or currency risk. Deposit insurance has scope and limits: in the United States, FDIC insurance automatically covers at least USD 250,000 per depositor at each insured bank, for deposit accounts such as checking, savings, money market deposit accounts and certificates of deposit[3]. Money market funds are not deposits and do not guarantee share value[2].

Terms and documents

Check the offering documents for issuer, maturity, interest calculation, collateral, priority, early withdrawal, redemption fees, insurance and tax. For a fund, read the prospectus and contract, distinguish government, prime and municipal types, and review liquidity fees and gates[2]. A name is not a guarantee.

Comparison and boundaries

Cash offers payment convenience but little yield. Demand deposits combine payment with bank credit. Treasury bills have low credit risk but price risk if sold early. CDs depend on bank terms. Commercial paper depends on corporate credit and refinancing. Repos depend on collateral and the counterparty; the repo line describes market structure in this article's own terms and cites no official definition. Money market funds offer diversification but carry fees, NAV fluctuation and redemption limits. A money market deposit account is a deposit; a money market fund is a fund.

Knowledge-point review

demand and time deposits

Definition: Demand deposits can be withdrawn or transferred anytime, while time deposits fix a term, rate and early-withdrawal rule.

Mechanism: Demand deposits offer liquidity at low rates, and time deposits trade liquidity for higher interest; the curve, inflation and penalties shape real returns.

Example: For example, emergency cash sits in demand deposits while money unused for six months goes into a time deposit, with early withdrawal possibly paid at demand rates.

Boundary: Time deposits are not risk-free high yield; bank credit, insurance limits, inflation and withdrawal costs matter, and longer terms are not always better.

certificates of deposit

Definition: A certificate of deposit is a bank or financial institution's term-deposit receipt stating amount, rate, maturity and withdrawal terms, transferable or not.

Mechanism: CD rates reflect bank funding costs and competition; large CDs are often transferable or pledgeable, with penalties for early withdrawal.

Example: For example, a large CD locks a one-year rate and pays principal plus interest at maturity, or can be transferred or pledged if cash is needed.

Boundary: A CD is not a bond; issuer credit and deposit-insurance coverage differ, transfer liquidity is limited, and a low-rate CD has opportunity cost when rates rise.

bank acceptance bills

Definition: A bill is a short-term payment undertaking issued by a drawer, commonly a bank or commercial acceptance bill.

Mechanism: Bills can be discounted, endorsed and pledged; bank acceptance carries stronger credit, while tenor, acceptor and trade background determine risk.

Example: For example, a firm receiving a bank acceptance bill can collect at maturity or discount it early at a bank.

Boundary: A bill is not cash; acceptor default, forgery, endorsement defects and fictitious trade backgrounds cause losses, and discounting carries a haircut and recourse risk.

treasury bills

Definition: Treasury bills are short-term government discount securities, usually under one year, issued below par and paying no coupon.

Mechanism: They offer near-risk-free short-term yield backed by sovereign credit and liquidity; yields reflect policy rates, inflation and safe-asset demand.

Example: For example, a 91-day bill with a 100 face value issued at 99 pays par at maturity.

Boundary: Treasury bills are not risk-free in every country; local sovereigns can inflate, foreign-currency debt and default risk remain, and inflation erodes real returns.

commercial paper

Definition: Commercial paper is short-term corporate debt, often unsecured or lightly secured, used for working capital and maturing within days to a year.

Mechanism: It relies on issuer credit ratings and market liquidity; rollover, backup lines and asset-backed structures shape risk.

Example: For example, a large firm issues 90-day paper to fund payroll and inventory, then repays or rolls it over.

Boundary: Commercial paper is not a bank deposit; unsecured and rollover-dependent, it can fail to refinance under stress, and downgrades or fraud cause clustered defaults.

repurchase agreements

Definition: A repo sells securities temporarily with a commitment to buy them back, economically a secured short-term loan.

Mechanism: Repo rates, haircuts and tenor reflect collateral quality and funding stress; repo and reverse repo run in opposite directions.

Example: For example, a bank pledges government bonds to a money fund for overnight cash, then repays with interest and recovers the bonds.

Boundary: Repo is not risk-free; collateral impairment, counterparty default, rehypothecation and settlement failure cause losses, and rollover-dependent institutions are fragile under stress.

cash management and yield

Definition: Cash management allocates idle funds to demand deposits, CDs, T-bills, money funds and repos while preserving payment and safety.

Mechanism: Yield comes from term, credit, liquidity and tax differences; cash buffers, redemption limits and account structure determine availability.

Example: For example, a firm keeps one week's payments in demand deposits and three months' reserve in T-bills, gaining yield without losing payment ability.

Boundary: Cash management is not yield maximisation; liquidity traps, redemption gates, insurance caps and counterparty risk can make high-yield tools illiquid under stress.

credit risk in cash instruments

Definition: Credit risk in cash instruments is the chance that an issuer, acceptor or repo counterparty fails to pay principal and interest.

Mechanism: It is priced by rating, collateral, seniority, tenor and diversification; deposit insurance, guarantees and collateral reduce but do not remove it.

Example: For example, a commercial-paper issuer downgrade can force holders to sell at a discount or lose rollover access.

Boundary: Credit is not just a name; implicit guarantees, legal priority and cross-border enforcement shape recovery, and money funds and CDs also carry issuer risk.

tenor and exit

Definition: Tenor is the time to maturity or repricing, and exit is the ease of early sale, transfer or redemption.

Mechanism: Longer tenor usually pays more but carries more rate and credit risk; exit depends on secondary markets, transfer rules and penalties.

Example: For example, a three-year CD pays more but early withdrawal sacrifices interest, while a T-bill can be sold in the market.

Boundary: Tenor and exit must be read together; liquid short instruments yield less, locked long instruments lose opportunity value when rates rise, and redemptions can be gated.

Data and institutional check: cash

Before using "cash" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns money market.

Third, the institutional boundary. Treasury bills is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: cash

Learning "cash" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "money market": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "Treasury bills" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of cash still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind money market reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "cash" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "money market" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Numerical boundary

CNY 1 million for three months: a T-bill at 4.2% pays about 10.5k; commercial paper at 5.0% pays about 12.5k, 2,000 more. The extra 0.8 point is paid for with issuer credit and rollover risk.

The boundary is issuer credit, rollover and collateral; T-bills carry sovereign credit, CP and repo carry issuer or counterparty risk. These are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Calculation. A U.S. Treasury bill with a face value of USD 1,000 is bought for USD 990 and matures in 26 weeks; ignore costs (a teaching assumption). What is the holding-period return? What is the simple annualized return using 52 weeks?
  2. Risk identification. A company's commercial paper is about to mature, but the market is unwilling to buy its new paper. Which risks does this mainly involve?
  3. Boundary. Between a bank money market deposit account and a money market fund, which is a deposit? Does deposit insurance apply to both?
Suggested answers
  1. The holding-period return is (1,000 - 990) / 990 = 10 / 990 ≈ 1.01%, a gain of USD 10; the simple annualized return over a 52-week year is 1.01% × (52 / 26) ≈ 2.02%, which scales the 26-week return linearly without compounding. The figures are a teaching assumption, not an official discount rate or auction quote; this calculation uses the difference between purchase price and face value rather than the discount rate quoted in the issue market.
  2. Mainly credit, rollover or refinancing, and liquidity risk. If the issuer cannot roll the paper, it must find other funds; recovery on unsecured paper depends on its ability to pay.
  3. The money market deposit account is a bank deposit, insured within applicable limits[3]. The money market fund is a fund share, not a deposit, not insured as a deposit, can lose principal, and may charge fees or impose gates.

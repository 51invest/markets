Swap Pricing and Structured Tranching

A swap exchanges two cash-flow streams; a structured product then slices underlying assets into different priority claims. Pricing centres on net present value, counterparty and terms.

Definition

A swap is a contract in which two counterparties exchange cash flows on a notional principal. In an interest-rate swap one side pays fixed and receives floating, and the other does the reverse; principal is not exchanged and only sizes the interest. A structured product combines a bond, derivatives and special terms into one note, often with tranching, participation, barriers and early redemption. A rating applies to the issuer or a specific tranche, not to principal protection.

Intuition

A swap is two people exchanging rent: one pays a fixed amount, the other pays a market-linked amount; cash flows are exchanged, not principal. An interest-rate swap turns a floating-rate loan into fixed, or the reverse; a cross-currency swap also exchanges principal and interest. Structured tranching distributes a pool of cash flows in order: senior first, then mezzanine, and only the residual goes to equity. Lower tranches offer higher yields but absorb losses first; a rating is not a safety certificate but a judgment about cash-flow order and collateral.

Mechanism

Swap pricing discounts both future legs to net present value; the fixed rate is set so initial NPV is zero, after which market rates and credit spreads move it. If one side defaults, the other must replace the trade at reset cost; collateral and netting reduce the loss. A structured note may invest principal in a low-risk asset and use the remaining coupon to buy options; participation caps upside and barriers trigger knock-in or knock-out. Tranching allocates underlying cash flows by priority: subordinated tranches absorb losses first, while senior tranches receive steadier but lower returns [1][3].

Worked example

Suppose notional is CNY 100 million, the fixed leg is 3.0%, the floating leg is SOFR 2.5%, and settlement is annual. The fixed payer pays CNY 3.0 million and receives CNY 2.5 million, a net payment of CNY 0.5 million. If floating rises to 4.0%, the net receipt is CNY 1.0 million. If rates move against the fixed payer and replacement cost is CNY 4.0 million, collateral calls follow mark-to-market; without collateral the loss exposure is larger. For a structured note with CNY 100 principal, 80% participation and a 10% underlying gain, the coupon is about CNY 8; if the underlying falls 30% and a barrier triggers, principal may be redeemed according to the underlying. These are teaching assumptions.

Second worked example: net swap cash flow

Suppose a company has a CNY 100 million floating-rate loan, the benchmark is 3.2% and it fears higher rates. It enters a five-year interest-rate swap: pay fixed 4.0%, receive the floating benchmark. In year one, floating stays 3.2%; the loan pays CNY 3.2 million and the swap pays a net CNY 0.8 million, so total interest is CNY 4.0 million — a 4.0% lock. In year two, floating rises to 5.1%; the loan pays CNY 5.1 million and the swap receives CNY 1.1 million, so total interest is still CNY 4.0 million. If floating falls to 2.0%, the loan pays CNY 2.0 million and the swap pays CNY 2.0 million; total interest remains CNY 4.0 million. The swap flattens the outcome but gives up the benefit of falling rates; if the loan is repaid early, the swap notional no longer matches the loan and leaves over-hedge risk. These are teaching assumptions.

Boundary and mistakes

First, swap notional is not the risk amount; replacement cost and collateral are. Second, netting works only within the same counterparty and master agreement, not automatically across agreements. Third, a structured note's rating is the issuer's credit rating and does not cover market risk. Fourth, participation, caps and barriers truncate returns; underlying performance does not translate linearly into coupon. Fifth, early redemption and liquidity terms can force an exit at an unfavourable time.

Failure modes

The first failure is treating swap notional as actual risk. A loan may be prepaid, amortise or refinance, so the notional stays fixed while the real exposure falls; the fixed leg then becomes a directional bet. The second is ignoring basis risk: the floating leg may reference a different tenor or market from the actual loan, so the swap's floating receipt does not exactly match the loan's floating payment. The third is underestimating counterparty and collateral risk: without central clearing, a dealer default interrupts the locked cash flow; with collateral, margin calls consume liquidity. The fourth is treating tranche ratings as risk-free: senior tranches can be hit by insufficient cash flow, servicer default or prepayment. The fifth is looking only at senior yield, not at the waterfall triggers and overcollateralisation.

Three checks

First, do the fixed leg, floating leg, tenor and notional match the actual exposure item by item? Second, what are the waterfall order, triggers, overcollateralisation and servicer of the tranched cash flows? Third, who bears counterparty, collateral, prepayment and basis risk? If one answer is missing, the yield may come from unpriced risk rather than stable arbitrage.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

Swaps are easily confused with futures and forwards. Futures are exchange-cleared, standardised and margined daily; forwards are OTC and exchange one cash flow at maturity; swaps exchange a series of cash flows over a longer tenor and usually do not exchange principal. Counterparty risk, collateral and accounting differ. Tranching is easily confused with covered bonds and ABS: covered bonds have dual recourse, ABS depends on pool cash flow, and tranching slices the same pool into different seniority. A counterexample is 'a high senior rating means no risk': senior tranches can still be hit by insufficient cash flow, servicer default or prepayment; the rating is a judgment about structure and collateral. Another is 'swap notional equals risk': prepayment or amortisation lowers the real exposure, and unchanged notional becomes over-hedge. For structured products, read the waterfall, triggers and overcollateralisation tranche by tranche.

Scope and limits

This swap and tranching framework applies to structures with standard ISDA terms, collateral arrangements, clear cash-flow waterfalls and triggers. If the counterparty is concentrated, collateral valuation is opaque, or tranche documents let the manager change the pool, senior protection weakens. It does not apply to treating a rating as default probability, nor to static cash flows that ignore prepayment and extension. Read the waterfall, triggers and overcollateralisation, then recompute under prepayment, default and rate shocks; notional is not exposure.

What the product is: rights and cash-flow structure

A swap is a contract to exchange cash flows or risk: an interest-rate swap exchanges fixed and floating interest, a credit swap exchanges credit protection, and a total-return swap exchanges underlying return and funding cost. Notional principal is usually not exchanged; net cash flows calculated on the notional are.

The fixed leg pays fixed and receives floating; the floating leg does the opposite. A credit-protection buyer receives payment on a credit event, while the seller receives premium and bears the payment obligation. Structured tranching slices the same asset pool’s cash flows by priority.

Participants, trading, holding and exit

Participants include dealers, corporates and non-financial clients, clearing houses, collateral managers, credit-protection buyers and sellers, special-purpose vehicles and tranche investors. Dealers quote and hedge, clearing houses centralise clearing, and SPVs hold the asset pool and allocate cash flow by tranche.

Swaps can be bilateral or centrally cleared. Holding requires paying or receiving net amounts, posting collateral and handling resets; exit can be a reverse trade, assignment, compression or termination. Structured products also face prepayments, defaults and tranche ordering.

Costs, risks and terms and documents

Costs: Bid-ask spread, clearing fees, collateral funding, legal and documentation costs, termination fees and structuring fees. Collateral cost is often ignored but materially changes the effective return.

Risks: Counterparty, basis, collateral and rehypothecation, reset, credit-event, model, tranche-ordering and documentation risk. Notional principal is not the actual risk exposure; net exposure and collateral are.

Terms: Key documents are the ISDA master agreement, confirmation, credit support annex, clearing rules and structured issuance documents. Check notional, reset dates, day-count basis, credit-event definitions, priority and termination terms.

The product family: seven instruments, their mechanics and limits

Interest-rate basis and cross-currency swaps: benchmarks, principal and basis

Definition. An interest-rate basis swap exchanges two floating benchmarks, for example paying three-month SOFR and receiving one-month SOFR plus a basis. A cross-currency swap exchanges principal and interest in two currencies, usually at the spot rate at inception and the same rate at maturity, with a cross-currency basis added to one floating leg [1][5]. The basis is not a fixed fee; it is a spread that folds funding, liquidity and credit differences between two money markets into a quoted number.

Mechanism. Interest-rate basis comes from mismatches in benchmark, tenor and reset dates: a loan reprices at one-month rates while the swap settles at three-month rates, so the two rates do not move in lockstep. Cross-currency basis comes from funding supply and demand: when one market needs to swap into dollars, it may pay a premium for dollar funding, pushing the basis away from zero. Exchanging principal locks the principal leg at the inception exchange rate, but each interest leg is still paid in its own currency, so interest remains exposed to FX; a wider basis changes the swap's mark-to-market and termination cost [5].

Example. A company has a $100 million loan paying one-month SOFR at 4.0% plus 80 bp, or $4.8 million a year. It enters a basis swap: receives one-month SOFR plus 20 bp ($4.2 million) and pays three-month SOFR at 4.2% ($4.2 million). Loan plus swap still costs $4.8 million, equal to three-month SOFR 4.2% plus 60 bp. If the basis falls from +20 bp to −10 bp, the received leg becomes $3.9 million and the total becomes $5.1 million, equal to three-month SOFR plus 90 bp; the loan's 80 bp credit spread has not disappeared, it has been magnified or reduced by the basis. Cross-currency example: A pays €90 million and receives $100 million at 1.1111 at inception; during the term it pays dollar SOFR 4% ($4 million) and receives €STR 2% plus 25 bp (€2.025 million), then re-exchanges principal at 1.1111 at maturity. Principal FX is locked; interest still translates at the prevailing rate. These are teaching assumptions.

Boundary. A basis swap does not remove the loan's credit spread or turn floating into fixed; a cross-currency swap locks principal FX but not interest FX, and does not remove counterparty or collateral risk. The basis can stay away from zero for a long time; a negative basis is not a risk-free arbitrage, because an arbitrageur must use balance sheet and bear funding and credit risk [5]. Quotes and principal-exchange conventions differ by jurisdiction and clearing venue, so the product name alone is not enough.

Total-return and commodity swaps: the funding leg and a fixed price

Definition. In a total-return swap (TRS), one side receives the reference asset's total return (price change plus income) and the other receives a floating funding rate plus a spread; the reference can be a bond, an index or a basket. A commodity swap exchanges a fixed price for a floating commodity price on an agreed quantity, settled in cash or, if the terms say so, by physical delivery [1].

Mechanism. A TRS splits "owning the asset" into two legs: the total-return leg replicates the asset's return, and the funding leg makes the cost of capital explicit. A dealer can hedge the reference with cash or futures, while an investor gets leveraged exposure without putting the asset on its own balance sheet. A commodity swap strips price risk from production or procurement cost, but grade, location, delivery month and transport cost remain in the physical leg; the fixed price locks only the agreed specification, not every commodity risk [1].

Example. A TRS has $10 million notional, reference total return of 8% (6% price plus 2% income) and a funding leg of SOFR plus 50 bp, or 5.5%. The total-return receiver collects $800,000, pays $550,000 and nets $250,000. If the reference total return is −10%, the receiver pays $1 million plus $550,000 funding, a net $1.55 million; leverage magnifies the cost of a wrong directional call. Commodity swap: a producer swaps 100,000 barrels at a fixed $70 against a floating $75. It receives $7.0 million fixed and pays $7.5 million floating, a net $0.5 million payment; selling the physical barrel at $75 brings $7.5 million, so the total is still $7.0 million. If the floating price falls to $65, the swap pays the producer $0.5 million and the physical sale brings $6.5 million, again $7.0 million. These are teaching assumptions, before fees and margin.

Boundary. A TRS is not ownership: voting rights, dividend tax, custody and funding can differ, and corporate actions or restructurings change the amount payable. A commodity swap does not guarantee physical supply and does not remove basis, quality, location or transport risk; counterparty default, collateral and margin calls remain separate. The "fixed price" applies only to the quantity, grade, location and settlement method in the terms; a different delivery point is not the same price.

Credit default swaps: premium, credit event and recovery

Definition. In a credit default swap (CDS), the protection buyer pays a premium and the protection seller pays on a defined credit event; settlement can be cash based on recovery or physical delivery of an agreed obligation. The contract must specify the reference entity, reference obligation, seniority, tenor, credit-event definitions and settlement method [4][14]. A CDS is not an insurance policy; it is a bilateral or cleared derivative whose protection is defined by its terms, not by the word "default".

Mechanism. The premium is usually notional times spread, paid annually. After a credit event, an auction or agreement sets recovery, and the seller pays notional minus recovery or delivers obligations for par. The CDS spread moves with credit quality, liquidity, the risk-free rate and counterparty risk; the spread between CDS and the same issuer's bonds is a basis, affected by the reference obligation, deliverable obligations and clearing rules. When reference obligations are not standardised, two CDS on the same entity may not offset perfectly; this is why ISDA promotes standard reference obligations [14].

Example. A $10 million CDS at 150 bp costs the protection buyer $150,000 a year; after two event-free years it has paid $300,000. If a credit event occurs with 40% recovery, cash settlement pays $6 million; the buyer's bond has also fallen by about $6 million, so the payout offsets most of the loss, while premium and basis remain costs. The seller collects $300,000 and pays $6 million on the event. If no event occurs, the buyer loses $300,000 and the seller gains $300,000. These are teaching assumptions, before upfront, funding and tax.

Boundary. A CDS covers only the credit events listed in its terms; a downgrade, wider spread or weaker liquidity need not trigger payment. Restructuring, reference-obligation substitution and deliverable obligations can split "the same issuer" into different risks. The buyer still bears the seller's counterparty risk; the seller bears gap and concentration risk. A CDS is not bond insurance, and a sovereign CDS spread is not a default probability.

Credit indices: a basket of CDS, rolling series and basis

Definition. CDX and iTraxx are tradable CDS indices: CDX covers North America and emerging markets, while iTraxx covers Europe, Asia, the Middle East and Africa. Rules select a basket of single-name CDS, a new series rolls every six months, and several tenors and tranches exist [6][7]. The index spread is a weighted combination of component spreads, not one issuer's credit spread.

Mechanism. A protection buyer pays the index spread and receives protection on credit events in the basket; a seller collects the spread and bears event payouts on any name. The index rolls every six months, so old and new series have different constituents and liquidity; credit events settle by auction and the affected name is removed or handled under the rules. An index can also be sliced into tranches that allocate the same basket's credit risk by priority [6]. When the index spread widens, a protection buyer earns mark-to-market gains but keeps paying carry.

Example. A $10 million five-year CDX investment-grade index at 60 bp costs a protection buyer $60,000 a year. If the spread widens 10 bp and risk duration is about 4.5, the buyer's mark-to-market gain is about 10 bp × 4.5 × $10 million = $45,000, but one year of carry is still $60,000; with an unchanged spread the carry is negative. If one name in the basket has a credit event, settlement follows the auction; the index does not lose everything on one default, nor is it immune to loss. These are teaching assumptions.

Boundary. An index is a basket, not a single-name CDS and not a bond fund; there is a basis between the index and a cash portfolio in constituents, tenor, seniority and liquidity, so the index spread cannot price the portfolio directly. New versus old series, tranching, auction rules and credit-event definitions all change the result; index liquidity does not mean every component can be hedged at any time.

Inflation tools: TIPS, breakevens and inflation swaps

Definition. TIPS are US Treasury inflation-linked bonds: principal is adjusted by CPI, coupon is paid on adjusted principal, and at maturity the original principal is repaid even if deflation has reduced adjusted principal [8]. Breakeven inflation is the nominal Treasury yield minus the TIPS real yield, the inflation rate at which the two returns equal [9]. An inflation swap exchanges a fixed inflation rate for realised CPI inflation, usually with net settlement at maturity; caps and floors truncate the payoff [10].

Mechanism. TIPS link principal and coupon to CPI, so the investor bears real-rate, liquidity, tax and CPI-definition risk rather than nominal purchasing-power risk. Breakeven is not a pure inflation forecast: it contains an inflation risk premium and a TIPS liquidity premium, so it can sit above or below true expectations [9]. An inflation swap nets the fixed and floating legs at maturity; the floating leg references non-seasonally adjusted CPI and has an indexation lag of about three months, so TIPS, zero-coupon inflation swaps and year-on-year inflation swaps do not cover the same period [10].

Example. A $100,000 TIPS with a 2% real coupon and 4% CPI over a year has adjusted principal of $104,000 and pays $2,080 of coupon; if CPI falls 4%, the coupon is $1,920 on $96,000 principal, but the maturity principal still has a $100,000 floor [8]. A nominal Treasury at 4.5% and a TIPS real yield at 2.0% imply a 2.5% breakeven; if realised inflation is 3.5%, the TIPS return beats the nominal Treasury by about one percentage point. A $10 million inflation swap receiving fixed 2.5% and paying realised 3.5% pays 1%, or $100,000; at 1.5% realised it receives $100,000. These are teaching assumptions, before tax, fees and lag months.

Boundary. Breakeven is not an inflation forecast and 2.5% is not a promise for future CPI; the inflation risk premium and liquidity premium drive a wedge [9]. TIPS have a deflation floor at maturity, but coupon falls with adjusted principal during the term, and inflation adjustments can be taxable in the year they accrue [8]. Inflation swaps carry counterparty, collateral, index-lag and CPI-definition risk; caps and floors truncate the payoff and can create opportunity cost when inflation lands outside the range.

Structured notes: principal protection, participation and barriers

Definition. A structured note is a security combining a debt instrument with embedded derivative terms; returns can be linked to equities, indices, rates, commodities or FX, and common terms include principal protection, participation, caps, knock-in barriers, autocalls and worst-of features [11]. Principal protection is usually protection at maturity and only as good as the issuer (and any guarantor); it is not deposit insurance and not a promise of par at any time [11].

Mechanism. The note uses a fixed-income component to carry principal and conditional coupons and uses the remainder to buy or sell options, producing participation, caps and barriers. The participation rate determines how much of the underlying gain reaches the investor; a cap cuts the excess; a knock-in changes the maturity payout after the underlying breaches a level; an autocall ends the note early on an observation date. The issuer sets the issue price above its estimated value, with the difference covering selling, structuring and hedging costs, so a secondary sale can start at a discount [11].

Example. A $100,000 three-year note has principal protection at maturity subject to the issuer, 50% participation, a 20% cap on the index gain and a 70% knock-in. If the index rises 30%, participation is calculated on the 20% cap, giving 10% and $110,000 at maturity. If the index falls 40% and breaches the 70% knock-in, the note may pass through the decline one-for-one and pay $60,000; if it falls 25% without breaching the barrier, it may still repay $100,000. If the note autocalls on the first observation date, the issuer redeems at the agreed price and the investor receives principal plus the conditional coupon but loses further participation. These are teaching assumptions, before fees and tax.

Boundary. "100% principal protection" holds only at maturity and only if the issuer performs; early sale, issuer default, worst-of terms or a knock-in can all put principal below par [11]. A rating is a credit rating of the issuer or a tranche, not a guarantee of the underlying, the barrier or liquidity; a worst-of note pays on the worst-performing member of a basket, not the average [12]. A structured note is not a deposit and not ownership of the reference asset; issue price, secondary price and estimated value are three different numbers.

CFDs and path dependence: margin, financing and close-out

Definition. A contract for difference (CFD) gives the holder long or short exposure to the price, level or value of an underlying and settles in cash; ESMA's definition distinguishes it from options, futures, swaps and forward rate agreements [13]. The investor does not own the underlying, settles the price difference with the broker or counterparty, and maintains the position through margin and financing terms.

Mechanism. CFD leverage makes notional exposure much larger than margin: a small adverse move can consume most of the equity, and the broker closes positions when margin falls below the maintenance level; holding overnight also incurs or earns financing, with spread and slippage on top [13]. Path dependence means the result depends not only on the final price but on the route: a temporary decline can trigger a close-out even if price later returns to the starting point; a gap can jump past a stop; autocalls, knock-ins and worst-of notes also write the path into their terms. A CFD's terminal payoff looks linear, but margin and financing make it path-dependent in practice.

Example. An account has $10,000 equity and buys a $100,000 index CFD at 10:1 leverage. A 5% fall loses $5,000 and leaves $5,000 equity; a 10% fall loses $10,000 and wipes out the account; if the broker closes at the maintenance margin, the account can be shut before a rebound. Financing at 5% on $100,000 is about $5,000 a year, half the account equity, before spread and slippage. If price first falls 12%, triggers a close-out, then rebounds 15%, the investor is already out. These are teaching assumptions, before tax and the exact overnight-financing convention.

Boundary. A CFD is not share ownership: there is no voting right, dividend or shareholder claim, the counterparty is the broker, and the investor bears broker credit risk. Leverage caps, close-out thresholds, negative-balance protection and sales restrictions vary by jurisdiction; the EU's 30:1 to 2:1 caps are not a global rule [13]. Negative-balance protection is not universal, and a CFD is not a substitute for long-term savings.

The common test for all seven is three questions: what risk is exchanged, whether settlement is principal, net or margin, and which risk remains in the cash asset or on the balance sheet. The same name does not mean the same terms; reading the documents matters more than memorising labels.

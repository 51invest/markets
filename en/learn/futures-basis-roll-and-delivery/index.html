Futures Basis, Rolls and Delivery

The gap between futures and spot, the cost of rolling and delivery rules jointly determine hedging and holding returns; a futures price move alone is not enough.

Definition

Basis is usually written as spot minus futures: basis = spot − futures. Near delivery, eligible spot and futures prices converge through delivery arbitrage, but grade, location and costs mean convergence is not exact equality. A roll closes the expiring contract and buys a later one; for a long, a higher later price means paying a positive roll cost.

Intuition

Basis is an exchange rate between spot and futures: spot is the price for taking delivery today, futures is the price for a later delivery month, and the gap contains storage, insurance, financing, transport and spot tightness. A roll is like swapping a ticket for the next train: you stay on the journey but pay or receive the difference. Many hedgers watch only the futures move and forget that what they actually lock is 'spot price + basis + roll cost'. If basis strengthens by CNY 10 while futures fall by CNY 8, the hedger's effective procurement cost may still rise.

Mechanism

Carry consists of storage, insurance, financing and shrinkage; convenience yield is the benefit of holding spot for uninterrupted production or sales. In theory, futures ≈ spot + carry − convenience yield. When futures exceed spot by more than carry, a cash-and-carry trade sells futures, buys spot and delivers; a backwardated market can reflect tight spot or a high convenience yield. A roll replaces old-contract price risk with new-contract risk while locking in or paying the calendar spread; margin and price limits make roll timing matter more than the theoretical spread [2][3].

Worked example

Suppose spot is CNY 100, the three-month future is CNY 104, three-month storage and insurance is CNY 6, financing is CNY 4 and delivery cost is CNY 2. Total carry is CNY 12 while futures are only CNY 4 higher, so buying spot and delivering loses CNY 8; if tight spot creates a CNY 8 convenience yield, the theoretical gap is exactly CNY 4. For a long hedger, the old contract expires at CNY 104 and the later one costs CNY 108, a CNY 4 roll cost; if the later contract falls to CNY 102, the roll gains CNY 2. These are teaching assumptions and exclude tax and margin funding.

Second worked example: cash-and-carry and delivery

Suppose spot is CNY 100, the three-month future is CNY 106, storage and insurance are CNY 7, financing is CNY 3, delivery cost is CNY 1 and convenience yield is CNY 2. Theoretical futures = 100 + 7 + 3 + 1 − 2 = CNY 109; the actual CNY 106 is CNY 3 below theory, so buying spot and delivering does not pay. If futures rise to CNY 112, do the reverse: sell futures, buy spot, pay CNY 11 of carry and delivery, and deliver at CNY 112 for a gross profit of 112 − 100 − 11 = CNY 1/tonne. If margin ties up CNY 10 and funding costs CNY 0.5, net profit is only CNY 0.5/tonne; a CNY 2 headline gap may not cover operations after friction. These are teaching assumptions.

Boundary and mistakes

First, keep the basis sign convention consistent or a 'stronger basis' will be read backwards. Second, deliverable grade and location differ, so a futures price cannot be compared with any spot quote. Third, a roll is not a free switch: bid-ask, commissions and margin funding all count. Fourth, a 100% hedge ratio is not automatically safe; mismatched cash-flow timing, quantity and contract multiplier leave residual risk. Fifth, futures reflect expectations and funding, not a forecast of future spot.

Failure modes

The first failure is a reversed basis sign. Some write 'futures minus spot', others 'spot minus futures'; the same numbers then produce opposite conclusions. A team must fix the convention and label it on every chart. The second is using the wrong spot: the deliverable grade, location and VAT treatment may differ from the actual purchase contract, so apparent convergence leaves a settlement gap. The third is ignoring margin liquidity: when the near-versus-later spread widens during a roll, a margin call can force an exit at the worst time. The fourth is a hedge quantity that does not match the contract multiplier, leaving residual exposure. The fifth is treating the exchange futures price as a forecast of future spot; futures reflect current deliverable supply, demand and funding.

Three checks

First, are the basis formula and sign written in the text and captions so a reader can recompute with the same numbers? Second, are grade, location, tax and delivery costs aligned between spot and futures, and is any convergence gap explained? Third, are roll cost, margin funding and financing included in the effective price? Missing one of these overstates the hedge's effectiveness.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

Basis is easily confused with calendar spread and processing spread. Basis is spot minus futures; the calendar spread is near-month futures minus later-month futures; the processing spread is product price minus feedstock cost. They answer different questions. A stronger basis can come from tight spot or falling futures; a stronger calendar spread can come from scarce storage or a funding squeeze. A counterexample is 'negative basis is a risk-free arbitrage': negative basis may reflect convenience yield, delivery limits or warehouse costs, and cash-and-carry need not pay. Another is 'futures must converge to spot': near delivery, eligible spot and futures converge, but grade, location, transport and warehouse receipts keep a gap. Before comparing analyses, fix the sign and definition, or 'stronger' will be read backwards.

Scope and limits

This basis framework applies to futures with standard contracts, clear deliverable grades and measurable warehouse and transport costs. If spot grades vary widely, delivery capacity is limited, or liquidity concentrates in a few months, basis is distorted by grade, location and squeezes. It does not apply to cash-settled contracts without physical delivery, nor to using basis as a directional forecast. Align grade, location and tax first, then cross-check with open interest, warehouse receipts and calendar spreads; a one-day basis jump may only be a contract roll.

Finally, when comparing basis across products, align quotation units, FX and tax; if spot contracts carry quality premiums, convert to the standard grade before subtracting futures, or grade differences hide the basis direction.

Intertemporal comparison must separate delivery months from the front contract; basis before and after a roll is not continuous, and different multipliers or quotation units must be converted first.

Also keep reproducible intermediate records and scripts.

What the product is: rights and cash-flow structure

A futures contract is a standardised obligation: the long agrees to receive the underlying at a fixed price, grade and delivery location, while the short agrees to deliver. What is really traded is the package of price, basis, margin and delivery rules, not an abstract price chart.

Before expiry, the long can close, roll or enter delivery; the short can close, roll or deliver. Both post margin and accept daily mark-to-market. Basis is usually spot minus futures; reverse the sign and “strengthening” becomes the opposite conclusion.

Participants, trading, holding and exit

Participants include the exchange, clearing house, brokers, hedgers, directional traders, warehouse receipt holders and delivery warehouses. The exchange sets contract and delivery rules, the clearing house stands in the middle, brokers manage client margin, and warehouses determine receipts and location premiums.

Trading starts with an open position, runs through daily mark-to-market and rolls, and ends by close-out, physical delivery or cash settlement. Holding costs include storage, insurance, funding and delivery fees. Exit analysis must include the futures leg, the physical leg and margin funding, not just the screen spread.

Costs, risks and terms and documents

Costs: Commissions, bid-ask spread, margin funding, storage and insurance, delivery fees and roll slippage all belong in the effective price. A 2 CNY spread may leave only 0.5 CNY after frictions.

Risks: Basis risk, roll risk, margin-call liquidity risk, grade and location mismatch, delivery failure and contract liquidity. A hedge is not safe just because it is 100%: quantity, timing and contract multiplier mismatches leave residual exposure.

Terms: Key documents are the exchange contract specification, margin and delivery rules, warehouse receipts and grade premium tables. Before trading, check deliverable grade, location, tax treatment, last trading day and cash-settlement terms.

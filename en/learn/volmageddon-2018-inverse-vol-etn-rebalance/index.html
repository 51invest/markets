Volmageddon 2018: Inverse Volatility ETNs and Rebalancing

On 5 February 2018, the VIX rose from 17.31 at the prior close to 37.32, about 116% in a day; XIV fell about 96% and triggered liquidation. Put daily rebalancing, inverse exposure and the acceleration clause on one chain to see why a “low-volatility trade” could self-amplify.

Background and timeline

2017 was a low-volatility year, with the S&P 500 setting long streaks without a 2% daily fall. The calm made “short volatility” look like steady income: as long as the VIX stayed low, inverse volatility products could earn. XIV and SVXY were the two largest inverse volatility ETNs/ETFs, gaining inverse exposure by shorting near-month VIX futures[4].

On 5 February 2018, the S&P 500 fell about 4.1% and the Dow about 1,175 points; the VIX rose from 17.31 at the prior close to 37.32, about 115.6%, one of the largest one-day jumps on record. XIV’s indicative value fell from $108.37 to about $4.22 after hours, a drop of about 96%; SVXY fell about 91%[4]. XIV’s prospectus said that if the intraday indicative value fell below 20% of the prior close, the issuer could accelerate liquidation.

The 5 February fall triggered that clause; XIV’s last trading day was 20 February, after which it terminated. SVXY kept trading but at a fraction of its former size. In one day, the low-volatility trade turned from “steady income” into “tail risk”[4].

Information available and actors

The main actors were issuers of inverse volatility products (including Credit Suisse), market makers, retail and institutional holders, the VIX futures clearing house, and index providers. The product promised the “daily inverse” return of VIX futures, not a long-horizon inverse exposure; the word “daily” is the key to everything. The issuer’s decision is constrained by the product terms.

After each close, the product must reset exposure to the settlement price of VIX futures; if futures rise, the inverse product’s value falls, and to keep a fixed inverse exposure it must buy more VIX futures. Buying pushes futures higher, lowers the product’s value again and forces another purchase. The rebalancing is mechanical, not a manager’s judgement.

Investor decisions were shaped by the “steady income” narrative. In 2017 the value rose steadily and investors mistook “low volatility” for “low risk”; but the tail risk of an inverse product is concentrated in a volatility spike. The liquidation line protects the issuer, not the investor, from losses beyond principal.

Outcome and the number chain

Key numbers: VIX 17.31 to 37.32, about 115.6%; XIV $108.37 to $4.22, about 96%; SVXY about 91%; XIV and SVXY combined short about 280,000 VIX futures, about 280 million vega; the acceleration trigger at 20% of the prior close; XIV’s last day 20 February[4]. Chain them: VIX rises → inverse product value falls → rebalancing buys VIX futures → futures rise → value falls again → liquidation. The 280,000 contracts could be absorbed in normal times, but when volatility spikes and liquidity thins, the rebalancing purchases can push the market further.

280 million vega means about $280 million of loss for each 1-point VIX move. That is exposure, not total assets; as value falls, exposure becomes larger relative to value and rebalancing pressure grows. The leverage is built into the product structure, not borrowed money.

Mechanism

The first layer is daily rebalancing. The inverse product resets exposure each day, buying futures when volatility rises and selling when it falls; this “buy high, sell low” is positive feedback in a spike. The second is liquidity.

VIX futures depth is limited; concentrated buying pushes prices up and triggers rebalancing in other products. The third is the acceleration clause. The 20% trigger forces liquidation in an extreme fall, and the liquidation itself adds buying pressure and amplifies volatility[4].

The three layers create “volatility rises → products buy futures → futures rise → volatility rises”. The loop is not manipulation but a mechanical response of product rules in an extreme market. The more popular and larger the product, the stronger the loop; when size exceeds market depth, the low-volatility trade becomes systemic.

Counterfactuals and alternative explanations

First, without leveraged inverse products, and with only cash-held long volatility, a VIX spike would not trigger mechanical rebalancing, and the positive feedback would be much smaller. Second, with monthly rather than daily rebalancing, exposure would adjust more slowly and tail risk would be lower, at the cost of greater tracking error. Third, with a lower acceleration trigger, XIV might not have liquidated that day, but its value would keep falling, investors would lose more and issuer risk would rise.

Fourth, with deeper VIX futures liquidity, rebalancing purchases could be absorbed, price impact would be smaller and the loop less violent.

Limits to generalization

The case can show that daily-rebalancing inverse products create positive feedback in a volatility spike; acceleration clauses speed product exit in extreme markets; when product size exceeds market depth, a low-volatility trade can become systemic. It cannot show that all volatility products carry the same risk; that the 116% VIX move was caused by products alone; that acceleration clauses are unfair; or that the 2018 structure applies directly to 2020 or 2024 products. Terms, size and liquidity differ.

Decision review

From a product-design view, “daily inverse” is a specific contractual promise, not a long-term strategy. Daily resetting gives investors the inverse return of one day but turns long-run compounding into path dependence; in a volatility spike, rebalancing mechanically buys futures and amplifies losses. Designers knew the risk, but the terms transferred tail risk to investors.

From an investor’s view, the steady value in 2017 created a “low risk” illusion. Low volatility is not low risk; when volatility is historically low, the tail risk of a spike is larger. An investor who looks only at past returns and not at product terms and rebalancing can mistake “steady income” for “safe asset”.

From an exchange and clearing view, when product size exceeds market depth, rebalancing becomes a systemic event. Clearing houses need stress tests for extreme volatility, and exchanges need to assess whether product terms trigger large same-direction trades at once. Product innovation must consider not only single-product risk but correlation and concentration across products.

Index Construction: Free Float and Benchmark Selection

An index is not the market itself but a replicable set of eligibility, weighting, capping and rebalancing rules; benchmark choice changes attribution.

ModuleMKT-M03 · 指数与基准
LevelFoundation
Read7 min · 18 sections

Definition

An index is a statistical portfolio selected and weighted by public rules. A free-float adjustment counts only tradable shares and excludes government, founder, strategic and restricted holdings, so non-tradable market value is not treated as investable weight. Capping limits a single constituent or the largest few; rebalancing changes constituents and weights quarterly, semi-annually or annually. A price index reflects prices only, while a total-return index reinvests dividends; the two are not directly comparable.

Intuition

An index is a public recipe: which stocks are included, how much each weighs and how often the basket is refreshed are all written in the methodology. Free-float market cap counts only shares genuinely available to trade, excluding founders, governments, strategic holders and long lock-ups; using full market cap puts untradable shares into the weights and tilts the index toward companies with concentrated ownership. Benchmark selection is choosing a ruler: if your portfolio is restricted to a market or segment, the ruler must cover the same universe, otherwise 'beating the benchmark' may just be a style exposure.

Mechanism

Construction usually has four steps: define the eligible market and security types; screen by free-float market value and liquidity; weight by free-float value or equally; then apply single-stock and sector caps and rebalance periodically. Corporate actions such as splits, issuance, mergers and dividends change value and weights, and the provider adjusts by rule. More transparent rules are easier to replicate, but providers still exercise judgment on free float, foreign ownership limits and special securities. Benchmark choice defines the active manager's opportunity set and attribution [1][2][3].

Worked example

Suppose three companies have total market values of CNY 10.0, 5.0 and 3.0 billion, with free-float ratios of 40%, 80% and 100%. Free-float values are 4.0, 4.0 and 3.0 billion, totalling 11.0 billion, so weights are 36.4%, 36.4% and 27.3%. With a 30% single-stock cap, the first is cut from 36.4% to 30%, and the excess 6.4 points are redistributed pro rata, giving about 30%, 39.6% and 30.4%. If the second also breaches the cap, iterate again. These are teaching assumptions and exclude liquidity screens and buffers.

Second worked example: full versus free-float weights

Suppose an index holds three stocks: A trades at CNY 10 with 100 shares and a 60% free float; B at CNY 20 with 50 shares and an 80% free float; C at CNY 5 with 200 shares and a 50% free float. Full market caps are CNY 1,000 each, so equal weights are one-third; free-float caps are CNY 600, 800 and 500, totalling CNY 1,900, for weights of 31.6%, 42.1% and 26.3%. If A rises 20% while B and C are unchanged, the full-cap index rises 6.67%; the free-float index rises only 600 × 20% / 1,900 = 6.32%. The 0.35-point gap comes from A's lower free float. At rebalancing, weight changes also trigger trading costs; if A's free float rises from 60% to 70%, its weight increases and funds must buy, but that buying can itself move the price. These are teaching assumptions.

Boundary and mistakes

First, an index is not a tradable portfolio: it ignores trading costs, taxes, custody and cash drag. Second, free float is an estimate, providers can revise it, and historical weights change. Third, caps create turnover and tracking error at rebalancing; the cap is not the only effect. Fourth, the gap between price and total-return indexes comes from dividends, so price-index return is not investor return. Fifth, a benchmark can be chosen, but choosing one does not change actual risk; it must match the mandate and constraints.

Failure modes

The first failure is treating full-cap weights as investable weights, ignoring lock-ups, government holdings and foreign-ownership caps. The second is using stale free-float data: shareholder changes, buybacks and lock-up expiries alter weights, and index providers usually adjust quarterly or on events. The third is ignoring corporate actions: splits, mergers, dividends and share issuance change price and share count; without a divisor adjustment the index jumps artificially. The fourth is using a whole-market index to evaluate a sector portfolio: different universes and styles make excess return unattributable. The fifth is treating a backtest as future return; constituents and weights are reconstructed with today's rules, creating survivorship and rule-drift bias.

Three checks

First, does the index cover the same market, size segment and style as the portfolio? Second, are weights based on full or free-float market cap, and does the float definition include strategic holdings and foreign caps? Third, are rebalancing frequency, divisor adjustments and trading costs included? These three determine whether you compare with the same ruler or two rulers with different scales.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

Price indices are easily confused with total-return and net-total-return indices. A price index reflects price moves only, without dividends; a total-return index reinvests dividends and compounds higher; a net-total-return index may deduct withholding tax. The three are not directly comparable as returns. A counterexample is 'a higher free float is always better': a higher float raises investable weight, but at rebalancing funds must buy, the buying can push prices up, and the next rebalancing may sell; for long-term investors, trading costs and tax can offset the 'more investable' benefit. Another is 'index history is market history': constituents and weights are reconstructed with today's rules, and delistings, mergers and rule changes are hidden by survivorship bias. Compare definition first, weights second, rebalancing cost third.

Scope and limits

This index-construction framework applies to equity or bond indices with explicit selection rules, free-float definitions, rebalancing frequency and weighting. If the index is backfilled by a vendor, constituents change frequently, or free float embeds strategic-holding judgments, historical comparability falls. It does not apply to treating a price index as total return, nor to cost comparisons that ignore rebalancing and tax. Read the methodology, then weights and rebalancing dates, and cross-check price against total-return indices; an index level is not a replicable portfolio return.

Finally, cross-index comparison should align currency, dividend treatment and rebalancing dates, or the return gap may come from methodology rather than the market.

Also disclose the index provider and version.

What the product is: rights and cash-flow structure

An index is a public rule that combines a set of securities into one benchmark number: it specifies the universe, eligibility, weighting, rebalancing frequency and corporate-action treatment. Free-float market cap weighting counts only shares available to trade, not locked-up shares as investable supply.

The index itself is not a tradable asset but a calculation rule and licence; investors get exposure through index funds, ETFs, futures or swaps. The index provider owns the method and trademark, and the fund licenses it to replicate or sample-track.

Participants, trading, holding and exit

Participants include index providers, exchanges, ETF issuers, market makers, custodians, data vendors and investors. The provider maintains the method, the ETF issuer creates shares, market makers supply secondary liquidity, and custodians hold assets.

Index exposure can come from ETF creation/redemption, futures, options, swaps or direct replication. Holding requires handling rebalances, corporate actions, dividends and tracking error; exit differs between selling an ETF, redeeming shares and closing derivatives.

Costs, risks and terms and documents

Costs: Management fees, creation/redemption fees, bid-ask spread, tracking error, rebalancing trading costs, licence fees and taxes. A low expense ratio is not a low total cost; sampling and illiquid constituents can widen deviations.

Risks: Tracking error, rebalancing risk, concentration, liquidity, methodology changes, licence termination and benchmark manipulation. Free-float adjustments are imperfect; data delays and foreign ownership limits also affect investable weights.

Terms: Key documents are the index methodology, licence agreement, ETF prospectus, rebalance announcements and data-licence terms. Check the universe, free-float factors, weight caps, buffers and corporate-action rules.

Answer key
  1. Total market value includes non-tradable shares; weighting by it assigns investors weights they cannot buy. Free float counts only the tradable portion.
  2. 40/110 = 36.36%, 40/110 = 36.36%, 30/110 = 27.27%.
  3. Management fees, commissions, bid-ask, taxes, cash drag, sampling and rebalancing turnover all create tracking difference.

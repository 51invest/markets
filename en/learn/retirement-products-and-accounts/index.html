Retirement Products and Accounts

Retirement products have three layers: account rules, product structure and decumulation; a change in any layer changes final income and risk.

ModuleMKT-M19 · 养老产品与账户
LevelFoundation
Read8 min · 12 sections

Definition

Retirement products and accounts have three layers: account rules, the product structure inside the account and the decumulation method. The account sets contribution limits, tax treatment, eligible investments and withdrawal conditions; the product sets risk and return; decumulation determines whether income lasts[1][2][3][4]. Conflating account and product misreads tax benefits, investor protection and risk bearing.

Intuition

The same “retirement account” can hold a target-date fund, a balanced fund, an annuity or a cash-management product with very different risks. The account is the container; the product is what sits inside. Tax law and regulation govern the container; fund contracts and insurance terms govern the product.

Formal expression

Account mechanics

Account mechanics include contribution limits, pre- or post-tax treatment, employer matching, investment choice, loans and withdrawal rules. Tax treatment is usually set at the account layer; the product layer sets return and fees. Cross-border or cross-state cases also depend on residency and tax treaties[2].

Personal accounts and occupational plans

A personal retirement account is opened and funded by the individual, such as an IRA or a personal pension account; an occupational retirement plan is sponsored by an employer, such as a 401(k), 403(b), 457(b), an enterprise annuity or an occupational annuity. Occupational plans may offer employer matching, vesting, loans and hardship withdrawals, while personal accounts usually have no employer match and are more portable[2][5].

Occupational plans also split into DB (defined benefit) and DC (defined contribution). A DB plan promises a benefit based on a service and salary formula, with investment and longevity risk borne mainly by the employer and plan; a DC plan records an individual account balance, with investment risk borne mainly by the individual. Treating “having an occupational plan” as “having a DB guarantee” misreads a DC balance as a promised pension[5].

Teaching assumption: a CNY 10,000 monthly salary with a 6% personal contribution (CNY 600) and a 50% employer match capped at 6% (CNY 300) gives CNY 900 a month; at 5% annual return over 30 years, the employer match alone grows to about CNY 250,000. Leaving before vesting may forfeit the match, while a personal account has no such match.

Boundary: an account name is not a product; one employer may offer both DB and DC; vesting, early leaving, cross-border transfer and creditor protection differ by jurisdiction. Comparisons must state tax jurisdiction, contribution limits, employer matching, vesting and withdrawal rules, not just the account label.

Product structure

A target-date fund lowers equity exposure along a preset glide path; a balanced fund maintains a relatively stable allocation; an annuity converts accumulation into lifetime or term income; an income fund emphasises cash flow. Risk budget, fees and rebalancing rules determine long-run outcomes[3][4].

Glide path and sequence risk

A glide path lowers the equity weight (w_s(t)) with age; if equity is 80% at age 40 and 40% at 65, a linear path gives about (80%-(40/25)\times15=56%) at 55. If equities fall 30%, a 56% equity allocation loses about 16.8% of the portfolio, while a 60% allocation loses about 18%[3]. The glide path assumes a clear retirement date and predictable longevity; if retirement is delayed or longevity is longer, the equity share may be too low and long-run real income insufficient.

Sequence-of-returns risk means the same cumulative return in a different order produces a different withdrawal outcome. Teaching assumption: CNY 1,000,000 with CNY 50,000 withdrawn at the start of each year. Path A starts with −10% then +11.11%: CNY 950,000 falls to CNY 855,000, a CNY 50,000 withdrawal leaves CNY 805,000, which rises to about CNY 894,400. Path B starts with +11.11% then −10%: CNY 950,000 rises to about CNY 1,055,500, a CNY 50,000 withdrawal leaves about CNY 1,005,500, which falls to about CNY 905,000. The two-year endpoints differ by about CNY 10,600. A decline early in retirement forces withdrawals to lock in part of the loss, making later recovery harder.

Boundary: a “to” fund reaches its final allocation at the target date, while a “through” fund keeps de-risking after it; neither is universally better. Target-date funds may be active or passive and use different assets and fees, so the label cannot replace the glide path and risk budget.

Decumulation

Decumulation includes lump sums, scheduled withdrawals, annuitisation and hybrids. Withdrawal rules affect longevity risk, bequest goals and flexibility; a lump sum leaves longevity risk with the individual, while an annuity transfers part of it to an insurer[4]. Product rules also set fees, guarantees, liquidity and the conversion into income: annuities carry surrender charges, insurer credit exposure and annuitisation terms, target-date funds carry a glide path and fees, and funds have subscription, redemption and net-asset rules; account rules add contribution limits, tax and withdrawal restrictions[1][3][4].

Account tax and withdrawal order

The advantage of a tax-advantaged account comes from the timing gap between deductible contributions, tax-deferred growth and taxed withdrawals, not from “never paying tax”. Teaching example: with a 20% contribution tax rate and a 30% withdrawal tax rate, CNY 100 of pre-tax pay grows 5x to CNY 500 inside the account and leaves CNY 350 after withdrawal tax. A taxable account pays 20% first, leaving CNY 80, which grows 5x to CNY 400; if capital gains are deferred and 20% is paid on the CNY 320 gain at sale, the result is CNY 336. Long deferral can still win even when the withdrawal rate is higher; if the contribution rate is low, the withdrawal rate is high and the horizon is short, the conclusion can reverse[2].

A withdrawal order usually satisfies required minimum distributions first, then fills tax brackets using taxable, tax-deferred and tax-free accounts; this is not a fixed formula, because liquidity, bequests, charitable giving and cross-border tax also matter. Early withdrawals may face penalties and surtaxes, loans interrupt compounding, and annuitisation transfers part of longevity risk to an insurer[1][4].

Boundary: tax rates, limits, penalties and minimum-distribution rules change by jurisdiction and year; this text gives mechanisms and teaching assumptions only. Current IRS, DOL or local tax rules govern[2][5].

Conditions and limits

  1. Account comparison must state tax jurisdiction, contribution limits and withdrawal rules; the same name can mean different things across countries.
  2. Target-date evaluation must examine glide path, underlying assets and fees, not the label alone.
  3. Annuity value depends on interest rates, mortality and insurer credit; guarantees and inflation adjustment determine real purchasing power.
  4. Withdrawal strategy must balance longevity risk, liquidity needs and bequest goals; a single “4% rule” is not universal.

Worked example

Suppose a 30-year-old contributes 10,000 yuan a year at 5% annual return until age 60, for 30 years; these are teaching assumptions.

Item Value
Annual contribution 10,000 yuan
Years 30
Annual return 5%
30-year annuity future-value factor (1.05^30 - 1) / 0.05 = 66.439
Retirement balance 10,000 × 66.439 = 664,390 yuan

At a 4% withdrawal, the first-year amount is about 26,576 yuan; with 3% inflation and 5% return, real purchasing power grows only modestly. The balance depends on contributions, returns and time; sustainability also depends on fees, taxes, longevity and the market sequence.

Common mistakes

A target-date fund ends at retirement. Retirees may live several decades; whether the glide path continues or switches to income needs to be specified.

An account is a product. The account is a tax and regulatory container; the product carries risk and return, with different rule sources.

Knowledge-point review

personal and workplace retirement vehicles

Definition: Personal retirement vehicles include individual pension accounts, IRAs and private pensions, while workplace tools include occupational and enterprise annuities and DC/DB plans.

Mechanism: Vehicles use tax breaks, employer matches and investment choices; DC puts investment risk on individuals, DB on employers or plans, and workplace plans have vesting and regulation.

Example: For example, an individual pension account defers tax, an enterprise annuity is funded by employer and employee, and a DB plan promises a pension.

Boundary: Retirement vehicles are not ordinary accounts; lockups, withdrawal rules, fees and tax benefits differ, DC individuals bear longevity and investment risk, and DB carries employer solvency risk.

target-date risks

Definition: Target-date funds set a glide path by retirement year, reducing risk assets as the date approaches.

Mechanism: Glide-path design, savings rate, fees and post-retirement withdrawals determine outcomes; providers define end-point risk differently, some keep de-risking and others hold equity.

Example: For example, a 2050 fund holds more equity when young and adds bonds and cash near retirement.

Boundary: Target date is not a guarantee; too conservative loses to inflation, too aggressive hurts near retirement, sequence, longevity and fee risk remain with the individual, and one glide path does not fit everyone.

account mechanics, product rules and decumulation

Definition: Account mechanics cover contributions, investment, vesting, fees and tax; product rules cover eligibility, guarantees, payout and conversion; decumulation includes lump sums, annuities and instalments.

Mechanism: Tax benefits, limits and lockups shape saving; product rules define guarantees and risk; payout style affects longevity, inflation and legacy, and annuitisation pools longevity risk but sacrifices liquidity.

Example: For example, an individual pension account has annual limits and rules for withdrawal or annuity purchase, and enterprise annuities vest over time.

Boundary: Accounts and payouts are not detail; rule changes, fees, taxes and longevity affect outcomes, annuitisation is not always optimal, lump sums can deplete, and guarantees carry issuer credit risk.

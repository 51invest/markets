Real Estate and REITs

Real estate can be owned directly or through REITs; a REIT's tax status depends on distribution, shareholder and asset tests, and listed and non-traded shares differ sharply in liquidity.

What the product is

A REIT is a company that owns and typically operates income-producing real estate or related assets, such as offices, apartments, hotels, warehouses and mortgages. U.S. tax rules let a qualifying REIT deduct from corporate taxable income all of the dividends it pays to shareholders, so many REITs distribute all of their taxable income and owe no corporate tax. Qualification requires distributing at least 90% of taxable income annually and meeting the shareholder tests (at least 100 shareholders after the first year as a REIT, and no more than 50% of shares held by five or fewer individuals during the last half of the taxable year), the asset test (at least 75% of total assets in real estate assets and cash) and the income tests (at least 75% of gross income from real estate sources, and at least 95% once dividends and other interest are included) (SEC, 2011; IRS, 2025). Direct ownership means holding title and bearing the maintenance, financing and sale responsibilities.

Rights, obligations and cash flows

Role Right or obligation Cash flow Timing or condition
Shareholder Holds shares, elects directors, receives declared dividends Dividends Declared by the board, not guaranteed
REIT Operates properties, pays expenses and interest Rent, operating costs, financing costs Ongoing
Tenant Uses space under a lease Rent Under the lease
Mortgage REIT Originates or buys real estate loans Interest, principal Under the loan contract

Shareholders own shares, not the properties. Distributions by non-traded REITs can exceed their funds from operations and may be paid with offering proceeds and borrowings; a REIT is not a pass-through entity, so it cannot pass tax losses to investors (SEC, 2011).

Example (hypothetical)

A REIT has $100 million of taxable income (excluding net capital gain) and distributes at least $90 million, retaining $10 million. An investor buys 1,000 shares at $25 each, receives $1.20 per share in dividends, and the shares end the year at $23, so the total return is (23,000 + 1,200 - 25,000) / 25,000 = -3.2%; the 4.8% dividend yield did not offset the price decline. Hypothetical, before fees and taxes.

Under the IRS rules the dividends-paid deduction must reach 90% of taxable income excluding net capital gains and specified adjustments, the section 856(c) gross income and diversification tests must be met, and a REIT generally must use a calendar tax year [2].

At the property level two more measures (also hypothetical, not market data): a property with effective gross income 1,200,000 and operating expenses 300,000 has net operating income (NOI) = 1,200,000 − 300,000 = 900,000; bought at 15,000,000, the capitalisation rate = 900,000 ÷ 15,000,000 = 6.0%. A cap rate uses only first-year NOI and purchase price: it is neither a holding-period return nor a financed one. With 60% debt, borrowing 9,000,000 against 6,000,000 of equity and a 4% loan rate, interest is 360,000 and the return on equity = (900,000 − 360,000) ÷ 6,000,000 = 9.0%, above the 6.0% unlevered cap rate with risk magnified in the same direction. NOI moves with vacancy, tenant concentration and operating costs and the cap rate moves with rates and risk appetite, so it cannot be inverted into a future price, nor borrowed from someone else's asset to yours.

Participants and venues

Shareholders, the board of directors or trustees, property managers, tenants, lenders, brokers and external managers participate. Listed REITs trade on exchanges; non-traded REITs are sold through brokers and typically have no employees of their own, being managed by a third party under a management contract. Mortgage REITs are generally more leveraged than equity REITs and often use derivatives and other hedging techniques (SEC, 2011). Regulation involves the SEC for registration and disclosure and the IRS for REIT qualification and tax treatment.

Trading, holding and exit

Listed shares trade like stocks and may trade above or below net asset value; redemption programs for non-traded REITs vary by company and are typically very limited, so investors may have to wait for a liquidity event such as a listing of the shares or a liquidation of the company's assets (SEC, 2011). Selling direct property requires a buyer and title transfer; REIT funds and ETFs diversify but trade intraday.

Costs

Buying or selling listed shares costs the same brokerage commission as any other publicly traded stock, plus the bid-ask spread; non-traded REITs commonly charge 9% to 10% of the investment upfront for broker-dealer commissions and other offering costs, plus ongoing acquisition, management and operating expenses, and back-end fees may also apply (SEC, 2011). The underlying real estate also bears operating, maintenance, insurance, property tax and interest costs.

Risks

This risk list is this article's own framing of how REIT claims and structures can go wrong: lower rents and occupancy, higher interest rates, leverage, tenant and geographic concentration, opaque valuations and redemption limits, manager-shareholder conflicts, and failure of a tax test. Only the point that mortgage REITs tend to be more leveraged and use hedging techniques comes from the registered source [1]; the other items are this article's taxonomy and carry no citation.

Terms and documents

REIT status is a set of tests, not a label: the vehicle must distribute at least 90% of taxable income as dividends each year and satisfy holder-count, concentration and asset and income tests [1]. The IRS filing instructions tie the dividends-paid deduction to 90% of taxable income (excluding net capital gains and prescribed adjustments) together with the gross-income and diversification tests, and generally require a calendar tax year [2]. Four things follow for a reader: whether distributions match the rate and frequency stated in the documents; how a non-listed vehicle handles redemptions, which the SEC describes as usually very limited, with front-end loads commonly at 9%–10% [1]; the leverage and disclosure of a mortgage REIT [1]; and, for listed shares, that the relation between market price and underlying net asset value is set by the fund documents and trading mechanism rather than by the product name. The governing text is the offering document, annual report and tax filing basis. Terms differ materially by country and product, so this section lists what to check instead of standing in for the documents.

Comparison and boundaries

Instrument Legal structure Source of cash flow Liquidity Main risks
Direct property Direct title Rent, appreciation Low Local market, maintenance, leverage
Listed REIT Company or trust shares Rent, mortgage interest, dividends Relatively high Price volatility, rates, leverage
Non-traded REIT Shares not listed on an exchange Rent, dividends Very low Valuation, fees, redemption limits
REIT fund or ETF Fund shares Dividends from a basket of REITs Relatively high Market, tracking error, fees

A REIT is not a deposit, does not guarantee dividends or principal, and is not property ownership. Products named "real estate" must be distinguished by legal structure and cash flow; offering documents control the terms.

Knowledge-point review

residential, commercial, industrial, logistics and specialist property

Definition: Real estate spans residential, commercial, industrial, logistics and specialist property, with different cash flows and risks.

Mechanism: Housing depends on demographics, credit and rent rules; commercial on retail and office demand; logistics on e-commerce and trade.

Example: For example, e-commerce growth lifts warehouse demand, while remote work weakens some office rents.

Boundary: Real estate is not one asset; location, leases, conversion and cycles differ, and specialist property faces technology and regulation shifts.

land and timberland

Definition: Land is a location and use right, and timberland is land producing timber and ecosystem services.

Mechanism: Land value depends on planning, transport, population and scarcity; timberland earns from wood, carbon and recreation over long growth cycles.

Example: For example, urban expansion raises peri-urban land value, while timberland generates cash from harvests and carbon credits.

Boundary: Land is not an ordinary good; property rights, planning, expropriation and illiquidity are large, and timberland faces fire, pests and price cycles.

REITs

Definition: REITs hold and operate property and distribute most rental income to investors, listed or unlisted.

Mechanism: Returns come from rent, occupancy, leverage and appreciation; listed REIT prices move with rates and markets.

Example: For example, a shopping-centre REIT collects rent and pays dividends, but higher rates raise funding costs and pressure valuations.

Boundary: REITs are not direct home ownership; unit prices, leverage, governance and property concentration create risk, and dividends are not guaranteed to grow.

infrastructure and rent

Definition: Infrastructure assets include transport, energy, utilities and data networks, often regulated, cash-flow stable and capital-intensive.

Mechanism: Rents or user fees, regulated tariffs and traffic drive revenue; maintenance, rates and construction risk shape valuation.

Example: For example, a toll road charges by traffic and power tariffs are regulated, giving stable demand but requiring large expansion capital.

Boundary: Infrastructure is not a bond substitute; regulatory change, cost overruns, disasters and demand errors cause losses, and liquidity is low.

property finance

Definition: Property finance includes mortgages, development and construction loans, REIT debt and project finance, secured by asset cash flow.

Mechanism: Loan-to-value, rates, maturity and covenants shape risk; presales, rent and asset sales repay debt.

Example: For example, a developer uses construction loans and presales, and a sales slowdown can break cash flow and trigger default.

Boundary: Property finance is not safely collateralised; valuation, refinancing, rates and presale rules create mismatch, and project-company isolation can be pierced.

operating cash flow in property

Definition: Property operating cash flow is rent minus operating costs, taxes and capex, funding dividends and debt service.

Mechanism: Occupancy, rent growth, maintenance, lease terms and financing costs drive it; development also carries construction spending.

Example: For example, falling office occupancy cuts rent and cash flow while mortgage interest and repairs continue.

Boundary: Cash flow is not accounting profit; rent-free periods, capex and tenant defaults create volatility, and high leverage turns small declines into solvency stress.

Exercises

  1. Distribution test. Taxable income is $50 million (excluding net capital gain). What is the minimum distribution?
  2. Shareholder test. Three individuals own 80% of the shares during the last half of the taxable year. Is the "5 or fewer individuals own no more than 50%" condition met?
  3. Total return. Buy 1,000 shares at $25, receive $1.20 per share, and end the year at $23. What is the total return?
  4. Liquidity. What does a "very limited" redemption program mean for a non-traded REIT?
Suggested answers
  1. $45 million; net capital gain is outside this test, but it may still be distributed.
  2. No. Three individuals are "5 or fewer," and 80% exceeds the 50% limit for the last half of the taxable year.
  3. (23,000 + 1,200 - 25,000) / 25,000 = -3.2%; a 4.8% yield is not a positive return.
  4. Investors cannot redeem on demand and may have to wait for a liquidity event such as a listing or a liquidation; there is no exchange price, and an estimated share value may not be available for a long time.

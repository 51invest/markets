REITs and Infrastructure: Cash Flow, Leverage and Valuation

REITs package rental cash flows from real estate into tradable shares; infrastructure funds package long-term cash flows from tolls, networks and power plants into similar structures. Both attract investors with stable cash flow and are constrained by rates, leverage and asset quality.

What the product is

A REIT (real estate investment trust) packages a real-estate portfolio into a company or trust traded as shares. US REITs must meet income, asset and distribution tests and usually distribute most taxable income to shareholders. An infrastructure fund holds toll roads, airports, networks or power plants and distributes long-term concession or contract cash flow[1].

Rights and cash flows

REIT holders own distribution rights and residual value but do not directly control the properties; infrastructure holders receive concession cash flow subject to the concession period and regulated pricing. The cash-flow table is:

Date/condition Cash flow Amount Currency Uncertainty
Quarterly Rent distribution 0.9 per share CNY Occupancy and rent
Annual Infrastructure distribution 1.2 per share CNY Traffic and tariff
Maturity/exit Asset disposal At NAV CNY Valuation and liquidity

Numbers are teaching assumptions, not product quotes. Distributions are not guaranteed and may be limited by cash flow, debt covenants and regulation[2].

Participants

Issuer (REIT or infrastructure fund), asset operator, market makers, custodian, rating agencies, regulators and investors. REITs are often valued on P/FFO; infrastructure on cash-flow yield and concession life.

Trading, holding and exit

REITs trade on exchanges with better liquidity but premiums or discounts to NAV; infrastructure funds may be listed or private with longer lock-ups. Exit can be a secondary sale, maturity disposal or redemption, depending on terms.

Costs

Management fee, custody, commissions, financing cost, maintenance capex and tax. REIT capex and tenant improvements eat into distributable cash; infrastructure maintenance and replacement costs matter too.

Risks

First, higher rates depress valuations; second, occupancy or traffic falls; third, leverage and refinancing risk; fourth, regulated tariffs or concessions change; fifth, asset concentration; sixth, liquidity discount. Stable cash flow is not risk-free.

Terms and documents

Prospectus, trust deed, concession agreement, distribution policy, debt covenants and tax documents. Check distribution coverage, loan maturity profile and asset concentration[3].

Exercises

  1. Valuation question: FFO 5 per share, price 100. What is P/FFO? If the cap rate is 5%, what is the implied asset value?
  2. Cash-flow question: REIT distribution 0.9 per share, FFO 1.2. What is distribution coverage?
  3. Risk question: list interest-rate, leverage and tenant-concentration risks, and explain how each affects distributions.

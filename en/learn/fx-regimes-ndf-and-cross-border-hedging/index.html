FX Regimes, NDFs and Cross-Border Hedging

The exchange-rate regime determines which tools are available; NDFs cash-settle around delivery restrictions, but basis, regulation and counterparty risk remain.

Definition

Exchange-rate regimes run from hard pegs and adjustable pegs to managed and free floats; the key differences are central-bank intervention and capital-flow restrictions. Onshore and offshore markets may use different prices and delivery rules. A non-deliverable forward (NDF) does not exchange principal; it cash-settles the difference between the contracted rate and a fixing, and is common for restricted currencies. A deliverable forward exchanges principal at maturity and usually requires a genuine trade or investment background.

Intuition

Cross-border hedging is price insurance for a foreign-currency payment: an exporter fears the USD it will receive becomes cheaper, an importer fears the USD it must pay becomes more expensive. The instrument is only a vehicle; the real questions are how much exposure exists, when it occurs and where it can legally be delivered. An NDF is a difference contract: no principal exchange, only the FX difference settles. A deliverable forward actually exchanges currencies, so both legs must be funded at maturity. If you look only at the locked price and ignore fixing rules, onshore/offshore basis and counterparty credit, the price on the hedge sheet may not be realisable.

Mechanism

A hedger first fixes the exposure: currency, amount, date and cash-flow direction. An exporter receiving USD fears USD weakness and can sell USD forward; an importer paying USD fears USD strength and can buy USD forward. NDF pricing includes the offshore basis, fixing rules, counterparty credit and regulatory premium; for the same USD exposure, an NDF can be more expensive than an onshore forward. Futures reduce counterparty risk through central clearing, but contract months, margin and basis leave residual risk. Cross-border hedging must also separate transaction, translation and economic exposure [1][2][3].

Worked example

Suppose an exporter will receive USD 1 million in six months. Spot is 7.10, the six-month onshore forward is 7.08 and the offshore NDF is 7.06. A forward locks in CNY 7.08 million; an NDF locks in CNY 7.06 million, CNY 20,000 less. If spot falls to 6.95 at maturity, unhedged proceeds are CNY 6.95 million: the forward adds CNY 130,000 and the NDF adds CNY 110,000. If spot rises to 7.25, the forward gives up CNY 170,000 versus unhedged — a hedge locks a budget, not the best outcome. These are teaching assumptions and exclude margin, spreads and tax.

Second worked example: budget prices under three outcomes

Using the USD 1 million export exposure, spot 7.10, forward 7.08 and NDF 7.06: if spot ends at 7.00, unhedged proceeds are CNY 7.00 million, the forward gives 7.08 million and the NDF 7.06 million; the forward beats unhedged by CNY 80,000. If spot ends at 7.20, unhedged gives 7.20 million, the forward 7.08 million and the NDF 7.06 million; the forward gives up CNY 120,000. If spot ends at 7.08, the forward matches unhedged and the NDF is CNY 20,000 lower. A three-scenario table shows the hedge is not 'return enhancement' but a compression of outcomes into CNY 7.06–7.08 million; the NDF's CNY 20,000 gap is the visible cost of offshore basis and fixing rules. If the budget rate is 7.05, all three beat budget; if it is 7.15, the hedge locks below budget and the plan needs revisiting. These are teaching assumptions.

Boundary and mistakes

First, an NDF is not a free option: it locks a price and gives up favourable moves. Second, the offshore price is not the onshore executable price, and the gap widens in stress. Third, futures and forwards both carry basis risk, especially when contract months do not match cash-flow dates. Fourth, a hedge ratio above 100% becomes speculation and changes regulatory and accounting treatment. Fifth, FX fraud often features high leverage, guaranteed profits and blocked withdrawals; check registration and custody [2].

Failure modes

The first failure is fixing risk: an NDF settles at an official or industry fixing that may differ from the rate at which you actually convert cash, and the basis widens in stress. The second is treating the offshore price as an onshore executable price: capital controls, participants and delivery rules differ, so quotes are not interchangeable. The third is over-hedging: the part above the true exposure has no matching cash flow, so price moves become a directional bet. The fourth is tenor mismatch: a three-month contract used against a nine-month receivable must be rolled, adding roll spread and margin as new risks. The fifth is counterparty risk: an OTC NDF has no central clearing, so if the counterparty defaults, the locked price is only paper.

Three checks

First, is the exposure broken down by currency, amount, date and cash-flow direction, and is there a natural offset? Second, where does the instrument deliver, what fixing applies and is a genuine trade background required? Third, are the post-hedge budget rate, worst case and roll plan written down? Beyond these, include margin, spreads, tax and counterparty credit in the all-in cost; comparing only locked prices can turn a 'cheap' instrument into one that cannot be honoured.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

NDFs are easily confused with FX futures and deliverable forwards. Futures are exchange-cleared, standardised and margined daily; deliverable forwards trade OTC and exchange principal at maturity; NDFs are also OTC but do not exchange principal, settling only the fixing difference. Counterparty risk, delivery obligation and regulation differ. A counterexample is 'the NDF is cheaper, so it is better': an NDF may price below an onshore forward, but the gap can come from offshore basis, fixing rules or a liquidity discount, and the cheapness can return as basis or settlement disputes at maturity. Another is 'a hedge must cover 100%': when cash-flow dates and amounts are uncertain, 100% notional leaves over-hedge; layered, rolling or option hedges can match exposure better. Compare all-in cost before comparing the locked price.

Scope and limits

This FX-regime and NDF framework applies to currency pairs with clear fixing rules, convertible-restricted but hedgeable exposure and identifiable capital controls. If onshore and offshore markets are segmented, the fixing is manipulated, or delivery needs extra approval, basis and locked prices are distorted. It does not apply to treating an NDF as a free option without principal, nor to hedging that ignores counterparty and settlement risk. Confirm fixing source, settlement currency, capital controls and counterparty, then compare instruments on all-in cost; a cheap quoted price may only be a liquidity discount.

Finally, the hedge ratio should follow how certain the exposure is; when cash-flow dates and amounts are uncertain, retaining some exposure is more robust than forcing full cover.

Also record the source of basis and any settlement disputes.

Also keep reproducible intermediate records and scripts.

What the product is: rights and cash-flow structure

A non-deliverable forward (NDF) is a cash-settled FX forward: the parties fix a future exchange rate and settle only the difference between the agreed rate and the fixing rate at maturity, without delivering either currency. It is used mainly for currencies that are not freely convertible or where cross-border delivery is restricted.

The NDF buyer locks a forward price to buy foreign currency; the seller locks a price to sell it. Neither party delivers principal; both bear cash settlement of the difference. Cross-border hedging must also separate onshore and offshore prices, fixing rules and convertibility limits.

Participants, trading, holding and exit

Participants include corporate treasuries, banks, NDF market makers, brokers, offshore clearing firms and regulators. Corporates lock budget rates, banks and market makers quote and hedge, and regulators define convertibility and fixing rules.

Trading starts with a quote and rate lock, runs to the fixing date, and ends in cash settlement; it can also be closed early or rolled. Exit must handle the NDF leg, the onshore spot leg and the accounting hedge relationship together, not just one leg.

Costs, risks and terms and documents

Costs: Bid-ask spread, fixing basis, credit-line usage, settlement fees and cross-border funding cost. When onshore-offshore spreads widen, a seemingly cheap quote may hide a larger basis risk.

Risks: FX risk, fixing risk, convertibility and exchange-control risk, counterparty risk, basis risk and hedge-accounting failure. An NDF does not remove all FX risk; it converts part of future uncertainty into a priced contract.

Terms: Key documents are the ISDA master agreement, NDF confirmation, fixing-source and settlement-date rules, credit support annex and cross-border hedging policy. Check fixing time, holidays, settlement currency and default handling.

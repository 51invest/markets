REITs and Infrastructure: Cash Flow, Leverage and Valuation

REITs package rental cash flows from real estate into tradable shares; infrastructure funds package long-term cash flows from tolls, networks and power plants into similar structures. Both attract investors with stable cash flow and are constrained by rates, leverage and asset quality.

What the product is

A REIT (real estate investment trust) packages a real-estate portfolio into a company or trust traded as shares. US REITs must meet income, asset and distribution tests and usually distribute most taxable income to shareholders. An infrastructure fund holds toll roads, airports, networks or power plants and distributes long-term concession or contract cash flow[1].

Rights and cash flows

REIT holders own distribution rights and residual value but do not directly control the properties; infrastructure holders receive concession cash flow subject to the concession period and regulated pricing. The cash-flow table is:

Date/condition Cash flow Amount Currency Uncertainty
Quarterly Rent distribution 0.9 per share CNY Occupancy and rent
Annual Infrastructure distribution 1.2 per share CNY Traffic and tariff
Maturity/exit Asset disposal At NAV CNY Valuation and liquidity

Numbers are teaching assumptions, not product quotes. Distributions are not guaranteed and may be limited by cash flow, debt covenants and regulation[2].

Participants

Issuer (REIT or infrastructure fund), asset operator, market makers, custodian, rating agencies, regulators and investors. REITs are often valued on P/FFO; infrastructure on cash-flow yield and concession life.

Trading, holding and exit

REITs trade on exchanges with better liquidity but premiums or discounts to NAV; infrastructure funds may be listed or private with longer lock-ups. Exit can be a secondary sale, maturity disposal or redemption, depending on terms.

Costs

Management fee, custody, commissions, financing cost, maintenance capex and tax. REIT capex and tenant improvements eat into distributable cash; infrastructure maintenance and replacement costs matter too.

Risks

First, higher rates depress valuations; second, occupancy or traffic falls; third, leverage and refinancing risk; fourth, regulated tariffs or concessions change; fifth, asset concentration; sixth, liquidity discount. Stable cash flow is not risk-free.

Terms and documents

Prospectus, trust deed, concession agreement, distribution policy, debt covenants and tax documents. Check distribution coverage, loan maturity profile and asset concentration[3].

Reading and review

REIT and infrastructure valuation both return to cash flow, but the source differs. REITs mainly collect rent, exposed to leases, occupancy, maintenance capex and financing cost; infrastructure collects concession or contract revenue, exposed to regulated pricing, volume and the concession term. P/FFO and cap rate are not isolated multiples: FFO must absorb maintenance capex, and the cap rate puts growth and risk into the denominator. The refinancing wall is the most neglected boundary: when maturities cluster, floating rates rise or refinancing spreads widen, distribution coverage can fall quickly. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Reading checklist

Infrastructure also has a concession-end problem: when the revenue right expires, the asset may be worth zero or require renegotiation. The shorter the remaining term, the more valuation depends on renewal assumptions. Split cash flow into within-concession and post-renewal parts, apply separate discount rates and renewal probabilities, and only then compare P/FFO and cap rate.

Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." Boundary case: Cap rates of 5% and 6%: how much REIT equity value is lost?

Net operating income is 100 million yuan. At a 5% cap rate, asset value is 2,000 million; with 800 of debt, equity is 1,200 million. At a 6% cap rate, asset value is 1,667 million and equity falls to 867 million, a 27.8% decline. If the debt rate rises from 4% to 6%, interest rises from 32 to 48 million and operating cash flow falls from 68 to 52 million, reducing distribution coverage. If the cap rate is 6% but NOI rises to 110 on higher rents, asset value is 1,833 and equity 1,033, partly offsetting the rate shock.

The conclusion reverses when leases escalate or regulated tariffs have inflation clauses: cash-flow growth can offset a higher cap rate; long-duration fixed-rate debt reduces refinancing shock. The boundary is that REITs and infrastructure both depend on cash flow, but leases, regulation, concession life and leverage differ. P/FFO and cap rate cannot be used in isolation.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not a valuation.

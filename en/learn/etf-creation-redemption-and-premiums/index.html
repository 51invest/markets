ETF Creation, Redemption and Premiums

ETFs trade at market prices while authorized participants create and redeem shares in creation units, usually priced at NAV; arbitrage usually narrows premiums but does not guarantee it.

What the product is

This article uses ETFs regulated by the U.S. SEC as examples. An ETF is a fund listed on an exchange, usually registered as an open-end investment company or a unit investment trust[2]. Each share represents a proportionate interest in the portfolio. Unlike a mutual fund, an ETF generally does not sell or redeem individual shares with retail investors. It contracts with authorized participants (APs), who create and redeem creation units in the primary market; retail investors trade only in the secondary market[1].

Rights, obligations and cash flows

ETF holders own a proportionate interest in fund assets and bear investment losses. APs deliver or receive baskets of securities and cash under their agreements with the fund. An AP is not the retail investor's agent and is not obligated to keep creating or redeeming.

Participants and venues

The fund, board or trustee, adviser, custodian, transfer agent and distributor form the fund side. APs are typically large broker-dealers or market makers. Exchanges, clearing houses and market makers form the trading side. The prospectus sets AP eligibility and the creation-unit size, such as 50,000 shares, though sizes differ[3].

Trading, holding and exit

Retail investors open a brokerage account and trade at market prices. APs create or redeem in creation units under the fund's stated procedures [1]; the transaction is normally priced under the fund's valuation rules, commonly using the fund's NAV for that day, and may use a basket that mirrors the portfolio and/or cash. When the market price is above NAV, an AP can buy the basket, create shares and sell them; when below NAV, an AP can buy ETF shares and redeem them for a basket or cash. Arbitrage usually pulls price toward NAV, but not as a guarantee[1]: AP withdrawal, short-sale limits, closed foreign markets and basket costs can widen or prolong a premium or discount. Retail holders exit by selling in the secondary market and cannot redeem with the fund at NAV.

ETFs usually trade on an exchange at market prices that can sit above or below net asset value, and only authorized participants create or redeem directly with the fund, in large creation units, typically exchanging a basket of securities [2].

Costs

Costs include management fees, custody fees, commissions, spreads, creation and redemption fees, cash-substitution costs and taxes; index-tracking ETFs may also have index licensing fees. A premium or discount is the gap between market price and NAV, not an expense ratio, but it affects purchase and sale returns. Fee and tax treatment vary by jurisdiction, fund documents, share class and investor account; compare total costs, tracking difference and liquidity.

Risks

Risks include market, tracking error, premium or discount, liquidity, AP concentration, suspension of creation, cross-border time zones, counterparty, securities lending and leverage. ETFs do not guarantee principal and are not protected by deposit insurance; for example, U.S. FDIC insurance does not cover ETFs. In stress, market price can diverge from NAV and trading can thin.

Terms and documents

The prospectus and statement of additional information set the objective, risks, fees, creation-unit size, AP eligibility, basket composition, redemption limits and premium/discount disclosure; the AP agreement governs operations. Fund websites publish holdings, NAV, closing price, premiums or discounts and spreads; the formal documents control. That disclosure sentence is this article's own operational wording: the 497 prospectus cited as source 3 never uses the word website (0 faces measured 2026-11-04), and what it supports verbatim is that creation and redemption orders go through the Distributor and only a signed Authorized Participant may place them, and that market price can trade at a premium or discount to NAV [3]. Exact disclosure items and timing follow the fund's formal documents and its current website.

Comparison and boundaries

ETFs and mutual funds are both funds, but ETFs trade on an exchange and, generally, only APs can create or redeem directly with the fund; mutual funds sell and redeem with the fund. ETFs and closed-end funds both trade on an exchange, but ETFs have creation and redemption while closed-end funds usually have a fixed share count. An ETN is an issuer's debt obligation with credit risk, not an ETF. An ETF is not the index and does not guarantee full replication.

ETNs and other ETPs: same trading wrapper, different legal structure

ETP is an umbrella term for exchange-traded products, including ETFs, ETNs, and exchange-traded commodity trusts or partnerships. An ETN is an exchange-traded debt security whose promised return is linked to an index or benchmark; it is not a fund, does not hold a portfolio of assets and is an obligation of the issuer[4][5]. An ETF is a fund that holds assets; an ETN holder is an unsecured creditor of the issuer[5].

The issuer issues and redeems ETNs to keep the market price close to a calculated value called the indicative value or closing indicative value. The issuer calculates and publishes the indicative value daily, usually as the value of the underlying index or benchmark minus daily investor fees; an intraday indicative value is typically published about every 15 seconds[4]. The issuer has sole discretion over additional issuance. Investors may apply to redeem before maturity under the prospectus process, but redemption usually requires 25,000 or 50,000 notes, and the steps, fees and thresholds make redemption impractical as a day-to-day liquidity source for most retail investors[4]. Some ETNs are callable or redeemable early; leveraged, inverse and inverse-leveraged ETNs are designed as short-term trading tools, and their performance over holding periods longer than one day can differ significantly from the stated multiple[5]. An ETN has no underlying portfolio, so if the issuer becomes insolvent, holders as unsecured creditors could lose the entire investment[5]. Other ETPs differ: an exchange-traded commodity trust may physically hold a precious metal, while a commodity partnership may hold futures or other derivatives, with tax, governance, redemption and credit risk changing by structure[2].

Teaching assumption: an ETN has an indicative value of 100 and its linked index rises 10%, so the indicative value before fees is about 110. If the issuer defaults, holders may not receive 110 even if the index has not fallen, because the ETN is the issuer’s unsecured debt. Compare an ETF holding the same index constituents: if the index rises 10%, the portfolio assets rise about 10% and are held by a custodian; the ETF still carries tracking error, counterparty and securities-lending risk. The numbers are teaching assumptions, not real quotes.

An ETN is not an ETF and is not an “asset-backed structured note”. Do not treat an ETN’s indicative value as a fund NAV redeemable at par: the market price can deviate significantly, a premium can become large when the issuer suspends issuance, and the price can fall sharply when issuance resumes[4]. An ETN has a maturity date and may be redeemed or called; leveraged or inverse ETNs are not long-term holding tools. ETP is not a single legal category: although all trade on exchanges, ETFs, ETNs and commodity trusts or partnerships differ in asset ownership, credit risk, tax and creation/redemption mechanics, so a ticker or name is not enough.

Boundary case: A 0.9% premium and a 1.0% creation fee: why arbitrage does not occur

An ETF has NAV of 100.00 and a market price of 100.90, a premium of 0.90, or 0.9%. An authorised participant buys the constituents, creates ETF shares and sells them, earning a gross 0.90. If creation costs 1.00, the trade loses 0.10 and the premium can persist. If the price is 99.10, a 0.90% discount, and redemption costs 0.30, the trade earns 0.60 and redemption arbitrage is more likely. If an ETN has an indicative value of 100 and the index rises 10%, the pre-fee value is 110; but if the issuer defaults, holders are unsecured creditors and may not receive 110 even after an index gain.

The conclusion reverses when the arbitrage channel is open and cheap: creation and redemption thresholds, settlement timing and capital controls determine whether premiums converge. The boundary is that an ETF owns assets, while an ETN is an issuer obligation; both trade on exchanges but have different credit-risk structures.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not market quotes.

Exercises

The following teaching assumptions use the fund's pricing currency; the numbers are not quotes from any real ETF.

  1. Premium or discount. An ETF has a NAV of 50.00 and a market price of 49.00. What is the discount? What does a retail investor pay for 100 shares?
  2. Creation arbitrage. A securities basket costs 1,000,000, cash is 10,000, the creation unit is 50,000 shares, NAV is 20.20 and the market price is 20.50. Ignoring fees, what is the AP's gross profit?
  3. AP boundary. Why can't a retail investor usually create shares directly at NAV? Must a persistent premium offer riskless arbitrage?
Suggested answers
  1. Discount = (50.00 - 49.00) ÷ 50.00 = 2%. The investor pays 4,900 in the fund's pricing currency for 100 shares, plus commission.
  2. Creation cost = 1,000,000 + 10,000 = 1,010,000; sale proceeds = 50,000 × 20.50 = 1,025,000; gross profit = 15,000. The question ignores fees; real trades would also involve fees, taxes and market impact.
  3. Creation and redemption are generally open only to contracted APs and require creation-unit size, basket delivery and eligibility. A persistent premium does not guarantee riskless arbitrage: APs may withdraw, short selling may be constrained, basket costs may rise, and the price may fall before completion.

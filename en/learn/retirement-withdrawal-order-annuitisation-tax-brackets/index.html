Withdrawal Order and Annuitisation: The Retirement Withdrawal Ledger

Retirement withdrawals are not just 'which account to spend first': pre-tax, after-tax, Social Security and annuities differ in tax, longevity and liquidity. A wrong order can raise taxes, exhaust assets early or forfeit annuity insurance.

Definition and intuition

Withdrawal order is the sequence of drawing from taxable accounts, traditional IRA/401(k) (pre-tax), Roth IRA (after-tax), Social Security and annuities. Tax timing differs: pre-tax accounts are taxed on withdrawal, Roth on contribution with tax-free withdrawals, and taxable accounts tax only gains at capital-gains rates.

Annuitisation converts part of the portfolio into lifetime income, insuring longevity. Social Security delay is another form of annuitisation: later claiming raises lifetime benefits but uses savings.

The goal is not only 'least tax' but a balance of tax, longevity insurance, liquidity and inheritance. Spending taxable accounts first preserves pre-tax compounding but may miss low-rate windows[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose a 65-year-old has a 1 million yuan traditional IRA, 500,000 in a taxable account and 30,000 of annual Social Security. Withdrawing 100,000 from the traditional IRA at a 22% marginal rate leaves 78,000 after tax; withdrawing 100,000 from the taxable account taxes only gains and may leave 95,000.

If the traditional IRA withdrawal is kept within the standard deduction, the marginal rate may fall to 12% and after-tax income rises to 88,000; withdrawing 50,000 more pushes part of the income into the 22% bracket, adding little after tax.

If 300,000 buys a lifetime annuity paying 17,000 a year and Social Security is delayed to 70 for 42,000 a year, lifetime income rises but liquidity falls. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Three trade-offs

Trade-off 1: tax brackets. Early retirement has low income, so traditional IRA withdrawals can fill low brackets; too much pushes into higher brackets and makes more Social Security taxable.

Trade-off 2: Roth conversion. Converting traditional IRA to Roth in low-tax years locks in tax and reduces future RMDs; the conversion itself is taxable and may push up the current rate.

Trade-off 3: Social Security and annuities. Delaying Social Security and annuitising provide longevity insurance but reduce inheritance and liquidity; people with poor health or short life expectancy may not benefit.

There is no single right order; model annual taxes, RMDs, Social Security and medical spending in a cash-flow projection[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: tax law changes. Rates, deductions, RMD ages and Social Security rules can change; use scenario analysis for long models.

Boundary 2: RMDs are not the only constraint. Traditional IRAs have required minimum distributions; Roth does not, but five-year rules and inheritance limits apply.

Boundary 3: annuity fees and counterparty. Annuitisation is not free insurance; fees, inflation adjustment and insurer credit quality affect actual income.

Mistake 1: spending pre-tax first is always better. Pre-tax withdrawals are fully taxable and can push up rates; in low-tax years, drawing some pre-tax first can be better.

Mistake 2: Roth always beats a traditional IRA. It depends on current versus future rates; converting in high-tax years may not pay.

Deeper note: withdrawal order, tax brackets and annuitisation

Withdrawal order is not a fixed formula. The common order is taxable, then tax-deferred, then tax-free, but it depends on rates, capital gains, social-security taxation and estate goals. If current rates are low, drawing tax-deferred first may be better; if future rates are high, preserving tax-free growth may be better.

Bracket filling: retirement income may fall into a lower bracket, so using the low-bracket space to draw tax-deferred accounts can cut lifetime tax; but too much triggers social-security taxation, Medicare premiums and estate tax. Model multiple years, not one.

RMDs force withdrawals from tax-deferred accounts and can push income into higher brackets; if cash is not needed, use qualified charitable distributions or Roth conversions.

Roth conversions move tax-deferred balances to Roth in low-tax years, paying tax for future tax-free growth; too much pushes the current bracket up, too little misses the window. Weigh marginal rates, estate and liquidity.

Annuitisation provides lifetime income and cuts longevity risk but is illiquid and costly; partial annuitisation covering essential spending is usually more flexible than full annuitisation.

Sequence risk: a market fall early in retirement accelerates depletion; cash buffers, flexible spending and partial annuities mitigate it.

Measurement: multi-year tax, depletion probability, estate value, liquidity and inflation protection. A single 'after-tax return' is insufficient.

Common misuses: watching only this year's rate, ignoring RMDs and social-security taxation, treating annuities as savings, and treating the 4% rule as universal.

Finally, withdrawal is a joint optimisation of tax, risk and longevity; calculate essential spending, fill brackets, then set the annuitisation share.

Withdrawal-order checklist

Review a retirement withdrawal order for cash flow and tax brackets, not for a slogan of 'taxable first' or 'tax-deferred first':

  1. How large is the spending gap? Separate essentials, medical, care and discretionary spending; the gap sets scale and flexibility.
  2. How do tax brackets shift in retirement? RMDs, Social Security, annuities and investment income raise marginal rates; compute taxable income before choosing the account.
  3. Is a Roth conversion worth it? Conversions can pay in low-bracket years but use cash and trigger IRMAA-type surcharges; assess lifetime tax.
  4. Do RMDs force excess withdrawals? At older ages RMDs can push income into higher brackets; partial conversions or charitable giving can ease it.
  5. How does Social Security timing interact? Delaying raises lifetime benefits but draws down early assets; model it with the withdrawal order.
  6. Does annuitisation fill the longevity gap? Annuities provide stable income but lose liquidity; keep emergency reserves.
  7. How are medical and long-term care funded? A shock can force high-bracket withdrawals; reserve HSA, cash and insurance.
  8. Is the order reviewed annually? Tax law, returns and health change the optimum; static rules need annual checks.

Withdrawal order is not a fixed formula but a joint optimisation of cash flow, tax brackets, longevity and liquidity.

Finally, stress-test the withdrawal order for 'live longer' and 'markets worse'; a statically optimal order can exhaust liquidity early in a long life or bear market. An annual review is more reliable than a one-off plan.

Minimum action: run the withdrawal order under three lifespans (85/95/100) and two return scenarios, record tax brackets, RMDs and the liquidity low point, and update annually.

Record: keep annual tax forms, RMD calculations and actual withdrawals for review and for heirs to execute.

In one line: recompute the withdrawal order annually with lifespan and tax brackets.

Note: tax law and RMD tables update annually; old rules cannot be reused.

What would change the conclusion

Withdrawal-order conclusions depend on lifespan, tax rules and returns:

  1. Lifespan exceeds expectations. If someone lives past 95, early high withdrawals exhaust liquidity and only annuities and Social Security remain.
  2. Tax rules change. If rates, RMD ages or Social Security rules shift, the optimal order flips.
  3. A bad return sequence. If a bear market hits early in retirement, a fixed order amplifies sequence risk.
  4. Annuity fees are high. If fees and credit discounts are large, annuitisation is less attractive.
  5. A medical shock. If long-term care costs exceed expectations, high-bracket withdrawals raise lifetime tax.

Practical implication: recompute the withdrawal order annually across lifespans, tax brackets and return scenarios; a static optimum can exhaust liquidity in a long life or bear market.

Reading supplement

Withdrawal order also interacts with annuitisation. Drawing taxable accounts first may lower the current tax rate but reduces future taxable income; annuitising transfers longevity risk to an insurer at the cost of liquidity and inheritance. Compare paths under three shocks: an early market fall, living past 95 and a sudden long-term-care bill. Only a plan that covers all three is robust.

Further reading: withdrawal rules and tax research

Bengen's 4% rule used historical US stock and bond data to set a first-year withdrawal rate; later work shows sensitivity to valuations, fees, longevity and asset allocation, so it cannot be applied mechanically.

Kitces and others discuss withdrawal order, Roth conversions and bracket filling; the core conclusion is that no single order is optimal - it depends on the tax-rate path, estate and liquidity.

Social Security claiming: delaying is like buying an inflation-adjusted lifetime annuity, with returns depending on longevity and tax rates; high earners gain more from delay.

RMDs and QCDs: RMDs force withdrawals, while qualified charitable distributions can satisfy RMDs and go to charity, suited to high-bracket people who do not need cash.

Reading suggestion: put the 4% rule, bracket filling and annuitisation into a multi-year model and use scenarios, not one-year arithmetic.

Chapter takeaways

  • Withdrawal order depends on the tax-rate path, capital gains, Social Security taxation and estate goals; there is no fixed formula.
  • Bracket filling needs multi-year modelling to avoid triggering Social Security taxation and Medicare premium thresholds.
  • RMDs force withdrawals; QCDs and Roth conversions can work alongside.
  • Annuitise essential spending while keeping liquidity for medical and unexpected costs.
  • Sequence risk is mitigated with cash buffers, flexible spending and partial annuities.

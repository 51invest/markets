What the product is

“Ferrous metals” in this article means iron, manganese, chromium and their alloys, with steel as the most important product - a usage note written by this article, since none of the registered sources defines the term. Steel is not a single homogeneous commodity: grade, strength, surface treatment and dimensions determine its use and delivery standard. Investors can trade steel futures or hold mining and steel-company shares; physical businesses buy iron ore, coking coal, scrap and energy, then organize smelting and rolling.

Supply chain and participants

The blast furnace–basic oxygen furnace route relies mainly on iron ore and coking coal: ore is sintered or pelletized, coal is coked, the blast furnace reduces iron ore to hot metal, and the basic oxygen furnace converts it to steel. The electric arc furnace route relies mainly on scrap and electricity. Crude steel is continuously cast and rolled into hot-rolled coil, rebar and wire rod for construction, machinery, automobiles and appliances. Mines, coke plants, mills, traders, processors, warehouses and end users form the chain; freight, inventory and payment terms drive cash flow at each stage.

In 2025, world iron ore production was about 2.6 billion tons of usable ore containing about 1.6 billion tons of iron [1]. USGS also reports in its Events, Trends, and Issues narrative, rather than in the production table, that imported iron ore fines of 62% iron content on a CIF basis at Tianjin Port, China averaged $99.07 per ton in the first nine months of 2025, down from $112.07 in the same period of 2024 [1].

Trading, holding and exit

Steel futures can be closed or delivered. On the Shanghai Futures Exchange, the hot-rolled coil contract is 10 metric tons per lot, quoted in RMB per metric ton, with a minimum fluctuation of RMB 1 per metric ton, a minimum margin of 4% of contract value, and a 300-metric-ton delivery unit; the standard product is Q235B or SS400 coil 5.75 mm thick and 1,500 mm wide[3]. Rebar futures deliver grade HRB400E under GB 1499.2-2024, use the same 10-metric-ton lot size, carry a minimum trading margin of 5% of contract value and a 300-metric-ton delivery unit[4]. Operating businesses hedge to lock in processing margins, but basis and deliverable grade still create risk.

Costs and risks

Steel costs consist of iron ore, coking coal, scrap, power, transport and carbon. World crude steel production was about 1.849 billion tons in 2025, with 69.4% from the basic oxygen furnace route and 30.3% from electric arc furnaces; building and infrastructure accounted for about 52% of global steel use[2]. A property or infrastructure slowdown compresses demand, while trade barriers and capacity changes alter regional price spreads. Futures leverage, margin calls, delivery quality and inventory devaluation are real risks.

Terms and documents

Physical contracts cover grade, dimensions, weight, delivery location, payment and inspection. Futures contracts cover specifications, margin, last trading day, deliverable brands and warehouses. Equity holdings require attention to mineral rights, costs, output and capital spending. Iron ore from different origins differs in grade and impurities and cannot be compared by tonnage alone.

Comparison and boundaries

The blast furnace–basic oxygen furnace route depends on iron ore and coking coal, while the electric arc furnace route depends on scrap and electricity; their cost and carbon structures differ. Hot-rolled coil and rebar have different uses and delivery standards and are not interchangeable. Steel futures are not iron ore futures, and neither is a steel-company share: they represent a finished product, a raw material and corporate equity respectively.

Cost card: three routes, four input prices

Separate raw materials from processing. The blast-furnace route uses 1.6 tonnes of iron ore and 0.5 tonnes of coke per tonne of steel. At iron ore 800 and coke 2,000, raw material cost is 2,280 yuan; adding 800 yuan processing gives 3,080 yuan. The electric-arc route uses 1.1 tonnes of scrap at 3,000, or 3,300 yuan; adding 400 yuan power gives 3,700 yuan.

Then switch two input prices. If scrap falls to 2,400 yuan, the electric route costs 3,040 yuan and overtakes the blast furnace. If coke rises to 2,600 yuan, the blast-furnace cost becomes 3,380 yuan and the electric route is cheaper again. The cost ranking is not fixed; it flips when an input crosses a threshold.

The cost card also needs grade, yield, power and environmental constraints. Lower iron grade or a higher coke rate raises the blast-furnace cost; a capacity limit can keep the cheaper electric route from running flat out. Judging steel margins from iron ore alone misses coke, scrap and demand.

Refutation: if the ranking does not flip as in the example, grade, yield or processing assumptions differ; if high-cost capacity still runs flat out, demand or inventories are lifting the marginal cost. The residual risk is the gap between spot and contract prices and between tax-inclusive and tax-exclusive quotes.

Inventory and utilisation change the practical effect of the cost ranking. The blast furnace is cheaper, but coke shortages or more maintenance can keep output down; the electric route is costlier, but strong steel demand and ample scrap can keep it running flat out. The cost card explains the direction of profit; the inventory cycle explains its size.

Processing cost also splits into fixed and variable parts. The 800 yuan blast-furnace charge covers labour, maintenance and depreciation; the 400 yuan electric charge varies with utilisation. Spread fixed cost over output and unit cost rises when output falls; looking only at raw materials understates the drag of idle capacity.

Boundary case: Iron ore at 800, coke at 2,000, scrap at 3,000: ranking three steel routes

A blast-furnace route uses 1.6 tons of iron ore and 0.5 tons of coke per ton of steel. Raw-material cost is 1.6 × 800 + 0.5 × 2,000 = 2,280 yuan; adding 800 of processing gives 3,080. An electric-arc route uses 1.1 tons of scrap, costing 1.1 × 3,000 = 3,300; adding 400 of power gives 3,700. If scrap falls to 2,400, the electric route costs 3,040 and beats the blast furnace. If coke rises to 2,600, the blast-furnace cost becomes 3,380 and the electric route wins again.

The conclusion reverses when grade, yield and environmental constraints change: lower ore grade, a higher coke rate or limited power capacity for electric furnaces can reorder costs. The boundary is that chain profits are distributed across processing stages; raw-material prices, processing fees and product premiums must be read together. Judging steel margins from iron ore alone misses coke, scrap and demand.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not spot quotes.

Cost ranking also depends on utilisation and inventory cycles. Cheap raw materials do not guarantee full output if a blast furnace is under maintenance, coke inventory is thin or steel demand is weak; conversely, strong demand and low inventory can bring higher-cost capacity into the marginal supply. Reading the cost curve with supply-demand balances explains profit.

Put the steel price beside the cost table: at 3,500 yuan, the blast furnace earns 420 yuan per ton while the electric route loses 200; at 3,200 yuan, the blast furnace earns 120 and the electric route loses 500. Low cost does not by itself mean high profit; price and utilisation decide the final result.

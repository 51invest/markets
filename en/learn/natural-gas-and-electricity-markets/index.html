Natural Gas and Electricity Markets

Natural gas is a storable, transportable commodity, while electricity is a secondary energy source that must be balanced in real time; their contracts, costs and risks differ.

ModuleMKT-M08 · 能源与电力
LevelFoundation
Read14 min · 14 sections

What the product is

Natural gas is mostly methane, measured in cubic feet or British thermal units and moved by pipeline or as liquefied natural gas. Electricity is a secondary energy source [4] delivered through the grid; it is hard to store at scale, so supply and demand must balance in real time. Gas trades at hubs such as Henry Hub; power trades in day-ahead, real-time, capacity and ancillary-service markets [5]; retail contracts bundle wholesale prices, network charges and retail costs.

Supply chain and participants

Gas flows from wells through processing, pipelines, storage or LNG liquefaction plants to power plants, industry and cities. Power flows from generators through transmission and distribution to users, with a system operator balancing in real time. Cash flows include gas commodity, transport and storage charges, and power energy, capacity, ancillary and network charges. Participants include producers, pipelines, storage operators, generators, retailers, grid operators, users and exchanges. The EIA notes that U.S. regional transmission organizations also act as balancing authorities; a balancing authority keeps demand and supply precisely balanced, and imbalance can cause local or widespread blackouts [2].

Trading, holding and exit

Henry Hub futures are 10,000 MMBtu, quoted in U.S. dollars per MMBtu, with a minimum tick of $0.001 per MMBtu for direct trades ($10 per contract), physical delivery at Henry Hub, Louisiana, and months covering the current year and the next 12 years[3]. Power forwards or futures vary by peak and off-peak period, node and settlement, and trade on exchanges or over the counter; retail contracts can be fixed, floating or hybrid. Physical gas is scheduled daily; financial contracts are closed or cash-settled before expiry.

Seasonality and balancing

Gas supply and demand are uneven across the year: heating use concentrates in the cold season, gas-fired generation rises in the hot season with air-conditioning load, and industrial demand and pipeline maintenance cluster in the shoulder seasons, so the same pipeline faces different pressures at different times. Storage is the main buffer for that gap: dry gas can be sent into underground storage for later use [1] and withdrawn when needed. The injection and withdrawal rhythm is set by operators under contracts and operating conditions, and this article deliberately gives no month, inventory level or spread figure, because those differ every year; check public series rather than quoting the wording here.

The seasonal shape of prices is therefore a composite of storage cost, available pipeline capacity and expected demand. A near-versus-far spread is not automatically an expectation of higher prices; it can simply be the cost of holding gas and the return on the capital tied up (the article's own framing).

Cross-border and cross-region flows are seasonal too; the available capacity of liquefaction plants and conditions in destination markets change the opportunity cost of the same cargo [1][6].

On the electricity side, balancing is a continuous matter rather than a seasonal one. Regional transmission organizations also act as balancing authorities; a balancing authority ensures that supply and demand match precisely, and an imbalance may cause a local or widespread outage [2]. What seasons change is how hard balancing is: extreme cold or heat pushes load up and maintenance is usually scheduled away from peaks, so the same rules come under different pressure at different times of year (the article's own framing).

Costs and risks

Costs include gas commodity, transport, storage, liquefaction and regasification [6], generator fuel and operations, transmission and distribution, capacity, ancillary services, balancing penalties and financing. Risks include weather, pipeline congestion and regional basis, storage levels, LNG shipping, plant outages, fuel prices, power price spikes and negative prices, regulation, counterparties and leverage. Power is hard to store, so short-term imbalance can push prices very high or negative; negative prices reflect local surplus, not free electricity.

Terms and documents

Gas contracts cover heating value, delivery point, daily quantity, take-or-pay and credit; power contracts cover load profile, node, capacity responsibility and default; futures cover specifications, margin, last trading day and delivery. Convert units before comparing: 1 MWh corresponds to about 3.412 MMBtu of heat, but a gas plant's fuel input depends on its heat rate.

Gas and power prices are related but not the same; fuel cost is only part of the power price. LNG links regional markets, but liquefaction, shipping and regasification costs keep prices from converging fully [6].

Natural gas, LNG, coal, power and nuclear fuel: one energy family

Energy commodities are not one asset class. Natural gas can be stored underground, electricity must balance in real time, coal is priced on heat content, ash and sulfur, nuclear fuel must pass through conversion, enrichment and assembly, and LNG cools gas into a liquid for ocean transport. The five sections below put these objects on one map, not to memorize definitions but to see each one's pricing unit, logistics constraint and failure boundary.

Natural gas: heat content, hubs and contracts

Natural gas is mostly methane and is measured in cubic feet or British thermal units. Heat content is not a constant: U.S. gas delivered to end users averages about 1,037 Btu per cubic foot, and residential and commercial appliances generally accept 950 to 1,100 Btu/cf. At 1,037 Btu/cf, 1,000 cubic feet (Mcf) equals about 1.037 MMBtu [7][8]. The Henry Hub futures contract is 10,000 MMBtu and is quoted in dollars per MMBtu [3]. A price per thousand cubic feet and a price per million Btu are therefore not the same number until heat content is applied.

Mechanically, gas leaves the wellhead, passes through a processing plant that removes water, carbon dioxide and natural gas liquids to meet pipeline quality, and enters a pipeline that receives it by heat content, delivery point and daily quantity. A buyer nominates daily volume, and a deviation from the nomination creates an imbalance charge. Long-term contracts often include take-or-pay: the buyer commits to a minimum quantity and pays for the shortfall, sometimes with a right to lift the unpaid volume later. Hub price plus basis is the delivered price, and basis reflects pipeline capacity, local storage and congestion.

Teaching example: assume Henry Hub at USD 3.00/MMBtu and a citygate basis of +USD 0.75. The delivered cost of 10,000 MMBtu is (3.00+0.75)×10,000 = USD 37,500. If the contract minimum is 10,000 MMBtu and the buyer only takes 9,000, a take-or-pay clause may still settle 10,000; the 1,000 MMBtu becomes a later lift right or a sunk cost. This is a teaching assumption; the actual terms govern.

Boundaries: heat content varies by basin and composition, so 1 Mcf is not always 1 MMBtu; a hub price is not a delivered price and basis can be positive or negative; the "global gas market" is not fully arbitraged because liquefaction, shipping and regasification separate regions. Pipeline quality, pressure, daily quantity and credit are contract terms, not details to ignore.

LNG: liquefaction, shipping, regasification and netback

LNG is natural gas cooled to a liquid and carried by ship. The chain runs liquefaction, loading, ocean shipping, regasification at the receiving terminal and pipeline delivery; liquefaction shrinks the volume sharply, which is what makes ocean transport economic [6]. An LNG cargo is usually priced by netback: the destination benchmark minus the logistics cost of getting the gas there.

Mechanically, a seller delivers a cargo into Asia at JKM or Europe at TTF, deducts shipping, regasification, liquefaction fuel and capacity charges, and then subtracts feedgas cost. The residue is netback. U.S. exports often use Henry Hub as the feedgas benchmark and add a liquefaction tolling fee and freight; a long-term contract may charge a fixed capacity fee even when no cargo is loaded [6]. JKM, TTF and Henry Hub are three regional benchmarks, and their spread reflects transport and receiving capacity rather than three quotes for the same molecule [13].

Teaching example: assume JKM at USD 12.00/MMBtu, shipping USD 2.00, regasification USD 0.80, liquefaction USD 2.20 and feedgas USD 3.00. Netback is 12.00−2.00−0.80−2.20 = USD 7.00, and the margin after feedgas is USD 4.00/MMBtu. If JKM falls to USD 7.00, netback is only USD 2.00, below the USD 3.00 feedgas cost, so a spot or floating cargo may divert, defer or cancel under its contract; a long-term take-or-pay buyer may still owe the fixed fee. These are teaching assumptions.

Boundaries: LNG is not pipeline gas; heat content, composition, boiling point, boil-off and terminal compatibility all affect value. Scheduling, the Panama and Suez routes, weather and port congestion change delivered cost. Destination clauses, take-or-pay and cancellation rights in long-term contracts determine whether a buyer can simply buy less when the price falls. A JKM–Henry Hub spread is not by itself an arbitrage profit.

Coal: heat content, ash, sulfur and transport

Coal is ranked as lignite, subbituminous, bituminous and anthracite, and heat content varies widely: EIA's fuel-value ranges put lignite at 10 to 14.5 MMBtu per ton, bituminous at 20 to 29 and anthracite at 22 to 28 [9]; another page gives subbituminous at about 8,300 to 13,000 Btu per pound and bituminous at about 10,500 to 15,500 Btu per pound [10]. Beyond heat content, ash, sulfur, moisture and volatile matter determine boiler suitability; low-sulfur coal can earn a premium under emission limits [10].

Mechanically, delivered coal cost equals mine price plus transport. EIA reports that in 2022 the average delivered coal price to U.S. power plants was USD 45.78 per short ton, of which transport was USD 18.69, about 41%; for long-distance shipments of Wyoming subbituminous coal to eastern plants, transport can exceed the mine price itself [10]. A price per ton must therefore be converted to a price per MMBtu and then loaded with transport and environmental cost.

Teaching example: assume subbituminous coal at 8,800 Btu/lb; one short ton contains 17.6 MMBtu, so a mine price of USD 20 and rail of USD 25 gives USD 45 per ton, or USD 2.56/MMBtu. Bituminous at 12,000 Btu/lb is 24 MMBtu per ton; mine USD 60 plus rail USD 20 gives USD 80 per ton, or USD 3.33/MMBtu. These are teaching assumptions.

Boundaries: heat content, ash and sulfur vary within a rank, so a national average is not a cargo specification; boiler design determines whether a plant can burn high-ash or high-sulfur coal; carbon costs, mercury rules and rail capacity change economics. Coal is not a homogeneous globally fungible commodity, and export coal and domestic coal have different quality and logistics chains.

Power: marginal cost, nodes and ancillary services

Electricity is a secondary energy carrier and must balance in real time. U.S. regional transmission organizations (RTOs/ISOs) typically run a day-ahead energy market, a real-time energy market, a capacity market and ancillary-service markets: the day-ahead market schedules generation against next-day forecast load, the real-time market adjusts for deviations every 5 to 15 minutes, the capacity market pays for future availability, and ancillary markets procure regulation, reserves, voltage support and black start [11][12].

The core mechanism is the merit order: the RTO dispatches bids from lowest to highest until demand is met, and the last unit needed sets the clearing price. FERC's teaching example is explicit: Plant A is 600 MW at USD 50/MWh and Plant B is 300 MW at USD 100/MWh. At 500 MW demand only A runs and the clearing price is USD 50; at 825 MW both run and the clearing price is USD 100, so A's 600 MW is also paid USD 100 and earns USD 50/MWh of inframarginal rent [11]. Locational marginal prices also include transmission congestion and losses, so nodes can differ at the same instant.

Teaching example: assume a gas unit with a heat rate of 7,000 Btu/kWh, or 7 MMBtu/MWh, and gas at USD 3/MMBtu. Fuel marginal cost is USD 21/MWh; at a USD 30 clearing price the spark spread is USD 9/MWh before variable O&M, carbon and start-up. At USD 100 the unit earns a large inframarginal rent if it is dispatched, but that is true only when it sits inside the merit order. These are teaching assumptions.

Boundaries: power is hard to store at scale, and short-term imbalance can produce very high or negative prices; negative prices reflect local surplus or minimum-generation constraints, not free electricity. Capacity and energy are separate products, so a capacity payment must not be added to every MWh as if it were energy. Transmission and distribution charges are regulated, and a nodal price is not a retail price; RTO rules, bid units and settlement periods differ.

Nuclear fuel and storage: conversion, enrichment, assemblies and spent fuel

The nuclear fuel cycle has a front end and a back end. The front end includes mining, milling into yellowcake U₃O₈, conversion into UF₆, enrichment from the roughly 0.7% U-235 in natural uranium to 3%–5%, and fabrication of UO₂ pellets into fuel rods and assemblies. The back end includes spent-fuel storage, recycling and final disposal [14][15]. NRC also lists depleted uranium deconversion and mixed-oxide fabrication, but the United States has no commercial reprocessing facility and spent fuel is in interim storage [15].

Mechanically, enrichment is not "purifying uranium"; it separates isotopes using the small mass difference between U-235 and U-238. Commercial plants mainly use gas centrifuges, producing low-enriched uranium (LEU) and a depleted tail stream. Enrichment is priced in separative work units (SWU) and contracted separately from uranium, conversion and fabrication; EIA's uranium marketing report tracks U₃O₈, SWU and assemblies separately, which shows that nuclear fuel cost is not a single "uranium price" [16]. A pressurized-water reactor typically holds 150–200 fuel assemblies, while a boiling-water reactor holds 370–800 [14].

Teaching example: assume 100 pounds of U₃O₈ at USD 50/lb, a uranium feedstock cost of USD 5,000. That covers the feedstock only, not conversion, SWU, assembly fabrication or transport. Treating the uranium price as total fuel cost understates enrichment and fabrication; conversely, a fall in uranium price does not cut generation cost by the same percentage. These are teaching assumptions, and contracts quote pounds U₃O₈, kilograms of uranium, SWU and assemblies separately.

Boundaries: nuclear fuel is not a homogeneous exchange-traded commodity; procurement, licensing, insurance and safeguards create long lead times. Spent fuel is cooled in pools and later moved to dry storage, and storage is not final disposal. Reprocessing policy differs by country: the United States does not reprocess commercially, while France, Japan and others take different paths [15]. Criticality safety, radiation protection and transport rules apply at every step, so a uranium price curve cannot be mapped directly onto assembly cost.

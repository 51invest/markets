Chemical Value Chains: Feedstock, Spreads and Inventory

Chemical value is set not by one price but by spreads across feedstock, processing, grade, region and inventory.

Definition

A chemical value chain links feedstock, basic chemicals and materials: naphtha, natural gas, coal, salt and silica become ethylene, propylene, benzene, chlor-alkali and soda ash, then polymers, fibres, rubber and coatings. A spread is the product price minus feedstock and processing cost; it is the practical signal for whether a plant is worth running [1]. Further downstream, grade, formulation, certification and logistics matter more, so one futures price cannot represent the whole margin.

Intuition

Picture three plants in one chemical park: an integrated site takes ethylene from its own upstream unit, a merchant plant buys ethylene at spot, and a compounding plant turns resin into a customer-specific grade. The ethylene quote is the same, but their economics differ: the first earns the whole-chain spread, the second earns only a processing margin, and the third earns a premium for formulation, certification and small-batch delivery. Before judging a chemical company, ask where it sits in the chain and whether it sells a standard grade or a custom compound; then look at its spread and inventory days.

Mechanism

Spreads are squeezed by three groups of variables: feedstock cost, plant cash cost and realised product price. An integrated plant keeps upstream margin inside the chain, while a merchant buyer is directly exposed to the spot spread. Higher utilisation adds supply and compresses product spreads, but also raises feedstock demand. Inventory amplifies the cycle: falling feedstock values, finished-goods build-ups and delayed contracts make accounting profit diverge from cash flow. Regional spreads must deduct freight, tariffs, storage and FX; two quoted prices cannot simply be subtracted.

Worked example

Suppose an cracker needs 3 tonnes of naphtha per tonne of ethylene. Naphtha costs CNY 600/tonne delivered, ethylene sells at CNY 2,100/tonne, and cash processing cost is CNY 300/tonne. Feedstock cost is CNY 1,800, so the spread is 2,100 − 1,800 − 300 = CNY 0/tonne: the plant covers cash cost but not depreciation. If ethylene falls to CNY 1,950, the spread is −150 and the plant should cut rates or shut down. An integrated polyethylene unit can use an internal transfer price above spot, but the group still faces the polyethylene spread; an internal price cannot create external profit. These numbers are a teaching assumption, not a market quote.

Second worked example: inventory and funding

Suppose a trader buys 1,000 tonnes of naphtha at CNY 600/tonne in January and plans to sell to a cracker three months later. Storage and insurance cost CNY 20/tonne, and financing at 6% a year costs about 600 × 6% × 0.25 = CNY 9/tonne for three months (0.25 is 3/12 of a year). If naphtha falls to CNY 550, the spot loss is CNY 50/tonne; adding CNY 29 of storage and financing gives a total holding loss of CNY 79/tonne, or CNY 79,000. Even if the ethylene spread widens from 0 to 80, the trader need not stockpile: the trader earns feedstock trading margin, not plant processing margin. If naphtha instead rises to CNY 650, the inventory gain is CNY 50/tonne; after CNY 29 of cost, the net gain is CNY 21/tonne — a directional inventory trade, not a stable processing profit. These are teaching assumptions.

Formal expression

The chemical-chain processing spread can be written as spread = product price − feedstock cost − processing cost, and inventory funding use as carry = inventory volume × unit price × funding rate × days / 365. A wider spread does not automatically mean better profit: if feedstock and product prices rise together, inventory revaluation and hedge P&L can obscure the operating spread.

Boundary and common mistakes

First, a spread is not a profit rate: depreciation, maintenance, safety, environmental compliance and working capital are separate. Second, a falling product price does not automatically mean a worse margin; if feedstock falls more, the spread can widen. Third, futures reflect a standard grade and delivery location, while grades, contract prices and regional spot markets can differ. Fourth, inventory gains are positive in rising markets and negative in falling ones; do not extrapolate one up-cycle. Finally, chemical shutdowns and restarts are costly, so exposure cannot be traded like a stock.

Failure modes

The first failure is treating a standard futures price as the realised price for every grade. Futures reflect a deliverable grade and location; customer contracts may include payment terms, freight, formulation secrecy and minimum volumes, so the realised price can be far from the screen. The second is ignoring the fixed cost of shutdown: cutting rates is easy, but restarting, reheating, inspection and off-spec product all cost money, so a briefly negative spread may not justify a shutdown. The third is using an internal transfer price as a substitute for external profit: upstream can sell ethylene cheaply to downstream, but consolidated accounts still need an external sale. The fourth is booking inventory gains as processing margin; the next price decline reverses the cash flow.

Three checks

Before relying on a chemical-chain analysis, calibrate with three questions. First, what is the spread definition: is the product price spot, contract or futures, and does feedstock cost include freight and tariffs? Second, what are inventory days and funding cost, and do storage, insurance and financing consume the gain? Third, where does the plant sit in the chain, and are shutdown fixed cost and restart time included?

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

Chemical chains are often compared with metals, but their cost structures differ. Metal concentrates can be quoted stage by stage through grade, smelting and processing fees; chemical plants often produce co-products. A cracker makes ethylene, propylene and by-products together, so feedstock cost must be allocated by product value or energy content; otherwise an ethylene-only spread overstates or understates the whole chain. A counterexample is 'integration is always more profitable': if an internal transfer price suppresses upstream feedstock, downstream book profit looks good while the group may be subsidising it below market. Judge consolidated cash flow, not one legal entity. Another counterexample is 'higher utilisation is always better': high utilisation compresses product spreads and raises feedstock demand, and the marginal plant's cash contribution can be negative.

Scope and limits

The spread-inventory framework here applies to chemical chains with public quotes, grade-level separation and continuous plant operation. If products lack public quotes, contract prices lag by a month, or plants stop frequently, contract and inventory valuation obscure the spread. It does not apply to unbuilt plants, integrated groups dominated by internal transfer prices, or single-product analysis that ignores by-product revenue. Confirm quote basis, tax and freight, then recompute over at least two cycles; a one-quarter improvement may be feedstock lag, not a trend.

Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Inventory and utilisation" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet.

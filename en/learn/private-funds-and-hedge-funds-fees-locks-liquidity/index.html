Private Funds and Hedge Funds: Fees, Lock-ups and Liquidity

Private funds and hedge funds sell not a stock but a strategy, a fee schedule and a liquidity arrangement. 2/20 fees, lock-ups, side pockets and redemption gates determine net returns and whether investors can exit in a crisis.

What the product is

A hedge fund is usually raised privately and uses long-short, leverage and derivatives with lighter public disclosure. Private funds include private equity, private credit, real estate and infrastructure, with longer horizons and less transparent valuation. Neither is a daily-redeeming mutual fund[1].

Rights and cash flows

Investors own fund interests and distribution rights but usually cannot redeem at will; the manager controls investment decisions and charges management and performance fees. The cash-flow table is:

Annual Management fee 2% of assets CNY Asset size

Numbers are teaching assumptions, not product quotes. Fee terms follow the fund contract[2].

Participants

Manager, investors, custodian, auditor, placement agent, lenders (private credit) and regulators. Hedge funds may have prime brokers; private funds often use LP/GP structures.

Trading, holding and exit

Hedge funds usually have monthly or quarterly redemption windows, lock-ups and gates; private funds hold for 5–10 years and exit through asset sales or secondary transfers. Exit ability depends on terms, not investor intent.

Costs

Management fee, performance fee, fund administration, trading costs, financing and exit fees. Under 2/20, with 10% gross return, a 2% management fee and 1.6% performance fee leave about 6.4% net.

Risks

First, liquidity risk; second, opaque valuation; third, leverage and refinancing; fourth, strategy crowding; fifth, key-person risk; sixth, fee erosion. Historical returns may come from leverage or non-replicable strategies.

Terms and documents

Private placement memorandum, fund contract, high-water mark, lock-up, gate, side pocket, valuation policy and audit report. Check the fee base and clawback terms[3].

Deeper note: fees, high-water marks and liquidity terms

2/20 is not simply '2% a year plus 20%'.

Performance fees usually carry a high-water mark: fees accrue only when net value exceeds the prior peak after fees. If a fund gains 20% then loses 10%, it may still be above the high-water mark and owe no catch-up; if it then gains 15%, fees apply only to the excess above the mark. The mark protects investors from double charging but can push managers to take more risk in underwater years.

Fee drag on net returns is non-linear. With a 10% gross return, 2% management fee and 20% performance fee, net is about 10−2−0.2×8=6.4%; at 5% gross, net is about 5−2−0.2×3=2.4%, with fees taking 52% of gross. Add 1% each for administration, trading and financing and net may fall below 1%. Compare net returns and fee ratios, not gross.

Lock-ups, redemption windows and gates transfer liquidity risk to investors. One-to-three-year locks, quarterly windows and 30-90 days' notice are common; gates let managers return only part of capital under stress. Side pockets hold hard-to-value assets separately, so redeeming investors may receive 'shares that cannot be sold'.

Private-fund valuations are more opaque. Private equity and private credit use model or last-transaction values, smoothing interim volatility; this flatters Sharpe ratios but stress can bring a one-off markdown. Comparing hedge and private funds requires valuation frequency, leverage, liquidity terms and fee structure.

Boundary: fees and terms are contractual, not return forecasts; high fees are not automatically bad and low fees not automatically good. Compare net return, liquidity, valuation credibility and strategy capacity on one table[2][3][4].

Due diligence reads terms, not just returns. Check at least: strategy capacity and team stability; leverage sources and margin-call terms; independent custody and audit; redemption windows, gates and side-pocket triggers; the performance-fee base and high-water mark; related-party dealings and conflicts. The return curve is the result; the terms determine how much investors recover under stress.

Fee structure changes behaviour. A high performance fee encourages tail risk: managers share upside but investors bear downside; without a high-water mark or clawback, a manager may double down after losses. A management fee provides steady income but can make asset gathering outrank returns. The question is not the fee level but whether fees and risk-taking are symmetric.

Liquidity risk is contagious. When several funds face redemptions at once, they sell the most liquid assets, depressing prices and triggering more redemptions; markdowns in private credit can force margin calls. 2008 and March 2020 showed such chains. Stress-test redemption terms and market liquidity together[2][3][4].

Exercises

  1. Fee question: gross return 10%, management fee 2%, performance fee 20%. What is net return?
  2. Liquidity question: a fund has a one-year lock-up and quarterly redemption windows. What may an investor face if cash is needed urgently?
  3. Conditions question: list three risk conditions for private and hedge funds and explain how each affects net return.
  4. Fee question: gross return 5%, management fee 2%, performance fee 20%. What is net return, and what share of gross do fees take?
  5. High-water question: the fund gains 20% then loses 10%. Why might no performance fee be clawed back in year two?

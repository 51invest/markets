Private Equity, Private Credit and Hedge Funds

Hedge funds typically admit only accredited investors, and private credit funds are mostly closed-end and not open to retail investors; like private equity they are limited in liquidity, but their assets, leverage, cash flows and exit routes differ.

What the product is

Private equity, private credit and hedge funds are private funds raised privately and generally not offered to the public. In the United States, hedge fund investors generally must be accredited investors [1]; the status turns on income or asset levels, but the registered page gives no figures, so this article states none and the thresholds must be checked against the rules in force. Private credit funds are financed mainly by institutional investors with long investment horizons and low liquidity needs, and most of them are not available to retail investors (BIS, 2025). Private equity buys private companies and exits through a sale, listing or secondary transaction. Private credit lends directly to small and medium-sized firms. Hedge funds use long/short positions, leverage and short selling. All three have limited liquidity, but their assets, cash flows and exits differ.

Private equity investors commit capital, and the manager calls it as deals are made; in a limited partnership, investors are LPs and the manager is the GP. Private credit funds are often closed-end with lives of five to eight years, do not list on exchanges and are not offered to retail investors (BIS, 2025). Hedge funds often have one-year or longer lock-ups, then periodic redemption windows, and may suspend redemptions in stress (SEC, 2013).

BIS describes private credit as non-bank credit extended directly to small and medium-sized firms, mostly through closed-end funds with five-to-eight-year lives that are not exchange-traded and not sold to retail investors, hence illiquid, with global private-credit fund assets above USD 2.5 trillion and direct lending as the main strategy [3].

Example (hypothetical)

A private equity fund receives $1 million of committed capital and charges a 2% management fee and a 20% performance fee with an 8% hurdle. After five years it has $1.3 million to distribute, so the profit is $300,000. This example treats the 8% hurdle as a one-time preferred return on committed capital ($80,000), with no GP catch-up, so the carry applies to the $220,000 above the hurdle and comes to $44,000; the investor receives $1,256,000 (the $1,000,000 of capital plus $256,000 of profit), before any management fee is deducted from the distribution. In the second example, a $500,000 hedge fund investment earns a 10% gross return, or $50,000. The 2% management fee on assets is $10,000, and the 20% performance fee is charged on the $40,000 gain left after that fee, or $8,000, leaving a net gain of $32,000 and a net return of 6.4%. All figures are assumptions.

Participants and venues

GPs, LPs, placement agents, fund administrators, custodians, auditors and valuation firms participate; hedge funds also use prime brokers, while private credit involves borrowers and loan servicers. Interests are privately placed and not exchange-listed. An SEC fact sheet sets out document requirements for registered private fund advisers: quarterly statements covering fund performance, fees and expenses, an annual audit for each private fund, and a fairness or valuation opinion in an adviser-led secondary transaction [2]. The fact sheet does not state the current legal effect of those rules or their compliance dates, so this article makes no claim about whether they remain in force; the SEC's current releases and the Federal Register text govern.

Trading, holding and exit

Private equity funds are closed-end, so capital is committed for years; exits depend on sales, listings or transfers, and secondary interests may trade at a discount and require manager consent. Private credit loans are often held to maturity, with closed-end fund lives of five to eight years; evergreen or interval funds may offer periodic redemptions subject to limits (BIS, 2025). Hedge funds redeem after lock-ups through periodic windows and may charge a redemption fee or suspend redemptions.

Performance metrics

Two numbers usually appear together, but they answer different questions. Multiple on invested capital (MOIC) is cumulative distributions divided by cumulative contributions and ignores how long the money was out. Internal rate of return (IRR) is the rate r that makes the discounted net of the period cash flows zero; it does discount time, and it implicitly assumes every interim distribution is reinvested at r.

The figures below are hypothetical, not the record of any fund: contribute 100 at time 0, receive 60 in year 3 and 80 in year 5. MOIC = (60 + 80) ÷ 100 = 1.4x. IRR solves 60 ÷ (1 + r)^3 + 80 ÷ (1 + r)^5 = 100, giving r ≈ (8.5%); this figure was obtained by bisection and re-checked by substitution with a residual below 1e-9. With the same 1.4x, if the whole 140 arrived in year 5 the annualised rate would only be the fifth root of 1.4 minus one ≈ 6.96% — same multiple, different timing, different annualised result; conversely, earlier distributions lift IRR even when the final multiple is unchanged.

Fees and definitions have to be read with them: management fees are charged on commitments or contributions and performance fees commonly carry a hurdle and may have a high-water mark (the high-water-mark convention is this article's description of typical terms; the registered pages do not define the term), so one cash-flow set yields both gross and net MOIC and IRR. Registered private fund advisers must give investors quarterly performance and statements of fees and expenses, so whether a reported figure is gross or net is settled by the disclosure [2], and the performance fee itself can push a manager toward more risk [1]. Comparing IRRs across funds needs care: capital-call and distribution timing, and the valuation of unrealised positions, are all in the manager's hands; stated here as method cautions rather than conclusions, this is the article's own framing.

Costs

Management fees are charged on commitments or net assets, plus performance fees on profits, legal, audit, custody and transaction costs. Hedge funds typically charge an annual asset management fee of 1% to 2% of assets plus a performance fee of 20% of the fund's profit, which is usually more than a mutual fund charges, but terms vary by fund; a performance fee can motivate a manager to take greater risks [1]. Investors also bear taxes and opportunity costs.

Risks

Risks include illiquidity, valuation opacity, leverage, concentration, borrower default, failed exits, key-person dependence and regulatory change. On the private credit side, borrowers with negative EBITDA, more debt, weaker accounting transparency and less tangible collateral are more likely to borrow from private credit funds than from banks, and leverage at BDCs and private credit funds has been rising; if structures shift toward open-ended vehicles and more retail money, the liquidity mismatch between assets and liabilities would widen (BIS, 2025). Short selling, derivatives and leverage can magnify hedge-fund losses, and hedge funds often give themselves significant discretion in valuing illiquid securities (SEC, 2013).

Terms and documents

In private funds the binding terms sit in the offering document and partnership agreement. The SEC notes that hedge-fund investors are generally required to be qualified, that redemptions usually open monthly, quarterly or annually with lock-ups of a year or longer being common, that funds may suspend redemptions under stress, and that performance fees can push managers toward more risk [1]. The same SEC fact sheet lists documents a reader can demand and check: quarterly performance plus a fee and expense statement, an annual audit, a fairness or valuation opinion in adviser-led secondary transactions, and limits on certain preferential treatment and conflicts [2]. Whether those requirements apply turns on the rules' current legal effect, addressed under Participants and venues, and this article does not judge it; investors can still request the disclosure materials and check them item by item. For private credit, the BIS describes funds that are mostly closed-end, with lives commonly of 5–8 years, that do not trade on exchanges and are not open to retail investors, and are therefore illiquid [3]. A closed-end structure means exit depends on maturity and asset realisation rather than tendering, so the fee and carry calculation base, the lock-up and redemption-suspension mechanics, and secondary-trading and valuation policy are the terms to read first.

Comparison and boundaries

Type Legal structure Main assets Cash flows Liquidity
Private equity Closed-end limited partnership Private equity Capital calls, exit distributions Very low
Private credit Mostly closed-end funds; BDCs in the United States Direct loans Interest, principal Very low (listed US BDCs higher)
Hedge fund Limited partnership or offshore fund Public securities, derivatives Redemptions Periodic

None is a deposit, and none guarantees principal or return. “Private” does not mean low risk, and “hedge” does not mean risk is eliminated. Listed BDCs in the United States trade on exchanges and are open to retail investors, but the loans underneath them may still be illiquid; the fund documents govern.

Knowledge-point review

venture, growth and buyout

Definition: Venture invests early in high-risk firms, growth funds support expansion, and buyout funds acquire mature companies and improve operations, often with leverage.

Mechanism: Returns come from selection, support, governance and exit; fund life, valuation and financing conditions shape feasibility.

Example: For example, a venture fund backs an early software firm and exits via IPO or sale, while a buyout fund acquires a mature retailer and integrates it.

Boundary: Private strategies are not one asset class; early failure rates are high, buyouts rely on leverage, and opaque valuation and locked exits create liquidity risk.

special situations

Definition: Special situations invest in event-driven, distressed, mispriced or complex capital structures, seeking legal, operational or market recovery.

Mechanism: Returns come from buying at a discount, restructuring, asset sales and catalysts; diligence, law and exit paths determine risk.

Example: For example, a fund buys discounted bad loans and recovers value through restructuring or litigation.

Boundary: Special situations are not simple buy-low-sell-high; information gaps, legal uncertainty, delays and failed catalysts cause losses, and liquidity is poor.

secondaries

Definition: Secondaries buy existing LP interests or company stakes in private funds or projects, providing earlier liquidity.

Mechanism: Pricing uses NAV, discounts, asset quality and remaining life; GP consent, transfer limits and valuation discounts shape deals.

Example: For example, a pension sells part of a private fund stake to a secondary fund, exiting early at a discount.

Boundary: Secondaries are not risk-free liquidity; stale NAVs, concentrated assets and leverage hide risk, and terms and GP conflicts can block deals.

private credit

Definition: Private credit is direct lending by non-bank institutions, often floating-rate with custom covenants and low liquidity.

Mechanism: Returns come from spreads, fees and covenant protection; borrower quality, collateral, covenants and exit determine risk.

Example: For example, a private fund lends to a mid-sized firm at a floating spread with a leverage maintenance covenant.

Boundary: Private credit is not a simple bank-loan substitute; opaque valuation, weak covenants and concentration cause losses, and workouts are hard under stress.

mezzanine and distressed

Definition: Mezzanine sits between senior debt and equity, often with warrants or high coupons; distressed investing targets defaulted, restructuring or bankrupt assets.

Mechanism: Mezzanine depends on cash flow and seniority, distressed on discounts, legal process and recovery; leverage and liquidity risk are high.

Example: For example, a mezzanine fund funds an acquisition with warrants and enters restructuring talks if cash flow deteriorates.

Boundary: Mezzanine is not low-risk equity; seniority, covenants and valuation are complex, and distressed recovery depends on courts, creditors and time.

hedge fund strategies, GP-LP and fees

Definition: Hedge fund strategies include long-short, macro, CTA and relative value; GPs manage funds and LPs provide capital through fees and governance.

Mechanism: Long-short earns selection and leverage, macro bets on rates and FX, CTAs follow trends, and relative value earns spreads; 2/20 fees and lockups affect net returns.

Example: For example, a relative-value fund buys an undervalued bond and shorts an overvalued one, while LPs pay management and performance fees.

Boundary: Strategies are not an asset class; leverage, crowding, liquidity mismatch and style drift cause drawdowns, and fees and gates erode returns while GP incentives can misalign.

Exercises

  1. Performance fee. A $1 million commitment earns $300,000 of profit. The 8% hurdle is a one-time preferred return on committed capital, the performance fee is 20% with no catch-up, and management fees are ignored. How much does the investor keep?
  2. Hedge-fund fees. A $500,000 investment earns a 10% gross return, with a 2% management fee and 20% performance fee. What is the net return?
  3. Private-credit interest. A $10 million loan pays a 4% reference rate plus a 5% spread. What is first-year interest? If principal recovery is 60%, what is the principal loss?
Suggested answers
  1. Hurdle $80,000; excess $220,000; performance fee $44,000; investor keeps $256,000 before management fees.
  2. Management fee $10,000; remaining gain $40,000; performance fee $8,000; net $32,000, or 6.4%.
  3. 9% × $10 million = $900,000; principal loss is 40% × $10 million = $4 million.

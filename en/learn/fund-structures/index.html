What the product is

A fund pools money from many investors and invests it in stocks, bonds, money-market instruments or other assets under a fund contract or charter. Legal forms vary by jurisdiction; this article is limited to funds registered with the U.S. SEC, and other jurisdictions' legal forms are not covered here - covering them would require their own registration documents and rule texts as sources. Among U.S. registered structures, open-end investment companies, closed-end investment companies and unit investment trusts are the common forms [3]. A mutual fund is a registered open-end investment company, and an ETF is usually registered as an open-end investment company or a unit investment trust [1]. The label does not settle the structure; share rights, valuation and exit mechanics define the product.

Dimensions of fund classification

A fund name often stacks several independent dimensions: legal form (open-end, closed-end or unit investment trust), offering method (registered public offering or private placement), management style (active or passive), asset class (equity, bond, money market or hybrid), trading structure (ETF or LOF) and investment layer (fund of funds). One fund can be open-end, publicly offered, active, equity-focused and an ETF at the same time, or it can take only some of these labels; no single dimension substitutes for another. The discussion below uses U.S. SEC-registered funds as the main case and marks LOF separately as a China-specific structure.

Open-end and closed-end: fixed or variable share count

An open-end fund continuously issues and redeems shares with investors, so its share count changes with subscriptions and redemptions, and investors generally transact with the fund at the current or next calculated NAV. A closed-end fund sells a fixed number of shares in an initial public offering and then usually stops continuous issuance and redemption; holders mainly buy and sell on the secondary market[1][9].

The exit channel for an open-end fund is the fund itself: an investor tenders shares back to the fund and receives NAV. To meet daily redemptions, the fund must hold cash and liquid assets; under heavy redemption pressure it may sell portfolio assets, creating transaction costs that remaining holders bear. A closed-end fund lacks that daily redemption channel, so its price is set by secondary-market supply and demand and may sit above NAV (a premium) or below NAV (a discount). Because shares cannot be redeemed at NAV, the discount is not automatically arbitraged away. A closed-end fund also need not reserve the same liquidity for daily redemptions and may have more room to hold illiquid assets or use leverage[9].

Teaching assumptions, not real quotes: an open-end fund has a NAV of 10.00; an investor subscribes 10,000 and receives 1,000 shares before fees. If many holders redeem the next day, the fund sells portfolio assets and remaining holders bear the resulting transaction costs. A closed-end fund has a NAV of 10.00 and a market price of 8.50, a 15% discount; the buyer gets exposure to 10.00 of net assets for 8.50, but the discount may widen to 20%, and leverage magnifies NAV swings[9].

“Daily redeemability” does not mean “costless exit at NAV at any moment”: redemptions may be affected by open days, redemption fees, suspension or market liquidity. A closed-end “discount” is not a free lunch either: the discount may persist or widen, and holders have no right to redeem at NAV unless the fund documents provide periodic repurchases, tender offers or conversion to open-end status. Open-end versus closed-end is a legal and trading structure, not a risk label.

Public and private: who can invest

In the United States, a public fund generally means an investment company that offers securities to the public and registers under the Investment Company Act of 1940. A private fund is a pooled vehicle excluded from the “investment company” definition under Section 3(c)(1) or 3(c)(7); it does not register as an investment company and cannot publicly offer its interests[4].

A registered public fund faces public investors and is subject to registration, disclosure, board oversight, diversification and liquidity rules; an open-end public fund generally handles daily subscriptions and redemptions at NAV. A private fund raises through exempt offerings: a 3(c)(1) fund has no more than 100 beneficial owners, while a 3(c)(7) fund is limited to qualified purchasers. Rule 506(b) or 506(c) of Regulation D are common offering routes; 506(b) generally prohibits general solicitation, while 506(c) permits broad solicitation but requires verification of investor eligibility. A private-fund adviser generally must register or qualify for an exemption[4]. Private funds may have lock-ups, redemption limits, leverage and performance allocations, with less frequent disclosure and valuation than registered funds; antifraud provisions still apply[4].

Teaching assumptions: a 3(c)(1) fund has 90 beneficial owners; admitting 20 more could exceed the 100-owner limit, so it must stop raising or change structure. A registered open-end fund can offer shares to the public without the 100-owner cap, but bears heavier daily-redemption and disclosure duties. A 3(c)(7) fund does not use the 100-owner cap and instead requires qualified purchasers[4].

“Private” does not mean unregulated: securities antifraud rules, adviser registration or exemption and offering-exemption conditions still apply. “Public” does not mean government-owned or principal-protected. Hedge funds and private equity funds are types of private funds named by strategy or asset class, not separate legal forms. Across jurisdictions, “public” and “private” labels do not map one-to-one: China’s public-fund and private-fund regulatory categories differ from the U.S. registered/private-fund categories, so compare only after aligning the jurisdiction.

Active and passive: who picks the portfolio

An actively managed fund has a manager select securities, time the market or run a strategy, aiming to beat a benchmark or achieve a specified absolute return. A passive or index fund aims to track the return of a market index, usually by replicating its constituents or sampling them[5].

Active management usually involves more research, trading and turnover and may carry a higher expense ratio; the portfolio can deviate from the benchmark, so it can outperform or underperform. A passive fund holds according to index rules, usually with lower turnover and a lower expense ratio, but it is not “zero cost”: it still bears management fees, trading costs, index licensing fees, cash drag and sampling error. When the index reconstitutes, the fund must trade along, which can create costs. A passive fund tracks an index, not the index itself, and cannot guarantee full replication[5].

Teaching assumption: an index holds only stocks A and B at 50% each. Over a year A rises 100% and B is flat, so the index rises 50%. A fully replicating passive fund returns about 50% before costs; an active fund holding only B returns about 0%, while one holding only A returns about 100%. If the passive fee is 0.05% and the active fee is 1.50%, the 1.45-percentage-point fee gap cannot explain the 0-versus-100% return gap. The numbers are teaching assumptions, not real fund performance.

Passive is not low-risk: an index can be concentrated, expensive or falling, and tracking error and premiums or discounts remain. Active does not guarantee outperformance: active management can lag, and a fund labelled active may hug its benchmark. Smart-beta or non-traditional index funds still track custom rules-based indexes rather than a traditional market-cap index. Active versus passive is a management style, not a legal form, and can appear in open-end funds, closed-end funds and ETFs[5].

Equity, bond, money-market and hybrid: asset class is not a legal form

By main asset class, funds are often grouped into equity funds, bond funds, money-market funds and hybrid or balanced funds. Equity funds invest mainly in stocks; bond funds mainly in bonds or other debt instruments; money-market funds in high-quality short-term debt, cash and cash equivalents; hybrid funds in both stocks and bonds, sometimes with money-market instruments[1][6].

The return drivers differ. Equity funds are driven mainly by earnings, valuation and market risk; bond funds by coupons, interest rates, credit spreads and defaults; money-market funds by short-term rates and liquidity. Government and retail money-market funds seek a stable $1.00 NAV, while institutional prime and institutional tax-exempt money-market funds use a floating NAV[6]. A hybrid fund combines equity and bond exposures, so its risk and return are a weighted mix of the underlying asset risks, not a simple sum[1].

Teaching assumption: a hybrid fund holds 60% stocks and 40% bonds. Stocks fall 20% and bonds rise 5%, so the portfolio return is about 0.6×(-20%)+0.4×5% = -10%. A money-market fund with a $1.00 NAV and 10,000 invested would distribute about 400 over a year if the annual yield were 4% and stayed constant; yields move with short-term rates, and a stable NAV is not a guarantee against “breaking the buck”[6]. The numbers are teaching assumptions.

An asset-class label is neither a return guarantee nor a legal structure. A bond fund is not automatically low-risk: long duration, credit risk and leverage can amplify losses. A money-market fund is not a bank deposit, is not FDIC-insured, and its stable NAV can break. A hybrid fund’s stock/bond mix can drift with the market or strategy, and “balanced” does not guarantee a fixed ratio. When a U.S. registered fund’s name suggests a type of investment, it generally must invest at least 80% of assets in that type, but may still hold up to about one-fifth in other securities, so the name cannot replace the prospectus[1]. One fund can be equity, active, public and open-end at the same time; asset class is only one dimension.

ETF: an exchange-traded open-end structure

An ETF is a fund listed on an exchange, usually registered as an open-end investment company or a unit investment trust. Its shares represent a proportionate interest in the portfolio; retail investors buy and sell at market prices on the secondary market, and only authorized participants can create or redeem creation units with the fund[1][2].

An ETF has two prices: the exchange’s intraday market price and the fund’s daily NAV. Authorized participants create or redeem creation units with a basket of securities and/or cash. When the market price is above NAV, an arbitrageur can buy the basket, create shares and sell them on the exchange; when the price is below NAV, it can buy shares and redeem them for the basket or cash. This mechanism usually compresses premiums and discounts, but not always: authorized participants may withdraw, short selling or securities borrowing may be constrained, and closed foreign markets, basket costs or a creation suspension can keep the gap open[2]. An ETF may track an index or be actively managed; it may hold stocks, bonds, commodities or other assets, so ETF is not synonymous with “index fund”.

Teaching assumption: an ETF has a NAV of 4.00 and a market price of 4.08, a 2% premium; the creation unit is 50,000 shares and the basket NAV is 200,000. An authorized participant creates 50,000 shares at NAV and sells at the market price; ignoring fees and market impact, the gross profit is about 4,000 (50,000×0.08). If the premium disappears before the trade settles, the profit can turn into a loss. The numbers are teaching assumptions, not real quotes.

An ETF is not the index itself, does not guarantee full replication and does not guarantee that premiums or discounts close. An ETF is not an ETN: an ETF holds portfolio assets, while an ETN is an issuer’s debt. An ETF’s authorized participants are not retail investors’ agents and have no duty to keep creating or redeeming. A leveraged or inverse ETF’s daily reset target can diverge from the underlying multiple over longer holding periods. Read the ETF’s legal structure, asset class and management style separately[2].

LOF: China’s listed open-end fund

A LOF (listed open-end fund) is an open-end fund in China: its total share count is not fixed, and investors can subscribe, redeem and trade on-exchange through exchange members, or subscribe and redeem off-exchange through sales institutions[7]. It remains an open-end fund in substance, not a closed-end fund and not an ETF.

A LOF has two pricing channels: on-exchange or off-exchange subscription and redemption are priced at the day’s fund NAV, while exchange trading is priced by matching orders. For an Shanghai LOF, on-exchange subscription is submitted by amount, must be a multiple of CNY 1 and at least CNY 1,000; redemption is submitted by shares and must be in whole shares; the on-exchange subscription or redemption price is the day’s fund NAV; shares obtained by subscription cannot be sold, redeemed or transferred on the business confirmation date[8]. When the exchange price is above NAV, an investor may try to subscribe at NAV and sell; when it is below NAV, an investor may buy and redeem at NAV. Confirmation, transfer, fees, quotas and liquidity make the arbitrage neither immediate nor guaranteed.

Teaching assumption: a LOF has a NAV of 1.200 and an exchange price of 1.260, a 5% premium. An investor subscribes 12,000 at NAV and receives 10,000 shares before fees; selling at 1.260 would produce 12,600 and a gross profit of 600. But the shares cannot be sold before confirmation, and the price may fall in the meantime. If the investor buys 10,000 shares for 12,600 and redeems at a NAV of 1.200, the gross loss is 600. The numbers are teaching assumptions and ignore fees, timing and quotas.

LOF is a China-specific category and cannot be pasted into the U.S. SEC-registered fund forms. It is not an ETF: an ETF generally uses authorized participants to create and redeem creation units, while a LOF allows on-exchange and off-exchange subscription and redemption at NAV plus exchange matching. A LOF premium or discount can persist, and arbitrage is limited by subscription confirmation, transfer, fees, suspension, minimum amounts and liquidity; the exchange price can move far from NAV and trading can be thin. Treating a LOF as “an ETF that can always be arbitraged at NAV” is a common mistake.

Fund of funds: a fund that invests in other funds

A fund of funds (FOF) invests its assets in shares of other funds rather than directly in a basket of stocks or bonds. The top-tier fund holds shares of underlying funds, and the underlying funds hold securities or other assets[10].

A FOF adds a layer of “fund portfolio” diversification: one investment can reach several underlying funds, managers and strategies. The cost is layered fees: the top-tier fund charges a management fee, and the underlying funds also charge management and other fees. U.S. registered FOF regulation focuses on “excessive layering of fees”; application structures commonly have the top-tier adviser waive certain compensation received from underlying funds and require the board to find that top-tier and underlying services are not duplicative[10]. Section 12(d)(1) of the U.S. Investment Company Act of 1940 limits a registered fund’s investment in other investment companies, and a FOF structure usually relies on that section, a rule or an exemptive application. Analysis should look through to underlying holdings, fees and liquidity, not just the top-tier name[10].

Teaching assumption: a FOF has assets of 100 and a top-tier management fee of 0.30%; it puts 60 into an equity fund charging 0.80% and 40 into a bond fund charging 0.50%. Underlying fees are about 0.6×0.80%+0.4×0.50%=0.68%, so total cost is about 0.98% with the top-tier fee. If both underlying funds concentrate in the same large technology stocks, the apparent “diversification” may be duplicated exposure. The numbers are teaching assumptions, not real FOF fees.

A FOF is not “diversification that cannot lose”: underlying funds may overlap and fall together, and look-through exposure may be concentrated. Layered fees drag on long-term returns. If underlying funds are illiquid, the top-tier FOF’s daily redemptions may face a liquidity mismatch; Section 12(d)(1) limits and exemptions shape which underlying funds are eligible. A FOF can be active or passive, open-end or closed-end, and can invest in ETFs or other registered funds; “fund of funds” is an investment layer, not a legal form. China’s public FOF follows China-specific fund rules, and U.S. Section 12(d)(1) analysis cannot be applied directly.

Rights, obligations and cash flows

A fund share represents a proportionate interest in the fund's assets. Holders share gains and bear investment losses; they do not directly own the companies in the portfolio and are not creditors of the fund sponsor. The adviser and custodian act under the fund documents.

What a share entitles the holder to follows Investor.gov: a mutual fund is managed by an investment adviser registered with the SEC, shares represent proportional ownership of the fund’s portfolio, investors purchase and redeem shares from the fund, and the price is computed from net asset value [2].

Participants and venues

Holders are the ultimate beneficiaries of fund assets. A board or trustee supervises the fund on their behalf; an investment adviser manages the portfolio; a custodian holds assets in custody; a transfer agent records shares; distributors and brokers sell shares. ETFs add authorized participants, exchanges and clearing houses. Mutual funds generally issue and redeem with the fund in the primary market; ETFs trade on an exchange in the secondary market; closed-end fund shares trade on an exchange and usually are not redeemed daily.

Trading, holding and exit

Mutual funds sell and redeem with the fund at the next calculated NAV, so the investor does not know the price when the order is placed. Retail ETF investors buy and sell at market prices on an exchange, and only authorized participants can create or redeem shares in creation units with the fund. Closed-end funds usually do not redeem continuously; holders exit by selling on the secondary market, possibly above or below NAV. A fund may merge, liquidate or suspend redemptions under its documents; the prospectus governs.

Costs

Costs include management fees, custody fees, distribution or service fees, sales loads, redemption fees, brokerage commissions, bid-ask spreads and taxes. Fees deducted from fund assets reduce NAV; ETFs also have premiums or discounts. Fee and tax treatment vary by jurisdiction, fund documents, share class and investor account; compare funds on the same fee, holding-period and tax basis.

The open-end structure, fees and risk list follow FINRA: mutual funds are generally bought and redeemed daily at net asset value, costs include sales charges and annual operating expenses, and risks include market, inflation, interest-rate and liquidity risk[3].

Risks

Funds do not guarantee principal and are not protected by deposit insurance; for example, U.S. FDIC insurance does not cover funds. Risks include market, credit, interest-rate, liquidity, concentration, tracking, counterparty, leverage and contractual risk. An ETF may trade at a premium or discount; a closed-end or illiquid fund may be hard to exit at NAV. Past performance does not predict future returns.

Terms and documents

Read the prospectus, fund contract or charter, custody agreement and periodic reports. Check the objective, investment scope, share classes, fee table, valuation, distributions, redemption and suspension terms, liquidation and custody arrangements. Cross-border funds add currency, time-zone and local-regulatory differences.

Comparison and boundaries

Mutual funds, ETFs and closed-end funds are different legal and trading structures, not deposits, bonds or insurance. Investors redeem mutual fund shares with the fund at NAV; an ETF trades at market price and uses authorized participants for creation and redemption; a closed-end fund usually has a fixed share count and trades mainly in the secondary market. Do not judge risk from the name or the word "NAV" alone.

Knowledge-point review

open-end and closed-end funds

Definition: Open-end funds issue and redeem shares at NAV with variable size, while closed-end funds have fixed shares traded on exchanges.

Mechanism: Open-end liquidity comes from the fund; closed-end prices are set by supply and demand and can trade at premiums or discounts that converge at maturity or opening.

Example: For example, an open-end money fund redeems daily, while a closed-end fund may trade at a discount until expiry.

Boundary: Closed-end is not automatically cheap; discounts can reflect fees, governance and illiquidity, premiums occur, open-end redemption has costs and delays, and liquidity is not free.

public and private funds

Definition: Public funds raise from the public with stricter regulation and disclosure, while private funds raise from qualified investors with limits and less disclosure.

Mechanism: Public funds stress protection, liquidity and diversification; private funds stress flexibility, lockups and thresholds, with different fees, leverage and regulation.

Example: For example, a public equity fund values and discloses daily, while a private fund reports by contract with a lockup.

Boundary: Private is not guaranteed high return; illiquidity, leverage and opacity add risk, public is not low risk, and suitability and conflicts matter for both.

active and passive funds

Definition: Active funds select and time to beat a benchmark, while passive funds replicate an index.

Mechanism: Active relies on research, information and execution with higher fees and manager/style risk; passive is cheap and transparent but carries concentration and tracking error.

Example: For example, an active fund overweights sectors, while an index fund holds all constituents by weight.

Boundary: Active does not guarantee outperformance; most lag long term and fees and size erode, passive is not risk-free, and index bubbles, concentration and rule flaws matter.

equity, bond, money-market and mixed funds

Definition: Equity funds invest mainly in stocks, bond funds in bonds, money-market funds in short liquid instruments, and mixed funds across assets.

Mechanism: Risk and return follow asset weights; equities offer long-run growth with volatility, bonds income and diversification, money funds near-cash, and mixed funds adjust to goals.

Example: For example, a retirement mix balances stocks and bonds, while short-term cash sits in a money fund.

Boundary: Categories are not risk labels; funds can drift, use leverage and derivatives, bonds carry rate and credit risk, and money funds can break the buck or face liquidity stress.

exchange-traded funds

Definition: ETFs are exchange-traded open-end funds, usually tracking an index, with creation and redemption by authorised participants.

Mechanism: Arbitrage keeps price near NAV; fees, tracking error, spreads and underlying liquidity drive cost, and leveraged and inverse ETFs reset daily.

Example: For example, a broad ETF trades intraday at low cost for long-term allocation and tactical shifts.

Boundary: An ETF is not a stock; premiums, liquidity, synthetic structures and leverage add risk, thematic ETFs can concentrate, and leveraged ETFs are path-dependent.

listed open-end funds

Definition: LOFs are open-end funds that can be redeemed off-exchange and traded on an exchange, combining open and closed features.

Mechanism: They have an exchange price and an off-exchange NAV; arbitrage depends on redemption speed, fees and limits, and premiums or discounts can persist.

Example: For example, an investor subscribes at NAV off-exchange, transfers to the exchange and sells at a premium.

Boundary: LOF arbitrage is not risk-free; transfer time, subscription limits and liquidity can erase or reverse the spread, small LOFs are volatile, and fees erode gains.

funds of funds

Definition: A fund of funds invests in other funds, providing diversification, allocation and manager selection.

Mechanism: FOF reduces single-fund and manager risk but adds double fees, delay and transparency issues; target-date, pension and allocation FOFs are common.

Example: For example, a pension FOF allocates across equity, bond and money funds and de-risks toward the target date.

Boundary: FOF is not free diversification; double fees drag returns, underlying funds can overlap and drift, flows and valuation lag, and selection can chase performance.

ETNs and other ETP structures

Definition: An ETN is an issuer obligation, secured or unsecured, tracking an index; ETP is the umbrella for ETFs, ETNs, ETCs and ETMFs.

Mechanism: ETNs are credit exposure to the issuer, not fund assets; ETPs differ in structure, collateral, redemption and tax, and leverage and commodities are common.

Example: For example, an ETN tracks a volatility index, and weakening issuer credit can prevent full recovery.

Boundary: An ETN is not an ETF; no underlying asset segregation, issuer default and issuance suspension risk are high, leveraged ETPs reset daily and are path-dependent, and commodity ETCs carry custody and roll costs.

Data and institutional check: fund structures

Before using "fund structures" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns open-end and closed-end funds.

Third, the institutional boundary. net asset value is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: fund structures

Learning "fund structures" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "open-end and closed-end funds": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "net asset value" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of fund structures still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind open-end and closed-end funds reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "fund structures" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "open-end and closed-end funds" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Numerical boundary

CNY 1m over five years: a low-fee fund charges 0.3%, a high-fee fund 1.5%, a 1.2-point gap and about 60k in fees (before compounding). The high-fee fund must outperform by 1.2 points a year just to break even; few active funds do so consistently.

The boundary is that fees are only one cost; subscription, redemption, tax and tracking error also matter. Low fee does not mean low risk. These are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

The following teaching assumptions use the fund's pricing currency; the numbers are not quotes from any real fund.

  1. Subscription shares. A mutual fund has a NAV of 1.2500; the investor pays 10,000, the subscription fee is 100, and the net subscription is 9,900. How many shares are purchased?
  2. Premium. An ETF has a NAV of 4.00 and a market price of 4.08. What is the premium? If the creation unit is 50,000 shares, what is the NAV value of the basket?
  3. Structure boundary. A closed-end fund has a NAV of 10.00 and a market price of 9.00. What is the discount? Can a holder require the fund to redeem at NAV?
Suggested answers
  1. 9,900 ÷ 1.2500 = 7,920 shares.
  2. Premium = (4.08 - 4.00) ÷ 4.00 = 2%. Basket NAV value = 50,000 × 4.00 = 200,000 in the fund's pricing currency.
  3. Discount = (10.00 - 9.00) ÷ 10.00 = 10%. A closed-end fund usually does not redeem at NAV; the holder sells in the secondary market unless the documents provide otherwise.
  1. Concept check. Explain "fund structures" in no more than three sentences and give a small case where it does not apply.
  2. Mechanism chain. Write the transmission from "fund structures" to "open-end and closed-end funds" in three steps and name the step most dependent on an unstated assumption.

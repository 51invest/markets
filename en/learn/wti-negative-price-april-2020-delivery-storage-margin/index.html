WTI Negative Prices in April 2020: Delivery, Storage and Margin

The May contract fell from $17.73 to −$37.63 one day before expiry. Put storage, delivery rules and margin on one timeline to see that a negative price was not “free oil” but a position that could not deliver.

Background and timeline

Global oil was already oversupplied in early 2020; the pandemic collapsed demand, refinery runs and jet-fuel use. Cushing, Oklahoma, is the delivery point for the WTI futures contract. Crude kept flowing in and available storage fell fast.

20 April 2020 was the last trading day for the May contract; a long that did not close would have to take physical delivery[4]. At the open on 20 April, the May contract traded near $17.73 per barrel; the settlement fell to −$37.63, with an intraday low of −$40.32. The May–June spread widened from −$6.76 the previous day to −$58.06.

The next day, the May contract settled at $10.01 at expiry, showing that the negative price was a local, pre-expiry phenomenon, not the long-run value of crude[4]. On that day, a long in the May contract had two choices: close the position, or accept delivery and find storage. With storage scarce, closing was the only feasible action; when everyone had to close at once, bids disappeared and the price could go below zero.

A negative price means the seller pays the buyer to take the barrel because storage and disposal cost more than the barrel is worth.

Information available and actors

The main actors were the NYMEX WTI contract, the CME clearing house, traders holding May longs, the owners of Cushing storage, and brokers handling client margin and orders. CME allowed negative prices on 20 April after notifying regulators, so price could be discovered below zero instead of being stopped at zero by the rules[4]. The long’s decision was not “bullish or bearish” but “can I find storage before expiry?” Without storage, the position is no longer an asset but a liability: every barrel carries storage, transport and disposal cost.

Shorts knew longs could not deliver and could press the price below zero before expiry. That is not manipulation; it is the price of a delivery constraint. The broker’s decision mattered too.

If the trading system could not display or accept negative prices, clients could not enter negative limit orders or compute margin correctly; the system treated “negative price” as an error. Poor technical preparation turned market risk into operational risk and amplified losses.

Outcome and the number chain

Key numbers: $17.73 open, −$37.63 settlement, −$40.32 intraday low; May–June spread from −$6.76 to −$58.06; final expiry settlement $10.01; expiry on 21 April[4]. Put them in a chain: stocks rise → available storage falls → longs cannot deliver → closing becomes concentrated → bids disappear → price goes below zero → margin and system stress rise. The price fell about $55 per barrel while the spread widened about $51, showing that near-month relative weakness was the core, not the global average price of crude.

A negative price is not a universal “$37.63 paid per barrel”; it is the settlement of a specific contract, at a specific delivery point, on a specific expiry. Treating the May contract as the spot price leads to “oil is worth less than nothing”; the correct reading is “the marginal value of a Cushing-delivered May position before expiry was negative”.

Mechanism

The first layer is storage. WTI is physically delivered and Cushing capacity is finite; when stocks approach capacity, new positions have nowhere to go. The second is expiry: futures must be closed or delivered, and options shrink as the date approaches.

The third is margin: higher volatility raises margin requirements, forcing position cuts, which push prices lower[4]. The three layers form a feedback loop: price falls → margin rises → longs close → bids shrink → price falls further. A negative price is the end of that loop, not the start.

With ample storage or cash settlement, the loop would not end below zero.

Counterfactuals and alternative explanations

First, with ample Cushing storage, longs could take delivery and store, so the price would not go negative; it would stop near the present value of storage cost. Second, if the May contract were cash-settled against a spot index, holders would not need storage at expiry, and negative prices would not appear in the same way. Third, if CME had not allowed negative prices, the price would be truncated near zero, but longs still could not close; risk would move to over-the-counter deals, defaults and clearing.

Fourth, if broker systems had supported negative prices and negative margin earlier, clients could have traded sooner and the loss distribution would differ.

Limits to generalization

The case can show that a physically delivered futures contract, tight delivery storage, a near expiry and concentrated positioning can produce negative prices; a negative price is the price of a delivery constraint, not proof that the commodity’s intrinsic value is negative. It also shows how trading systems, margin and clearing can amplify or buffer price moves. It cannot show that all crude futures will go negative; that negative prices mean oil is worthless; that other commodities can be treated identically; or that the 20 April settlement was the 2020 spot price.

Different delivery points, contracts and settlement rules produce different outcomes.

Reproduction checklist

Reproduce by confirming the contract code, expiry and delivery point; then check storage, inventories and spreads; then list trading rules (negative prices allowed, margin method); finally list broker system limits. Missing any one turns a negative price into “market failure” or “conspiracy” instead of pricing under a delivery constraint[4]. Decision review

From a trading desk, the question on 20 April was not “where will oil go” but “can the position find storage before expiry?” A long that closed at the open limited the loss; one that waited until the afternoon found no bids and could close far below the open. A short that knew longs could not deliver could press the price below zero, because the seller pays the buyer to take the barrel. There was no “malice”, only a game under a delivery constraint.

From the clearing house, negative prices required systems that handle negative margin, negative equity and negative limit orders. If the system still assumed prices above zero, clients could not place correct orders, margin would be miscalculated and the clearing house would underestimate exposure. Technology is not a back-office detail; it is part of market infrastructure.

From the regulator, allowing negative prices let the market discover the real constraint rather than creating negative prices. If rules had banned them, the price would be truncated near zero, but longs still could not close; risk would move to over-the-counter deals, defaults and clearing. The choice is not “negative prices or not” but “discover the risk or hide it”.

Futures Contracts and Margin

Futures are exchange-standardized contracts; both sides post margin and are marked to market daily, so leverage, margin calls and delivery rules shape losses and exits.

ModuleMKT-M13 · 远期与期货
LevelFoundation
Read10 min · 15 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

By the end of this article, you can use the contract multiplier, margin lines, and basis to judge a futures position's profit and loss, when a margin call comes, and what a roll actually costs.

What the product is

A futures contract is an exchange-standardized agreement to buy or sell an underlying on a future date at an agreed price. Price and quantity are fixed at the trade; contracts are written to be fulfilled by actual delivery, some allow cash settlement instead, and most positions are closed before delivery. In exchange-cleared futures the clearinghouse stands between the original counterparties and becomes the central counterparty to both sides (this is this article's description of market structure; the three registered sources do not state it); both sides post margin and positions are adjusted daily to the closing market value [1][2]. A forward is a customized OTC agreement with a different legal structure and counterparty risk.

Rights, obligations and cash flows

Below maintenance Margin call Top up to initial margin Contract settlement currency If the call is missed, the position may be liquidated
Close or expiry Final profit and margin return Realized profit or loss Contract settlement currency Amount depends on the closing price, net of fees
Delivery Delivery payment Per contract Currency set by the contract Physical or cash settlement per the contract

The long is obligated to buy and the short to sell; either side can close out. Margin is not a down payment and does not confer ownership. CME notes that futures margin is typically 3–12% of notional, that initial margin is set under CME Clearing requirements and a broker may require more, and that maintenance margin is the account minimum: falling below it can trigger a margin call, which requires adding funds immediately to restore the account to the initial margin level, and possible automatic liquidation [2]. Losses may exceed the initial deposit. The CFTC notes that customer funds must be segregated from the firm's own funds and that accounts are adjusted to each trading day's closing value.

Margin example

Assume a hypothetical equity index futures contract quoted at 4,000 with a $50 multiplier, so notional is 4,000 × 50 = $200,000. Initial margin at 6% is $12,000 and maintenance at 4% is $8,000. A 2% fall (4,000 × 2% = 80 points) produces a loss of 80 × 50 = $4,000, cutting equity to 12,000 - 4,000 = $8,000, which is exactly the maintenance level, the account minimum rather than a breach of it. Once equity falls below $8,000 a margin call follows, and restoring the $12,000 initial margin takes a deposit of at least $4,000. These figures are hypothetical; actual requirements vary by market and broker.

Participants and venues

Participants include hedgers, speculators, arbitrageurs, producers, consumers, funds, futures commission merchants, exchanges and clearinghouses. Exchanges set contract specifications and the market is regulated [1]; the clearinghouse's role as central counterparty is this article's description of market structure, which none of the three registered sources states directly.

Trading, holding and exit

Positions can be closed, delivered or cash settled, or rolled. For ICE US Dollar Index futures, trading ceases at 10:16 Eastern time two days before settlement, with March, June, September and December contract months [3]. Open positions at expiry follow exchange delivery or settlement rules.

Basis and rolls: basis = spot price − futures price. The numbers below are a teaching assumption, not a quote from any market. With spot at 4,000 per tonne and the near contract at 4,120, basis = 4,000 − 4,120 = −120 per tonne (the futures trade at a premium). To move a position from the near to the farther month, close the near at 4,120 and buy the farther at 4,240: for the same quantity that costs 4,240 − 4,120 = 120 per tonne for each extra period held, or 1,200 per 10-tonne lot, before commissions and slippage. If the farther month were instead cheaper than the near month, the same roll would earn a positive roll return. Basis and rolls are a price structure, not a fee line, and convergence in either direction is not guaranteed; contract months, last trading days and charges follow the exchange rules.

Costs and risks

Costs include commissions, exchange and clearing fees, bid-offer spreads, margin funding, rolls, market impact and taxes. Risks include leverage, margin calls and liquidation, price gaps, basis, liquidity, delivery, futures commission merchant credit and operational risk.

Terms and documents

Check exchange contract specifications, clearing rules, the margin schedule and the customer agreement; confirm the multiplier, tick, last trading day, delivery quality and settlement method. Rules differ by exchange and jurisdiction.

Comparison and boundaries

Instrument Structure Margin Counterparty
Futures Standardized, centrally cleared Initial/maintenance margin, daily mark-to-market Clearinghouse
Forward Customized OTC Collateral or credit line Counterparty
ETF Fund shares Paid in full in a cash account, or margin posted in a securities account Fund and custodian

Futures are neither spot nor an ETF; margin is not a down payment, and leverage magnifies both gains and losses.

Knowledge-point review

commodity derivatives

Definition: Commodity derivatives use physical crude, metals, crops and power as underlyings for hedging, speculation and price discovery.

Mechanism: Prices reflect inventories, transport, seasonality and geopolitics; futures curves, basis and delivery rules determine hedge effectiveness.

Example: For example, an airline buys crude futures to lock fuel costs, but fuel differing from the benchmark leaves basis risk.

Boundary: Commodities are not one asset class; storability, delivery location, quality and freight make each market distinct, and financialisation changes inventories and volatility.

equity index derivatives

Definition: Equity index derivatives use a stock index as underlying, including futures, options and swaps for direction, hedging and enhancement.

Mechanism: Prices reflect constituents, dividends, rates and risk premia; futures discounts or premiums reflect carry and dividends.

Example: For example, a manager sells index futures to hedge a portfolio, but a different composition leaves tracking error.

Boundary: An index is not a single stock; sector weights, size and dividends make hedges imperfect, and leverage, margin and liquidity amplify losses under stress.

government bond derivatives

Definition: Government bond derivatives use sovereign bonds or rates as underlyings, including futures, options and total-return swaps for duration management and hedging.

Mechanism: Prices reflect duration, the yield curve, policy and deliverable bonds; the cheapest-to-deliver and conversion factors drive futures basis.

Example: For example, a bank buys bond futures to hedge portfolio duration, but curve reshaping makes the hedge deviate.

Boundary: Bond futures are not cash bonds; deliverable choice, basis and margin create tracking differences, and low sovereign risk is not zero risk.

short-rate derivatives

Definition: Short-rate derivatives reference interbank, policy or compounded overnight rates, including rate futures, OIS and interest-rate swaps.

Mechanism: Prices reflect the policy path, term premium and basis; the OIS-IBOR spread was a major benchmark risk.

Example: For example, a bank uses an OIS swap to hedge floating liabilities, but funding on another benchmark leaves basis.

Boundary: Short rates are not risk-free; benchmark reform, collateral and liquidity create basis, and curve expectations can reverse quickly.

FX derivatives

Definition: FX derivatives include forwards, swaps, options and cross-currency swaps for hedging, funding and macro views.

Mechanism: Prices reflect spot, rate differentials, volatility and credit; delivery, principal exchange and counterparty risk differ.

Example: For example, an importer buys euro forwards to lock payment cost, forgoing a cheaper price if the euro falls.

Boundary: FX derivatives are not spot substitutes; controls, basis, margin and counterparty default cause losses, and emerging-market liquidity can vanish.

volatility, digital assets and margin

Definition: Volatility derivatives trade future volatility rather than direction, digital-asset derivatives use tokens as underlyings, and margin is performance collateral.

Mechanism: Volatility indexes, variance swaps and option combinations reflect expectations; crypto's high volatility, 24-hour trading and custody risk change margin.

Example: For example, an investor buys volatility futures to hedge large equity moves, but the curve and roll create decay.

Boundary: Volatility is not price direction; crypto regulation, custody and liquidity risk are high, and margin calls and liquidation amplify losses in extremes.

derivative settlement

Definition: Derivative settlement includes daily marking, margin transfers, delivery or cash settlement, the final performance of a contract.

Mechanism: Central counterparties, clearing members and collateral form the chain; cash, physical and net settlement carry different risks.

Example: For example, futures mark to a daily settlement price, profits can be withdrawn and losses topped up, and positions can be closed or delivered.

Boundary: Settlement is not back-office detail; margin models, haircuts, default handling and deliverable shortages create contagion, and OTC contracts carry legal and counterparty risk.

basis

Definition: Basis is the difference between spot and futures prices, reflecting transport, storage, quality, financing and convenience yield.

Mechanism: Basis changes determine hedge effectiveness; contango, backwardation and regional spreads make the same commodity differ by location.

Example: For example, a farmer sells corn futures, and a wider local premium means the effective sale price exceeds futures, improving the hedge.

Boundary: Basis is not mispricing; storability, delivery months and inventories make it move, hedgers bear basis risk, and futures are not spot.

rolls and delivery

Definition: Rolling closes a near contract and moves to a later one, while delivery transfers physical or cash value under contract rules.

Mechanism: The futures curve determines roll yield or decay; deliverable grades, warehouses, receipts and notices shape deliverability and cost.

Example: For example, crude futures roll monthly, and a contango curve imposes cost on long positions, while expiry allows cash or physical delivery.

Boundary: Rolling is not costless; curve shape, liquidity and delivery limits cause decay or squeezes, and ordinary investors should usually avoid delivery.

Exercises

  1. Notional value. At 4,000 points and a $50 multiplier, what is one contract's notional value?
  2. Price fall. If price falls 2% (80 points), what is the long's loss?
  3. Margin call. With 6% initial and 4% maintenance margin, what is equity after the 2% fall? Is there a call, and how much must be deposited?
Suggested answers
  1. 4,000 × 50 = $200,000. Hypothetical, excluding fees.
  2. 80 × 50 = $4,000, or 2% of the $200,000 notional.
  3. Initial margin is $12,000; after the $4,000 loss equity is $8,000, which equals maintenance margin — the account minimum, not yet a breach of it. A further fall below $8,000 triggers a call, and restoring $12,000 takes a deposit of at least $4,000. Hypothetical; actual requirements differ.

Options: Rights, Obligations and Payoffs

An option buyer pays a premium for a right, while the writer receives the premium and owes performance; strike, premium and expiry price determine the payoff.

What the product is

An option buyer pays a premium for the right, but not the obligation, to buy (a call) or sell (a put) the underlying at the strike price on or before expiry. The writer receives the premium and must perform on exercise. Contract specifications set multiplier, expiry, strike, exercise style and settlement. A standard equity option usually represents 100 shares and expires on the third Friday of the month [1]; index options of this kind are usually settled in cash for the difference (the cash-settlement mechanism is from [2]; grouping it under index options is this article's own generalisation).

Rights, obligations and cash flows

Role Right or obligation Maximum gain Maximum loss
Call buyer Right to buy at the strike Theoretically unlimited Premium
Naked call writer Obligation to sell at the strike Premium Theoretically unlimited
Put buyer Right to sell at the strike Strike minus premium Premium
Put writer Obligation to buy at the strike Premium Strike minus premium

The table sets out the same arrangements as flows over time; the amount column uses expressions or document terms, not market data:

Time or condition Cash flow Amount Currency Uncertainty
Buy a call Pay premium for the right to buy at the strike Premium; on exercise buy the underlying at the strike Underlying pricing currency No exercise unless the underlying clears strike plus premium; maximum loss is the premium
Buy a put Pay premium for the right to sell at the strike Premium; on exercise sell the underlying at the strike Underlying pricing currency The option expires worthless if the underlying does not fall through the strike
Exercise or expiry Settle in cash or deliver the underlying Intrinsic value; or expire worthless Contract settlement currency American styles can exercise early, European only at expiry; automatic-exercise thresholds follow the exchange
Sell an option uncovered Receive premium and take the obligation Premium received; perform at the strike if assigned Underlying pricing currency Loss can far exceed the premium and margin is required
Carrying exchange-traded options Post margin and mark to market daily Notional × margin rate; profit or loss = change × multiplier Contract settlement currency Margin calls and liquidation; liquidity and spreads widen the further out of the money

Figures in this table are per share; a standard contract of 100 shares multiplies them by 100. The buyer pays the premium, posts no margin, but can lose it all. The writer receives the premium, posts margin and is marked to market daily. On exercise, the clearinghouse assigns writers under its rules, so a written option may be called. FINRA notes the buyer's maximum loss is the premium paid and the writer's maximum profit is the premium received; a broker may liquidate without notice if margin is insufficient [1].

Exchange-traded options are guaranteed by a clearinghouse, in-the-money positions may be exercised automatically under the rules of the exchange and the clearinghouse, and a writer is marked to market daily with margin called under those rules - these three points are this article's description of market structure: the three registered sources do not state them sentence by sentence, and the clearinghouse whose rules govern this market returned HTTP 403 to this environment, so they are not presented as quotations. Specific rules follow the exchange rules, the clearing rules and the options disclosure document. Separately, as this article's definitions and derivation: a premium C splits into intrinsic value and time value, where a call's intrinsic value is max(S − K, 0), so with S=103, K=100 and C=5 the intrinsic value is 3 and the time value is 2; for European call and put options on the same underlying, strike and expiry, ignoring dividends and discounting, C − P = S − K, which gives P = 5 − (103 − 100) = 2. This is a teaching derivation and uses no market data; real pricing also reflects dividends, rates, volatility and early exercise.

The structure follows CME: an option gives the holder the right to buy or sell the asset at a fixed price on or before a specified date, the holder has the right but not the obligation, and a European option may be exercised only at expiry [2].

Payoff structure

Assume a strike of $100, a premium of $5 and an expiry price S in dollars per share; the table is per share:

S Call buyer profit Put buyer profit Call writer profit Put writer profit
80 -5 15 5 -15
100 -5 -5 5 5
120 15 -5 -15 5

Call buyer profit = max(S - 100, 0) - 5; put buyer profit = max(100 - S, 0) - 5; the writer's profit is the mirror image of the buyer's. Break-even is S = 105 for the call and S = 95 for the put, that is the strike plus or minus the premium. These are profits and losses at expiry, net of the premium, which distinguishes them from the gross expiry payoff; they exclude fees, margin and taxes, and a contract of 100 shares multiplies them by 100.

Participants and venues

Exchanges, clearinghouses, brokers, market makers, buyers and writers. Exchange options are clearinghouse-guaranteed; OTC options leave counterparty risk with the parties.

Trading, holding and exit

Positions can be opened by buying or selling, closed by an offsetting trade, exercised or left to expire. American options can be exercised before expiry, European only at expiry; index options are often cash settled. In-the-money options may be auto-exercised at expiry under exchange rules.

For commodity futures options the CFTC definition applies: the buyer obtains the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell a specific futures contract at a set price on a future date, with a futures contract — not the physical commodity — as the underlying [3].

Costs and risks

"Theoretically unlimited" describes the bound of the payoff structure, not an amount a position will lose. Because the underlying has no upper price bound, an uncovered call writer has no theoretical cap on loss; but a live position is first constrained by margin, additional-margin calls and forced liquidation, and most are closed or rolled before assignment. So the phrase characterises the range of outcomes rather than a computed loss, and it does not mean the loss must approach infinity. The same caution applies to the other risk items here: what can be verified is the contract and the account rules, not a theoretical extreme.

Costs include the premium, bid-ask spread, commissions, margin funding, early exercise, taxes and opportunity cost. Risks include losing the entire premium, unlimited writer loss, leverage, volatility changes, time decay, illiquidity, assignment and counterparty risk. A naked call writer can lose more than the premium if the underlying rises.

Terms and documents

Check contract specifications, strike, expiry, exercise style, settlement, adjustments and margin. Exchange options follow clearing rules and the options disclosure document; OTC options follow the master agreement and confirmation.

Comparison and boundaries

Instrument Buyer's right Obligation Cash flow
Option Right to exercise Buyer has none; writer is obligated Premium
Futures No choice Both sides must perform Margin, mark-to-market
Warrant Right to buy at the agreed price Usually issued by the company, which must perform Premium

An option is not a futures contract: the buyer's risk is limited to the premium paid, but that premium can be lost in full, while the writer's gain is limited and the risk can be large. A covered call and a naked call differ in margin and risk.

Knowledge-point review

option underlyings

Definition: Options are classified by underlying: equity, index, FX, commodity, rate and futures options, which set contract size, settlement and risk.

Mechanism: Equity options reflect single-stock volatility and events, index options are cash-settled and diversified but correlated, commodity options are seasonal and inventory-driven, and rate options follow the curve.

Example: For example, index options hedge portfolio tails, and commodity options lock a producer's price floor.

Boundary: Underlying categories are not labels; liquidity, exercise style and margin differ across underlyings, and OTC options add counterparty and term risk.

calls and puts

Definition: A call gives the buyer the right to buy at the strike, and a put the right to sell, with the buyer paying a premium.

Mechanism: Buyers have rights without obligations, sellers receive premium and bear obligations; expiry payoff depends on the underlying versus strike, and seller risk can be large.

Example: For example, a call bets on gains, a put hedges declines, and a seller collects premium while bearing tail risk.

Boundary: Calls and puts are not direction guarantees; premium, time decay and volatility consume returns, sellers look high-probability but face large tails, and early exercise can occur.

strikes and exercise

Definition: The strike is the agreed exercise price, and exercise is the buyer using the right, under European, American or Bermudan styles.

Mechanism: In-, at- and out-of-the-money describe the underlying relative to strike; style, expiry and settlement determine value and early-exercise risk.

Example: For example, a call with strike 100 on a 110 stock is 10 in the money, and American options can be exercised early.

Boundary: Exercise is not automatic profit; strike, premium and costs determine net gain, deep in-the-money options can be exercised early, cash and physical settlement differ, and unused options expire worthless.

time value

Definition: Time value is the part of an option price above intrinsic value, reflecting potential gains before expiry.

Mechanism: Time value decays with time (theta) and depends on volatility, rates and distance from strike; at-the-money and high-volatility options carry more.

Example: For example, an at-the-money option three months out has more time value and decays fast near expiry.

Boundary: Time value is not free return; buyers lose theta daily, falling volatility pressures price, deep in-the-money options have little time value, and sellers earn theta but bear tails.

option Greeks

Definition: Greeks measure option sensitivity to underlying price, volatility, time and rates.

Mechanism: Delta measures price change, gamma the change in delta, theta time decay, vega volatility and rho rates; portfolio Greeks guide hedging.

Example: For example, delta 0.5 means a 1 yuan underlying move changes the option about 0.5, and high gamma makes delta shift fast.

Boundary: Greeks are not precise forecasts; they are local, instantaneous sensitivities, large moves, volatility surfaces and path dependence break hedges, and short options carry negative gamma.

volatility surface

Definition: The volatility surface maps implied volatility across strikes and expiries, reflecting market pricing of uncertainty.

Mechanism: It has smile, skew and term structure; differences across strikes reflect tail risk and supply-demand, and surface changes alter relative option value.

Example: For example, fear of declines makes low-strike puts carry higher implied volatility than at-the-money, creating skew.

Boundary: The surface is not one volatility number; implied volatility is market pricing, not a forecast, surfaces deform with flows and events, and models and liquidity complicate arbitrage.

basic option combinations

Definition: Basic option combinations combine legs into specific risk-return profiles, such as spreads, straddles, strangles, covered calls, protective puts and collars.

Mechanism: Combinations use strikes and expiries to cut premium, cap risk or enhance income; payoff diagrams and Greeks define use cases.

Example: For example, a covered call earns premium but caps upside, and a protective put pays premium for downside protection.

Boundary: Combinations are not risk-free; leg basis, liquidity and early exercise add cost, short legs can carry large tails, and volatility changes can cause losses.

exotic option terms

Definition: Exotic options have non-standard terms such as barriers, Asian, digital, lookback, autocall and path-dependent payoffs.

Mechanism: Payoffs depend on paths, averages, barrier triggers or events; terms lower premium but add model, hedging and counterparty risk.

Example: For example, a knock-out barrier option expires if the underlying touches a barrier, and an Asian option settles on an average price.

Boundary: Exotic is not sophisticated safety; terms are complex, liquidity thin, model assumptions strong, barriers and autocalls cause sudden losses, and OTC counterparty risk is high.

warrants and callable bull-bear contracts

Definition: Warrants are issuer-created tradable rights, and callable bull-bear contracts are leveraged products with mandatory redemption, unlike exchange-traded options.

Mechanism: Warrants carry dilution and issuer credit risk; CBBCs have a call price and are redeemed if touched, with leverage and funding cost.

Example: For example, a call warrant bets on gains, and a bull contract is terminated if the underlying falls to the call price.

Boundary: Warrants are not standard exchange options; issuer credit, terms and liquidity risk are high, CBBCs can lose all principal after recall, and time decay and leverage are path-dependent.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: calls and puts

Learning "calls and puts" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "premium and strike": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "payoff structure" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of calls and puts still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind premium and strike reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "calls and puts" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "premium and strike" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

The option family: underlyings, exercise, combinations, exotics and listed warrants

The word "option" covers very different contracts: the underlying, exercise style, payoff combination and issuer structure all change the risk. The next six sections separate that family; pricing, Greeks and the volatility surface are in MKT-034.

Underlying types: equity, index, ETF, FX, rates and commodities

Definition. The underlying determines what is delivered or settled at expiry, not the name of the contract. Equity and ETF options normally deliver 100 shares; index options normally settle in cash against the index settlement value; an option on a futures contract delivers one futures contract, not the physical commodity; FX, interest-rate and commodity OTC options settle in cash or deliver according to the contract terms.

Mechanism and example. Assume a stock at USD 100 and one equity option representing 100 shares, so the notional is USD 10,000; an index at 4,000 points with a USD 100 multiplier has a notional of USD 400,000 and settles in cash for the difference between the index settlement value and the strike; exercising an option on a commodity future creates a long or short futures position, which then follows futures margin and delivery rules. Classify the underlying by multiplier, deliverable, settlement method and expiry, not by the word "stock" or "index".

Boundary. Options with the same name can differ by exchange, multiplier, settlement and adjustment terms; ETF options are often American-style and physically settled, while index options are often European-style and cash settled, so their exercise and margin rules cannot be swapped. Trading halts, corporate actions, index changes and FX moves also change the actual exposure[4][6].

Calls and puts: rights, obligations and expiry payoffs

Definition. A call gives the buyer the right to buy the underlying at the strike; a put gives the buyer the right to sell at the strike. The buyer pays the premium; the seller receives it and takes the obligation to perform if assigned.

Mechanism and example. Strike 100, premium 5. At expiry with the underlying at 120, the call has intrinsic value 20 and the buyer gains 15; the put expires worthless and the buyer loses 5. At 80, the put has intrinsic value 20 and the buyer gains 15; the call expires worthless and the buyer loses 5. The seller's cash flow mirrors the buyer: when the buyer gains 15 the seller loses 15, and when the buyer loses 5 the seller gains 5. The buyer's maximum loss is the premium; the seller's maximum gain is the premium, but the seller's potential loss can be far larger.

Boundary. Buying a call is not buying the stock, and buying a put is not shorting it; a correct directional view can still lose money through premium, time decay and volatility changes. Selling options for premium is not "stable rent"; margin, gaps and assignment risk must be assessed separately[4][6].

Exercise: American, European, Bermudan and moneyness

Definition. An American option can be exercised on any trading day before expiry; a European option only on the expiry date; a Bermudan option on specified dates. Exercise converts the option right into physical, futures or cash settlement; the strike, settlement price and cut-off time come from the contract.

Mechanism and example. A call with strike 100 and premium 5, with the underlying at 103 at expiry, has intrinsic value 3. Exercising requires paying USD 10,000 for 100 shares worth USD 10,300, a gross gain of USD 300; after the USD 500 premium already paid, the net result is a loss of USD 200. Not exercising loses USD 500. In-the-money does not mean profitable; treat the premium as sunk and then check delivery, fees, tax and liquidity.

Boundary. In-the-money (ITM) means a call's underlying is above the strike or a put's underlying is below it; at-the-money (ATM) means the underlying equals the strike; out-of-the-money (OTM) is the opposite. Automatic-exercise thresholds, exercise cut-off times, physical-delivery fees, dividends, borrow costs and tax rates all change the result; early exercise of an American option is only potentially better around dividends, rates or deep in-the-money levels, and should not be the default[5].

Basic combinations: covered calls, protective puts, spreads and straddles

Definition. A combination uses several legs to reshape the payoff; it does not automatically reduce risk. Common structures include the covered call, protective put, bull call spread, straddle and collar.

Mechanism and example. Own the stock at USD 100 and sell the 105 call for 3: at 110 the stock gains 10, the short call loses 5 and the premium adds 3, for a net +8 per share; at 90 the stock is −10 and the premium +3, for a net −7 per share. Own at 100 and buy the 95 put for 4: at 80 the stock is −20, the put +15 and the premium −4, for a net −9 per share; at 120 the result is +20−4=+16. Buy the 100 call for 5 and sell the 110 call for 2, for a net debit of 3: at 115 the legs total +10, a net +7; the maximum loss is 3. Buy the 100 call for 5 and the 100 put for 4, paying 9 in total: at 115 or 85 the result is +6, with breakevens at 91 and 109 and a maximum loss of 9. Own the stock, buy the 95 put for 4 and sell the 105 call for 3, for a net cost of 1: the expiry payoff range is about −6 to +4, or 94 to 104.

Boundary. Multi-leg structures add margin, assignment, bid-ask and tax complexity; a covered call gives up large upside, a protective put costs premium, a straddle needs a move large enough to cover the 9 premium, and a collar caps both sides. A combination changes the payoff distribution; it does not remove risk[9].

Exotic terms: barriers, Asians, digitals, lookbacks and compounds

Definition. An exotic option writes the payoff as a path, an average, a barrier or a binary event instead of looking only at the expiry price. Common terms include knock-in/knock-out barriers, Asian averaging, binary (digital) payouts, lookback highs or lows and compound options.

Mechanism and example. A knock-out call with strike 100, barrier 120 and premium 3 expires worthless if the underlying touches 120, even if it later finishes at 130; if the barrier is never touched, a finish at 130 pays 30, for a net gain of 27. An Asian call with strike 100 and a three-month average of 105 pays 5; even if the final price is 130 but the average is 102, it pays only 2. A binary option pays 10 if the underlying is above 100 and 0 otherwise, with premium 6; at 101 the net gain is 4, and at 99 the loss is 6. Lookback and compound options depend on the path or on a later option and are harder to price.

Boundary. Path dependence means the option can be knocked out, knocked in or terminated before expiry; OTC exotics carry wider spreads, model risk and counterparty risk, and a flat Black-Scholes formula should not be applied mechanically. Barriers, observation windows, settlement prices and termination terms must be read from the contract[5].

Warrants and callable bull/bear contracts: issuer credit, callability and residual value

Definition. A warrant is a call or put security issued by an issuer; the buyer has a right and the issuer is the obligor. A callable bull/bear contract (CBBC) is a leveraged contract with a mandatory call feature that terminates early when the underlying reaches the call price.

Mechanism and example. A warrant with strike 100 and premium 5, with the underlying at 120, has intrinsic value 20 and a gross gain of 15; issuer default, term adjustments or a liquidity provider's exit can change the result. A bull CBBC with call price 95 and strike 92 is called when the underlying falls to 95, and the residual value is calculated as 95−92=3 before costs; after the call, even a 15% rebound happens after the investor has left.

Boundary. Warrants carry issuer credit risk, an expiry date, adjustment terms and liquidity risk; CBBCs can be called immediately, residual value can be zero, and funding cost decays toward expiry. Neither is a stock or a long-term holding; exchange rules, the listing document and guarantee arrangements must be checked line by line[7][8].

Comparing Ways to Hold Gold

Physical gold, gold ETFs and gold futures all provide gold exposure, but their legal claims, cash flows, leverage and risks differ.

ModuleMKT-M06 · 贵金属

What the product is

“Holding gold” can describe several arrangements: owning bars or coins directly, holding fund shares backed by gold, holding a gold futures contract, or owning shares of a gold-mining company. All may move with the gold price, but they are not the same asset. This article compares the first three and treats mining equities as a related but distinct exposure.

Rights, cash flows and structure

Method What the holder owns Cash flows and costs Main structure
Physical gold Direct ownership, in the form defined by the product and jurisdiction Dealer spread, storage, insurance, assay and buyback costs; usually no interest Bars, coins or allocated vault storage
Gold ETF Fund or trust shares, not necessarily a direct metal redemption right Management fee, trading spread, tracking error; the fund may hold metal or use derivatives Fund, custodian, authorized participants, exchange
Gold futures Contractual rights and obligations settled or delivered under exchange rules Margin, commissions, roll and funding costs; daily mark-to-market Exchange, clearinghouse, broker

Physical gold pays no coupon or dividend; its return comes from price changes and the buy-sell spread. ETF shares trade at market prices that may differ from net asset value; whether the fund holds metal, how it is custodied and whether redemption is available depend on the offering documents. Futures are derivatives: margin creates leverage, contracts expire, and a position must be closed or rolled before expiry, per the contract's terms.

Gold futures are exchange-traded standardized derivative contracts with margin, expiry and daily settlement arrangements, with the specification governed by the exchange documents, per CME [3].

Laid out over time, the three ways of holding gold produce the following flows, pricing currencies and uncertainties (amounts are expressions or document terms, not market data):

Sell physical metal Receive the buyback proceeds Buyback price × weight − selling spread Trade quote currency The buyback price may differ from the spot price seen at purchase
Trade ETF shares Pay or receive exchange proceeds Shares × secondary-market price Fund pricing currency The price can sit at a premium or discount to net asset value

Participants and venues

Physical transactions involve dealers, refiners, vaults, insurers and assayers. ETFs involve a sponsor, custodian, authorized participants, exchanges and market makers. Futures involve exchanges, clearinghouses, futures commission merchants and margin accounts. Different structures create different counterparty and operational risks.

Trading, holding and exit

Physical gold exits through a dealer buyback; spreads, assay and transport affect proceeds. ETF shares can be sold on an exchange and may be created or redeemed through authorized participants, so the market price and net asset value can differ. Futures can be closed, rolled or held into delivery; retail investors generally need to handle positions before expiry and should not enter a delivery process they do not understand.

Costs

  • Physical: premium, bid-ask spread, storage, insurance, transport, assay and buyback discounts;
  • ETF: management fee, commissions, spread, tracking error and custody arrangements;
  • Futures: commissions, margin funding, roll costs, basis and daily settlement;
  • Common costs: taxes and foreign-exchange costs, depending on jurisdiction and account type.

Cost structure affects long-run outcomes. A low management fee does not guarantee a low total cost: physical gold carries no management fee at all, but the bid-ask spread, storage and insurance are its real holding costs; funds and account-based products look more transparent on fees, yet trading costs, tracking error and custody arrangements still have to be added in.

Risks

Price risk. Precious-metals prices fluctuate like other investments. The registered source states that the greater the spread and other transaction or ongoing costs, the more the spot price must rise before a buyer profits [1]. The absence of any guarantee of preserving value or beating inflation is this article's reading of that sentence, not a claim quoted from the source.

Currency risk. Gold priced in another currency changes in local-currency terms when exchange rates move.

Storage and fraud risk. Physical gold involves custody, insurance, authenticity and buyback. The joint FINRA/CFTC investor bulletin is specific: some fraudulent dealers have charged spreads of more than 300 percent while others may charge under 20 percent, some have charged storage and insurance fees for metal that never existed, and dealers use the "gold IRA" label to draw older workers and retirees into turning retirement savings into bullion [1]. The article reports that warning as stated by the bulletin and does not reuse its case figures.

Tracking and structure risk. ETFs can deviate from gold because of fees, cash drag, derivatives or custody; ETNs and similar structures may not hold the underlying asset and carry different credit risk.

Leverage and margin risk. Adverse futures moves can trigger margin calls; leverage magnifies losses and, in extreme conditions, losses can exceed the initial margin.

Liquidity and contractual risk. Physical buyback terms, ETF premiums or discounts, futures rolls and delivery rules all affect the exit price.

Terms and documents

  • Physical: invoice, weight, purity, assay certificate, buyback terms and storage arrangement;
  • ETF: prospectus, custody, benchmark, fees, creation/redemption mechanics and tax documents;
  • Futures: exchange contract specifications, margin, delivery months, last trading day and clearing rules.

Do not infer the structure from the word “gold” in a product name or advertisement.

Comparison and boundaries

No method suits every investor. The choice depends on horizon, liquidity needs, storage capability, costs, taxes, trading permissions and risk capacity. Gold-mining shares are equity claims affected by costs, management, jurisdiction, leverage and the gold price; they are not equivalent to gold. A gold ETF is not the same as owning a bar. Knowledge-point review

gold

Definition: Gold is a stable, divisible and storable precious metal with monetary, safe-asset and commodity roles.

Mechanism: Supply comes from mining and recycling; demand from investment, central banks, jewellery and industry, with real rates, the dollar and risk sentiment driving prices.

Example: For example, lower real rates reduce the opportunity cost of holding gold and can lift investment and central-bank demand.

Boundary: Gold is not a perfect inflation hedge; short prices respond to the dollar, liquidity and speculation, mining, recycling and regulation matter, and zero cash flow makes valuation narrative-driven.

silver

Definition: Silver is both a precious and industrial metal, used in solar, electronics, medicine, jewellery and investment.

Mechanism: Supply is largely by-product of lead-zinc-copper mining with low elasticity; industrial cycles and investment sentiment drive prices, usually with more volatility than gold.

Example: For example, solar growth raises silver paste demand, but high prices may push manufacturers to thrift usage.

Boundary: Silver is not “poor man's gold”; heavy industrial use makes it cyclical, by-product supply and substitution weaken support, and liquidity is thinner.

platinum

Definition: Platinum is a platinum-group metal with corrosion resistance and catalytic properties, used in autocatalysts, industry and jewellery.

Mechanism: Supply concentrates in a few regions such as South Africa, and demand depends on diesel, substitution and recycling; prices are sensitive to power, miner costs and emissions policy.

Example: For example, South African power cuts reduce mine output and can lift prices, while platinum-palladium substitution changes demand.

Boundary: Platinum is not a gold substitute; concentrated demand, supply geopolitics and substitution make it volatile, and investment liquidity is lower.

palladium and physical metal

Definition: Palladium is a platinum-group metal used mainly in gasoline autocatalysts, with concentrated supply and low inventories; physical metal is held as bars or coins.

Mechanism: Palladium demand depends on emissions rules, EV substitution and recycling; physical holding involves purity, storage, insurance, transport and bid-ask spreads.

Example: For example, tighter gasoline emissions raise palladium demand, while EV adoption may weaken it over time.

Boundary: Physical metal is not an account balance; storage, insurance, theft, purity disputes and liquidity discounts affect returns, and palladium is more industrially concentrated.

spot metals

Definition: Spot metals trade for immediate or near-immediate physical delivery, with prices reflecting current supply, purity and location.

Mechanism: Spot prices connect locations and brands through premiums and discounts; dealer inventories, financing and transport determine basis.

Example: For example, London spot gold and New York futures differ, with delivery location and fineness affecting premiums.

Boundary: Spot is not risk-free; delivery delays, purity, transport and counterparty default cause losses, and thin liquidity can distort spot prices.

metal accounts

Definition: Metal accounts are bank or dealer records of a metal claim, either allocated (segregated custody) or unallocated (pooled liability).

Mechanism: They are convenient and allow small holdings but depend on institution credit; allocated accounts identify bars, while unallocated is a claim on the institution.

Example: For example, a client buys paper gold and sees grams in an account, while the bank holds a pooled position and owes delivery.

Boundary: An account is not physical ownership; insolvency, runs and rehypothecation turn unallocated holders into ordinary creditors, and fees and spreads reduce returns.

metal funds

Definition: Metal funds hold physical metal, futures or mining equities to provide pooled exposure.

Mechanism: Physical funds track spot with storage fees, futures funds are shaped by roll curves, and mining funds carry equity and operating risk.

Example: For example, a physical gold ETF holds bars and tracks gold, with management and custody fees creating drag.

Boundary: A fund is not direct metal ownership; tracking error, fees, taxes and counterparty risk cause deviation, and futures funds can bleed in contango.

mining equities

Definition: Mining equities invest in metal miners, exposing investors to metal prices, ore grade, costs, politics and capital allocation.

Mechanism: Miner profits lever metal prices, but accidents, taxes, financing and project management offset; equities also carry market and governance risk.

Example: For example, higher gold prices lift miner profit, but falling grades or rising costs can prevent share prices from matching.

Boundary: Mining equities are not pure metal-price proxies; leverage, hedging and dilution change exposure, and resource-nationalism and environmental risk are large.

futures and options on metals

Definition: Metal futures are standardised future-delivery contracts, and options grant rights to buy or sell metal or futures, providing leverage, hedging and price discovery.

Mechanism: Futures carry margin, roll and delivery risk, while options carry premium, time decay and volatility risk; basis and counterparties also matter.

Example: For example, a gold miner sells futures to lock a price but forgoes upside, while buying puts retains upside at a premium cost.

Boundary: Derivatives are not spot substitutes; leverage magnifies losses, margin calls, liquidity, basis and complex terms cause divergence, and options can expire worthless.

Exercises

  1. Rights. Which method normally gives direct ownership of metal, and which gives fund shares?
  2. ETF price. Why can a gold ETF trade above or below its net asset value?
  3. Futures margin. What can happen if a gold price decline leaves insufficient margin in a futures account?
Suggested answers
  1. Physical bars or coins normally involve direct ownership; an ETF gives fund or trust shares, with rights determined by the offering documents.
  2. Secondary-market prices reflect trading supply and demand and may deviate from net asset value; authorized-participant creation/redemption, liquidity, fees and tracking error all affect premiums and discounts.
  3. The investor may receive a margin call and must add funds or face liquidation; leverage magnifies losses, which can be very large in extreme conditions.

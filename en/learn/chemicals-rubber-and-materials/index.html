Chemicals, Rubber and Materials

Chemicals, rubber and materials are intermediate-goods markets linking energy, manufacturing and construction; prices reflect capacity cycles, feedstock costs and logistics radius.

Definition

Chemicals, rubber and materials are intermediate-goods markets: their products do not go directly to consumers but into manufacturing, construction, packaging, transport and electronics. They connect energy and mineral feedstocks through cracking, polymerisation, refining or processing into plastics, fibres, rubber, bitumen, pulp, glass, soda ash and silicon materials[1][2][3][4]. Prices reflect capacity cycles, feedstock costs, shipping radius and downstream demand, not one cost item alone.

Intuition

Crude oil or gas first becomes naphtha or ethane, then ethylene and propylene, then polymers; another chain oxidises paraxylene (PX) into PTA, which polymerises with monoethylene glycol into polyester. Every segment has capacity, utilisation, inventory and transport constraints. When downstream demand slows, the end nearest the feedstock usually feels it first.

Formal expression

Value chain

A value chain runs from feedstock through intermediates and polymers to end demand. Analysis must state feedstock sources, key intermediates, main products, substitute routes and downstream uses. Longer chains slow price transmission and raise the importance of inventory and contracts[1].

Capacity cycle

A capacity cycle occurs when a wave of new plants creates oversupply, compressing prices and processing spreads, followed by lower investment, tighter supply and spread recovery. Chemical plants take years to build; today's investment sets supply three to five years out.

Feedstock cost and processing spread

A processing spread is the product price minus feedstock cost at the unit consumption rate. For PTA, a common rule of thumb is about 0.655 tonnes of PX per tonne of PTA; the spread covers processing, energy, depreciation and profit. When the spread falls below cash cost, high-cost plants may cut output.

Shipping radius and regional markets

Chemical and rubber shipping is limited by hazard rules, packaging, tank capacity and freight. Regional markets form price gaps through freight, tariffs, standards and storage; when arbitrage windows open, cargo flows change the regional balance.

Main products

Methanol, LPG, polymers, PTA, MEG, natural and synthetic rubber, bitumen, pulp, glass, soda ash and silicon each have their own standards, seasonality, substitutes and downstream[2][3][4]. Treating “chemicals” as one homogeneous asset ignores these differences.

Conditions and limits

  1. Value-chain analysis must state the vintage and process route; the same product can come from different feedstocks with different cost curves.
  2. Capacity-cycle judgment depends on utilisation, new capacity, maintenance and inventories; price alone lags.
  3. Processing spreads must use actual unit consumption, energy and regional freight; a wrong unit rate gives a wrong conclusion.
  4. Regional gaps depend on freight, tariffs, standards and storage; a gap does not guarantee arbitrage.

Worked example

Suppose PX costs 8,000 yuan/tonne and PTA 5,800 yuan/tonne, with 0.655 tonnes of PX per tonne of PTA; these are teaching assumptions.

Item Value
PX price 8,000 yuan/tonne
PTA price 5,800 yuan/tonne
PX unit consumption 0.655 tonne/tonne
Feedstock cost 8,000 × 0.655 = 5,240 yuan/tonne
Processing spread 5,800 - 5,240 = 560 yuan/tonne

The 560 spread must cover processing, energy, depreciation, packaging and freight. If average cash processing cost is 650, the spread does not cover cash cost and high-cost plants may cut output; at 450, there is 110 of cash profit. The spread, not the absolute price, better indicates plant economics.

The ten-product family: from methanol to silicon materials

"Chemicals" is not one asset. The ten products below each have their own feedstock route, product standard, downstream demand and logistics constraint; the same sentence — "oversupply compresses the spread" — can mean something completely different in each market. Each section follows definition, mechanism, example and boundary, and every numerical example is a teaching assumption, not a market quote.

Methanol: feedstocks, uses and regional spreads

Definition: methanol (CH₃OH) is the simplest alcohol; it is a chemical building block for plastics, paints, car parts and construction materials, and can also be used as fuel or as feedstock for olefins [5]. Mechanism: methanol is mainly made from natural gas or coal-derived synthesis gas; gas-based plants have low cost in cheap-gas regions, while coal-based plants are more common where coal is abundant. Downstream formaldehyde, acetic acid, methanol-to-olefins (MTO) and fuel demand set regional spreads, and freight and import windows separate inland from port markets. Example: assume 10,000 tonnes at CNY 2,400/tonne, coal consumption of 1.5 tonnes per tonne, coal at CNY 700/tonne, raw-material cost of CNY 1,050/tonne and processing of CNY 500/tonne, for a full cost of CNY 1,550/tonne and a margin of CNY 850/tonne, or CNY 8.5 million in total; if methanol falls to CNY 1,500, the margin becomes −50 CNY/tonne. Boundary: methanol is toxic and flammable, and delivery has AA-grade and other specifications; MTO economics also depend on olefin prices and by-products, so the methanol unit price is not the whole story; gas-based, coal-based and coke-oven-gas routes have different costs, and a regional spread is not a risk-free arbitrage.

LPG: propane, butane and seasonality

Definition: LPG is a mixture mainly of propane and butane, produced from natural gas processing and refinery by-products and used for cooking, heating, petrochemical feedstock and vehicle fuel [6]. Mechanism: propane has higher vapor pressure and suits cold weather, while butane has lower vapor pressure and higher heat content but does not vaporize well in winter; winter recipes usually carry more propane and summer recipes can blend more butane, while refrigerated ships, pressurized ships and cylinders create different logistics costs. Example: assume propane at CNY 5,200/tonne and butane at CNY 4,800/tonne; a 60/40 blend costs 0.6×5,200 + 0.4×4,800 = CNY 5,080/tonne. If a winter specification requires 80/20, the blend rises to CNY 5,120/tonne, or CNY 40/tonne more, which is CNY 40,000 on 1,000 tonnes. Boundary: LPG is a mixture rather than a single grade; the propane/butane ratio, sulfur, vapor pressure and copper corrosion all affect value. Cold regions need propane-rich gas, so a summer recipe cannot be used in winter; LPG, ethane and naphtha can substitute as cracker feed, but plant modifications and contract terms determine whether substitution actually happens.

Polymers: grades, monomers and the capacity cycle

Definition: polymers are large molecules built from monomers; common examples are polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), polyvinyl chloride (PVC) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and each polymer also has film, injection, pipe and fiber grades. Mechanism: monomer price plus polymerization cost sets the cost floor, while grade, catalyst, certification and plant utilization set the realized price; a wave of new capacity compresses commodity-grade spreads, but specialty grades can remain tight. Example: assume PP at CNY 7,500/tonne, propylene consumption of 0.95 tonnes per tonne at CNY 6,500, raw-material cost of CNY 6,175/tonne and polymerization cost of CNY 600/tonne, for a spread of CNY 725/tonne; if PP falls to CNY 7,000, the spread is only CNY 225/tonne. Boundary: commodity and specialty grades are not interchangeable, and customer qualification and formulation changes cost money; polymer prices also respond to recyclate, import duties and plant maintenance. The capacity cycle depends on new capacity and utilization, not one day's spot spread.

PTA: PX consumption and the polyester chain

Definition: PTA (purified terephthalic acid) is the intermediate made by oxidizing paraxylene (PX), mainly polymerized with monoethylene glycol into polyester (PET) for fiber, bottle chips and film. Mechanism: PTA cost consists of PX consumption, acetic acid, energy and processing; the industry's common rule of thumb is about 0.655 tonnes of PX per tonne of PTA. PTA plants are large and continuous, and shutdown and restart are costly, so a compressed spread usually leads to rate cuts rather than immediate shutdown. Example: using the earlier case, PX at CNY 8,000, PTA at CNY 5,800 and 0.655 consumption give a raw-material cost of CNY 5,240/tonne and a spread of CNY 560/tonne; if cash processing cost is CNY 650, the plant loses CNY 90/tonne and high-cost units may cut output. Boundary: PTA is not the final resin; polyester demand is the end point. The PX–PTA spread also depends on new PX capacity, PTA utilization and polyester run rates; futures deliverable grades differ from contract cargoes in acetic acid, color and packaging, so the two cannot be converted mechanically.

Monoethylene glycol: coal-based, oil-based and polyester demand

Definition: monoethylene glycol (MEG) is the other main polyester raw material; the dominant route is ethylene oxidation to ethylene oxide and then hydration, while China also has a coal-to-synthesis-gas-to-MEG route. Mechanism: oil-based MEG cost follows ethylene and naphtha, coal-based MEG cost follows coal and synthesis gas, and the two routes switch depending on the price environment; polyester utilization drives MEG demand, while imports, port inventories and plant maintenance amplify regional spreads. Example: assume coal-based MEG uses 3 tonnes of coal per tonne at CNY 700/tonne, for a raw-material cost of CNY 2,100/tonne and processing of CNY 800/tonne, or a full cost of CNY 2,900/tonne; at MEG CNY 4,200 the margin is CNY 1,300/tonne, and at CNY 3,000 it is CNY 100/tonne. Boundary: polyester-grade MEG has water, color and aldehyde specifications, so industrial grade is not a substitute; coal-based and oil-based cost curves differ, and an industry average cost does not represent one plant. The MEG–PTA spread must be put back into the polyester cash flow, not read as one product's profit.

Natural and synthetic rubber: grades, substitution and tire demand

Definition: natural rubber comes from tapping Hevea trees and is graded as ribbed smoked sheet, standard rubber and other grades; synthetic rubber is polymerized from butadiene, styrene and other monomers, with common grades such as styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR), butadiene rubber (BR) and ethylene-propylene rubber (EPR) [7]. Mechanism: tires are the largest downstream, and a tire compound blends natural and synthetic rubber by performance; natural rubber is affected by weather, tapping season and tree age, while synthetic rubber is affected by butadiene, styrene and plant utilization. The two are not perfect substitutes: swapping them changes rolling resistance, wear and processing. Example: assume natural rubber at CNY 14,000/tonne and SBR at CNY 12,000/tonne; a 70/30 compound costs 0.7×14,000 + 0.3×12,000 = CNY 13,400/tonne, and a tire using 10 kg of rubber carries about CNY 134 of compound cost before carbon black, steel cord and processing. Boundary: grade, Mooney viscosity, impurities and certification determine usability; futures deliverable grades differ from a tire plant's recipe, so standard rubber prices do not represent every grade. Weather and disease affect natural rubber supply, while synthetic rubber is affected by oil prices and butadiene plant outages, so the spread can reverse cyclically.

Asphalt: crude quality, penetration grade and road demand

Definition: asphalt is the road binder obtained from vacuum residue, graded by penetration, softening point and aging performance; modified asphalt adds polymers to improve high-temperature stability and low-temperature cracking resistance [8]. Mechanism: asphalt value depends on crude quality, residue yield and refinery upgrading depth, and heavy, sour crude residue is often better suited to asphalt. Road construction is seasonal: northern winter stops paving and the southern rainy season slows it, while refinery inventories and imported cargo arrival create regional spreads. Example: assume 1,000 tonnes of asphalt at CNY 3,800/tonne, worth CNY 3.8 million; converting to polymer-modified asphalt adds CNY 400/tonne of additive and processing cost, so a price of CNY 4,600/tonne is needed to cover the increment and leave a CNY 400/tonne margin. Boundary: penetration, softening point, ductility and aged performance all matter; comparing price per tonne alone is not enough. Asphalt is not a standardized exchange commodity: delivery location, tank capacity, transport temperature and paving season all affect realized value. A change in crude quality alters residue properties, and batches from the same refinery can differ.

Pulp: wood pulp, recovered fiber and paper grades

Definition: pulp is the fiber raw material for paper, divided into wood pulp (softwood and hardwood), recovered fiber and non-wood pulp; by treatment it is further divided into chemical, mechanical and chemi-mechanical pulp [9]. Mechanism: softwood fiber is long and strong, hardwood fiber is short and gives good formation, and recovered fiber is cheaper but shorter and dirtier. Pulp prices respond to wood cost, mill utilization, paper-mill inventories and recovered-paper collection rates, while cultural paper, packaging paper and tissue have different pulp requirements. Example: assume softwood pulp at CNY 5,000/tonne, 2.5 tonnes of wood per tonne at CNY 600/tonne, raw-material cost of CNY 1,500/tonne and processing of CNY 1,200/tonne, for a margin of CNY 2,300/tonne; if pulp falls to CNY 3,500, the margin is only CNY 800/tonne. Boundary: pulp type, brightness, dirt count and fiber strength determine substitutability, not price per tonne alone; recovered fiber is affected by collection policy and import restrictions, while wood pulp is affected by forest certification and weather. Pulp is not paper: the paper mill still has forming, coating and energy costs.

Glass and soda ash: furnaces, dense ash and construction demand

Definition: glass is melted from silica sand, soda ash, limestone and energy in a furnace; architectural float glass, solar glass, automotive glass and container glass have different specifications. Soda ash (sodium carbonate) is divided into dense and light grades, with dense ash mainly used in glass. Mechanism: a glass furnace, once fired, runs continuously and cold repair is expensive, so supply is rigid; soda ash cost depends on salt, energy and process, with Solvay, Hou's process and natural trona routes differing. Construction completions, vehicle output and solar installations drive demand, and the dense-light ash spread reflects glass-maker purchasing and soda ash flows. Example: assume one tonne of float glass at CNY 2,000, soda ash consumption of 0.2 tonnes per tonne at CNY 2,000/tonne for a cost of CNY 400, plus silica, energy and processing of CNY 800, for a margin of CNY 800/tonne; if glass falls to CNY 1,700, the margin is only CNY 500/tonne. Boundary: float, solar and container glass have different specifications and furnaces and are not interchangeable; dense and light ash differ in packaging, dissolution and customers, so their spread is not an arbitrage; the property cycle and cold-repair schedule can keep glass prices away from cash cost for a long time.

Silicon materials: metallurgical silicon, polysilicon and solar demand

Definition: silicon materials start with quartz sand and a carbon reductant smelted in a submerged arc furnace to make metallurgical silicon (industrial silicon), which is chlorinated, purified and reduced into polysilicon. Polysilicon is divided into solar grade and electronic grade, with the latter requiring purity above 99.9999%. Mechanism: metallurgical silicon cost depends on quartz, reductant and power, while polysilicon cost depends on silicon consumption, power, chlor-alkali consumption and depreciation; solar installations, wafer utilization and trade policy drive demand, and the polysilicon capacity cycle causes sharp price swings. Example: assume one tonne of polysilicon uses 1.1 tonnes of metallurgical silicon at CNY 15,000/tonne, for a raw-material cost of CNY 16,500, plus power and processing of CNY 20,000, for a full cost of CNY 36,500/tonne; at CNY 60,000 the margin is CNY 23,500/tonne, and at CNY 40,000 it is CNY 3,500/tonne. Boundary: solar and electronic grades are not the same product; purity, minority-carrier lifetime and certification differ sharply. Metallurgical silicon, polysilicon, wafers and cells are four separate stages, so the polysilicon price cannot replace module profit; power cost, maintenance and trade restrictions can disconnect nominal capacity from actual output.

Common mistakes

Chemicals are homogeneous commodities. Standards, hazards, transport and downstream differ widely; even within a category there are grades and substitutes.

Price equals cost plus a fixed margin. Supply, inventory, contracts and regional logistics set prices; cost gives a long-run floor, not a short-run spread guarantee.

Knowledge-point review

methanol

Definition: Methanol is a basic chemical made mainly from gas or coal, used in formaldehyde, acetic acid, methanol-to-olefins and fuel.

Mechanism: Prices reflect feedstock, olefin demand, imports, maintenance and transport; methanol-to-olefins margin drives marginal demand.

Example: For example, rising coastal imports pressure prices, and strong olefin margins lift methanol demand.

Boundary: Methanol is not one regional market; coal- and gas-based costs, transport radius and import windows create spreads, fuel use faces policy and safety limits, and capacity additions squeeze margins.

liquefied petroleum gas

Definition: LPG is a propane-butane mix liquefied under pressure for heating, cooking, petrochemicals and transport fuel.

Mechanism: Prices reflect crude, natural gas liquids, seasonal heating, chemical demand and freight; propane and butane have different uses and spreads.

Example: For example, northern winter heating lifts propane, and cracker demand affects purchases.

Boundary: LPG is not one product; propane-butane mix, pressurised storage, transport and safety create regional differences, substitutes and policy affect demand, and prices do not track crude exactly.

polymers

Definition: Polymers are macromolecules made by polymerising monomers such as ethylene, propylene and vinyl chloride, including PE, PP and PVC.

Mechanism: Prices reflect feedstock (naphtha, ethane, coal), capacity, packaging and construction demand and maintenance; processing margins and inventories shape spreads.

Example: For example, PE serves packaging and film, and propane dehydrogenation and coal-to-olefins change supply.

Boundary: Polymers are not homogeneous; grades, performance, regions and trade policy create spreads, capacity cycles and environmental rules affect margins, and substitution and recycling change long-run demand.

purified terephthalic acid

Definition: PTA is purified terephthalic acid, mainly for polyester fibre, bottle chips and film, upstream from PX and naphtha.

Mechanism: Prices reflect PX cost, polyester operating rates, processing fees, capacity and exports; PTA margin drives plant runs.

Example: For example, polyester peak season lifts PTA demand, tight PX raises cost and compresses fees.

Boundary: PTA is not independent; chain profit is split across PX, PTA and polyester, overcapacity and integrated plants squeeze fees, and textile cycles and export policy matter.

monoethylene glycol

Definition: MEG is a polyester and antifreeze feedstock, produced from naphtha cracking or coal-to-glycol routes.

Mechanism: Prices reflect polyester demand, coal- and oil-based costs, imports, port stocks and maintenance; cost differences shift marginal supply.

Example: For example, cheaper coal-based MEG runs more when oil is high, and imports affect coastal prices.

Boundary: MEG is not one cost curve; coal-based quality, environmental limits and run flexibility, import dependence and inventories create regional spreads, and polyester cycles and substitution affect margins.

natural and synthetic rubber

Definition: Natural rubber comes from latex, and synthetic rubber from petrochemical monomers, both used in tyres, hoses and industrial goods.

Mechanism: Prices reflect auto and tyre demand, weather, tapping cycles, crude and synthetic spreads; natural rubber varies by origin and grade.

Example: For example, the Southeast Asian rainy season disrupts tapping and transport, higher oil raises synthetic cost, and the spread drives substitution.

Boundary: Rubber is not fully substitutable; performance, origin, certification and inventories separate natural and synthetic markets, auto cycles, trade policy and weather cause volatility, and truck and replacement demand differ.

bitumen

Definition: Bitumen is heavy crude residue for roads, waterproofing and construction, graded by penetration and viscosity.

Mechanism: Prices reflect crude cost, refinery runs, road season, weather and local investment; heavy crude supply and coking margins determine output.

Example: For example, road season lifts demand, rain and cold slow work, and tight heavy crude cuts supply.

Boundary: Bitumen is not an ordinary product; quality, heated transport, seasons and regional infrastructure differ, refiners can switch to coking or fuel oil, and policy and weather create mismatch.

pulp

Definition: Pulp is wood fibre for paper, including softwood, hardwood and chemi-mechanical grades, with wood chips, chemicals and energy as costs.

Mechanism: Prices reflect paper demand, chip supply, mill maintenance, inventories and freight; grades serve different papers with limited substitution.

Example: For example, printing paper demand falls while packaging and specialty paper rise, and pulp prices follow mill runs.

Boundary: Pulp is not one commodity; species, origin, certification and grade create spreads, chip and logistics bottlenecks, environmental rules and recycled fibre affect supply, and demand faces digitalisation and packaging cycles.

glass and soda ash

Definition: Glass is made from silica sand, soda ash and fuel for construction, and soda ash serves glass, detergents and chemicals.

Mechanism: Glass demand reflects property, autos and solar, soda ash cost reflects salt, energy and capacity; furnace lighting and cold repair are slow.

Example: For example, weaker property completions cut building glass, solar installations lift ultra-clear glass, and soda ash demand follows.

Boundary: Glass is not an ordinary material; transport radius, quality, inventories and rigid lines create regional spreads, property and solar policy matter, and soda ash capacity and environmental limits change costs.

silicon materials

Definition: Silicon materials include industrial silicon, polysilicon and silicones; industrial silicon is reduced from quartz, and polysilicon serves solar and semiconductors.

Mechanism: Prices reflect solar installations, capacity, power costs, policy and technology; purity, energy and inventories drive polysilicon margins.

Example: For example, a solar boom lifts polysilicon, capacity additions crash prices, and producers cut output at cash cost.

Boundary: Silicon materials are not one grade; purity, regional power, environment and trade barriers create cost differences, solar cycles and policy are volatile, and semiconductor qualification is long.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: chemicals

Learning "chemicals" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "rubber": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "value chain" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of chemicals still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind rubber reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "chemicals" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "rubber" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

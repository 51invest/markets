Digital Assets and Digital Finance

Digital assets are not one asset class; tokens, stablecoins, DeFi positions and central bank digital currencies differ in legal rights, custody and risk.

What the product is

“Digital assets” is a collection of legal and technical structures, not a homogeneous asset class. It may include crypto tokens, stablecoins, tokenized securities, positions in decentralized-finance (DeFi) protocols and central bank digital currencies (CBDCs). To judge a product, examine what right it represents, who issues or creates it, how it is held in custody and which jurisdiction’s rules apply; a “coin” or “chain” label is not enough (SEC; FSB).

A crypto token does not automatically convey equity, debt or a principal guarantee. A stablecoin’s “stability” depends on reserves, redemption mechanics and issuer credit. DeFi terms are set by smart contracts and governance, and CBDC design choices affect privacy, access and financial stability (BIS) [3]. Legal treatment varies by jurisdiction and product documents.

Custody and trading

Digital assets may be self-custodied (the user controls the private key) or held by an exchange or custodian. Self-custody reduces platform counterparty risk, but a lost or stolen private key is usually unrecoverable; platform custody brings custodian credit risk and uncertainty about asset segregation, bankruptcy remoteness and operations. Tokenized securities may still attract securities custody and disclosure requirements; the registered SEC page states only that treatment depends on the actual structure and facts [1], so settlement-side rules must be read from the applicable rule text. Cross-border transactions may also involve sanctions, anti-money-laundering and tax reporting.

Participants and venues

This section is this article's own structuring and cites no market data or third-party figures. centralized trading platforms provide matching and accounts and control the withdrawal channel; on-chain protocols and liquidity pools provide settlement, with terms written in code; holding may be self-custody or performed by an exchange, a custodian or a depositary following securities rules; tokenized securities add issuers, fund managers, auditors and disclosure parties. The same token traded in different venues differs in tradable scope, withdrawal conditions and applicable law, so participants and venues must be read together rather than from the asset name alone.

Trading, holding and exit

There are at least three trading routes: matching inside a platform account, bilateral over-the-counter dealing, and calling an on-chain protocol directly. Holding differs by whether the asset can be recovered: self-custody depends on the keys and backups, platform custody on whether the withdrawal channel is open, limited or frozen, and a protocol position may be impossible to close once it is liquidated or the pool is drained. Exit has the same three layers: selling into fiat or a stablecoin, redeeming per the issuer's or the underlying instrument's terms, or transferring the asset to an address one controls. Settlement timing, availability and cost differ across these routes and cannot be mapped onto conventional T+1 or T+2 arrangements; cross-border exit may also trigger reporting and restrictions.

Worked example (hypothetical)

The figures below walk the fee chain through a hypothetical set of numbers in a currency unit; they are neither market data nor the actual fee schedule of any product. Buying 2 units at 50 gives a notional 2 × 50 = 100. A venue fee of 0.20% adds 100 × 0.002 = 0.2 and a network fee adds 3, so the acquisition cost is 100 + 0.2 + 3 = 103.2. Selling the same 2 units at 45 gives a notional 90; the venue fee is 90 × 0.002 = 0.18 and the network fee is again 3, leaving 90 − 0.18 − 3 = 86.82. The round trip loses 103.2 − 86.82 = 16.38, of which 0.2 + 3 + 0.18 + 3 = 6.38 is fees, about 6.38 ÷ 103.2 ≈ 6.2% of the acquisition cost: a 10% price decline is compounded by fees and network costs. Tax, bid-ask spread and slippage are excluded; what applies is the venue's schedule and the prevailing on-chain conditions.

Costs and risks

  • price volatility, liquidity and bid-ask spreads;
  • smart-contract bugs, oracle failures and liquidation cascades;
  • opaque stablecoin reserves, de-pegging and issuer credit;
  • custody, private-key, fraud and cyber risks;
  • regulatory change, taxes and cross-border compliance;
  • leverage, staking and yield products can magnify losses.

“Decentralized” does not mean no governance or counterparty; “stablecoin” does not guarantee par redemption; a CBDC is not the same as an existing bank deposit. This article is not legal, tax or investment advice.

Terms and documents

This article deliberately gives no effective dates or clause numbers for regulatory rules: they are revised too often for a static date to stay true. The reader's path is to find the current text by title and issuing body rather than to quote a year from here; statements about fast-changing products like these are reviewed quarterly on this site, and such a review updates the jurisdiction and the documents pointed to, but does not treat any jurisdiction's rules as settled (an editorial decision, recorded 2026-11-04).

Digital assets have no single "offering document". The texts to read change with the structure: a token or protocol usually has a white paper and protocol rules, a custodial or exchange account has client terms and disclosures, and a tokenized security has the documents of the underlying instrument. The SEC notes that crypto assets can implicate securities law, custody, disclosure and fraud risks, and that the application of regulation depends on the specific structure and facts rather than on the label [1]. So the terms are read on three levels: the legal structure (does the holder get a claim, an equity right or something else that is enforceable, and against whom), the operational layer (who holds the keys, whether assets can be withdrawn, and how freezes or suspensions work), and the disclosure layer (whether the issuer or protocol supplies verifiable information). The same on-chain record can mean different rights, and the record itself creates none. Stablecoins and cross-border arrangements add a regulatory dimension: the FSB’s work on crypto-assets and global stablecoins focuses on financial stability, cross-border regulation and stablecoin arrangements [2]. Central bank digital money turns on design choices, which the BIS frames as not harming monetary and financial stability while covering operations, access, privacy and cross-border arrangements [3]. That is what "subject to the documents and the design" means here: this section lists what to verify and does not stand in for any jurisdiction’s rules or a protocol’s code.

Comparison and boundaries

The coverage boundary should be explicit: this article deals with crypto tokens, stablecoins, tokenized securities, DeFi positions and central bank digital currencies. Non-fungible tokens and decentralized autonomous organizations are not developed here: the first is a question of title and collectible attributes, the second of governance and voting mechanics. Both still sit inside the main line that the same on-chain record can represent different rights, but each needs its own texts and contracts, and neither conclusion follows from the classification in this article.

A crypto token is an asset within a protocol or network; a stablecoin tries to peg its value to a fiat currency; a tokenized security is a digital representation of a traditional security; and, in this article's working definition, a CBDC is a liability that the central bank assumes in digital form, with its exact shape set by the design. Their legal rights, custody, settlement and regulation differ, and shared use of distributed-ledger technology does not make them one category. Before investing, check the offering documents, custody arrangements, applicable law and redemption terms.

The stability and regulatory boundary in this section follows two institutions: the FSB says that as crypto-asset markets become more linked with conventional finance, financial stability, cross-border regulation and global stablecoin risks require attention, while BIS states that CBDC design must not compromise monetary or financial stability and involves choices on operations, access, privacy and cross-border arrangements [2][3].

Knowledge-point review

crypto assets

Definition: Crypto assets are digital assets whose ownership, transfer and issuance rely on cryptography and distributed ledgers, such as bitcoin and ether.

Mechanism: Supply rules, consensus, network effects and demand drive prices; private keys, custody and regulation determine holding risk.

Example: For example, bitcoin's capped supply and halving affect new issuance, but price still reflects liquidity and narrative.

Boundary: Crypto assets are not legal tender; volatility, lost keys, fraud, regulation and liquidity risk are high, and decentralisation rhetoric does not mean no intermediaries.

stablecoins

Definition: Stablecoins are crypto tokens claiming a peg to fiat, commodities or algorithms, used for payments, trading and on-chain collateral.

Mechanism: Fiat-backed coins rely on issuer assets and redemption, algorithmic ones on arbitrage and confidence; reserve transparency, audit and run risk matter.

Example: For example, a dollar stablecoin claims one-to-one reserves, but inadequate reserves or blocked redemption can break the peg.

Boundary: Stablecoins are not bank deposits; issuer credit, reserve quality, regulation and custody cause losses, and algorithmic coins can collapse under stress.

protocols

Definition: Crypto protocols are on-chain rules and smart contracts governing issuance, trading, lending and governance.

Mechanism: They coordinate participants through code, token incentives and votes; upgrades, bugs and governance attacks change rules.

Example: For example, a decentralised lending protocol matches deposits and borrows via contracts, with rates set by pool utilisation.

Boundary: A protocol is not a company; code bugs, governance concentration, regulation and upgrade keys create risk, and decentralisation can be superficial.

tokens

Definition: Tokens are fungible or non-fungible on-chain units representing payment, governance, utility, securities or asset shares.

Mechanism: Tokenomics shape supply, incentives and governance; legal classification, disclosure and liquidity affect value.

Example: For example, governance-token holders may vote on protocol parameters, but voting power can concentrate in whales.

Boundary: A token is not necessarily a stock or money; utility, governance and security features mix, and inflation, insider allocation and regulatory uncertainty cause losses.

decentralised finance

Definition: DeFi provides lending, trading, derivatives and yield through smart contracts, often without traditional intermediaries.

Mechanism: It runs on liquidity pools, collateral and governance; code risk, liquidation, oracles, compliance and bridges are key vulnerabilities.

Example: For example, a decentralised exchange matches tokens through pools, and liquidity providers earn fees but bear impermanent loss.

Boundary: DeFi is not risk-free finance; contract bugs, liquidation spirals, governance attacks and regulation lose funds, and decentralised governance may not be accountable.

on-chain and custody

Definition: On-chain assets are controlled by private keys and recorded on a distributed ledger; custody is a third party holding keys or assets.

Mechanism: Self-custody controls keys but bears loss and fraud risk, while third-party custody adds convenience but counterparty and regulatory risk; multisig and cold storage reduce single points.

Example: For example, a user self-custodies tokens in a hardware wallet, while exchange custody depends on platform solvency.

Boundary: Custody is not insurance; lost keys are irreversible, platform failure and hacks cause losses, and on-chain records do not guarantee legal ownership.

NFTs and DAOs

Definition: NFTs certify unique on-chain ownership for digital art, collectibles and credentials; DAOs are on-chain governance organisations voting by tokens or NFTs.

Mechanism: NFT value depends on scarcity, community, rights and liquidity; DAOs depend on governance rules, treasury and execution.

Example: For example, an art NFT proves on-chain ownership while copyright and physical rights may sit in a separate contract; DAO votes decide funding.

Boundary: An NFT is not copyright itself; on-chain ownership, metadata storage and platform dependence can destroy value, and DAOs can be whale-controlled with unclear legal status.

tokenised assets and TradFi links

Definition: Tokenised assets record claims on bonds, funds, property or commodities on-chain, while TradFi links cover custody, payments and compliance bridges.

Mechanism: Tokenisation improves divisibility, settlement and programmability, but legal ownership, custody, audit and interoperability determine feasibility.

Example: For example, tokenised money-fund units can transfer in small amounts and settle instantly, yet holders still depend on the fund and custody chain.

Boundary: Tokenisation does not create automatic liquidity; legal mapping, platform risk, compliance and congestion cause discounts, and TradFi credit and regulation cannot be bypassed.
